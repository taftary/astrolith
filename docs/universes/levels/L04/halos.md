# Halos — the galaxy beyond the galaxy

Loop doc for Issue #221 (iteration 5). A galaxy does not end where its
glow fades. Around every disk and bulge floats a vast dim halo: ancient
stars, ~150 globular clusters, shredded dwarf companions stretched into
streams, and a reservoir of gas waiting for future star birth. Our
halo runs ~500,000 light-years across — five times the disk. How a halo
interacts with the other L4 parts — dwarfs it is built from, spirals it
wraps, nuclei that heat its gas, ellipticals it leaks into — runs
through every section below.

## How it looks

Almost nothing — and that is the point. The stellar halo is a sparse
spherical cloud of old stars, readable only by counting: 11.5-billion-
year-old suns, stellar archaeologists' tracers of the galaxy's youth.
Inside it hang the globulars: huge ancient star-cities, Omega Centauri
holding up to 10 million stars in a ball half a degree across — a full
Moon of packed starlight, 17,000 light-years away. And the streams: the
Sagittarius dwarf's stars strung along its orbit in a ribbon wrapping
us, the Magellanic gas ribbon looping halfway around the sky.

## How it changes over time

Halos are built, not born: dwarf after dwarf falls in, tides strip it,
and its stars join the halo while its gas rains toward the disk. Each
stream is a merger fossil with a readable age — the Sagittarius stream
records billions of years of ongoing disruption. Globulars date the
assembly: their ages cluster around the galaxy's great building epochs.
The gas halo breathes in the same rhythm, fed by infall and fountains
from the disk, drained by star birth and nuclear winds.

Sources: Wikipedia "Sagittarius Stream" (accretion history); Wikipedia
"Sagittarius Dwarf Spheroidal" (ongoing disruption); NASA halo-gas
explainers.

## How it is created

Every halo is a graveyard of eaten dwarfs. Small companions spiral in
through dynamical friction, tides peel them shell by shell, and the
debris spreads along the orbit into streams that fade over billions of
years into a smooth halo. The biggest building event we know is
Gaia-Enceladus: a 50-billion-solar-mass dwarf that merged 8–11 billion
years ago, puffed our thin disk into the thick disk, and still
dominates the metal-rich halo (full story in `lifetime.md`).

Sources: ESA Gaia ghosts release (Enceladus merger); Wikipedia "Gaia
Sausage".

## How it interacts with the others

- **With dwarfs:** dwarfs are halo raw material — today's Sagittarius
  stream is tomorrow's smooth halo (see `dwarfs.md`).
- **With spirals:** the halo's gas reservoir feeds the disk's future
  star birth; three Sagittarius disk punches triggered ours (see
  `spirals.md`).
- **With nuclei:** jets and winds heat halo gas and can shut off the
  infall, starving the disk (see `nuclei.md`).
- **With ellipticals:** at cluster centers the halo has no edge — its
  outer stars drift off as intracluster light (see `ellipticals.md`;
  L03 `icm.md`).

Sources: ESA Sagittarius collisions release; NASA SVS Markarian 573;
A&A brightest-cluster-galaxy sample.

## Size, mass, example objects

Stellar halos reach hundreds of thousands of light-years; the Milky
Way's runs ~500,000 across against a disk D25 of ~87,000. Globular
systems number in the hundreds for big galaxies.

Example objects:

- **Sagittarius Stream** — tidally stripped stars of the Sagittarius
  dwarf, wrapping the Milky Way, mapped in position, velocity and
  chemistry. Source: Wikipedia "Sagittarius Stream".
- **Omega Centauri** — 10 million stars, 17,000 light-years away,
  biggest globular, likely a stripped dwarf core. Source: ESO eso0844a.
- **Magellanic Stream** — giant gas ribbon from the Magellanic system,
  future fuel for our disk. Source: NASA Magellanic Stream page.
