# Blocks — apartment and office towers, slabs, curtain walls

Part doc for Issue #230 (Round 2 loop). One of six L13 parts: apartment and office blocks at 10–100 m.

## How it looks

A block is a vertical stack of identical floors on a steel or concrete skeleton. The outside
wall usually carries no weight — it hangs like a curtain — so it can be mostly glass in a
regular grid of windows. Two shapes dominate: the point tower, a compact square footprint
rising straight up in the downtown core, and the slab, a long rectangular plate often twinned
at right angles to leave open ground, like the twin 26-story slabs of 860–880 Lake Shore
Drive joined only by canopy and plaza. Offices read as full glass curtains with deep uniform
floors, often bronze or grey tint; homes read as smaller punched windows with brick panels,
balconies, and curtains. Older offices step back in Art Deco setbacks with a spire on top.

## How it changes over time

Blocks age through their skin and systems: concrete spalls, steel corrodes, single-pane
curtains leak, elevators and HVAC date. The answer is often recladding — new low-E glass on
the old frame, as at 1271 Avenue of the Americas, which gained half again as much vision
area while keeping its proportions — or deep energy retrofit, as at the Empire State's 2009
$106M overhaul that rebuilt all 6,514 windows and won LEED Gold. Whole towers change purpose:
vacant offices become apartments, the largest Manhattan conversion so far yielding 1,320
rentals from 1.1M sq ft of office, though deep glass plates convert worse than narrow historic
ones. At the end of life a tower is renovated, demolished, or never completed.

Sources: Pei Cobb Freed recladding pages; Skyscraper Center Empire State pages; Center for American Progress conversion pages.

## How it is created

Two frame families carry every tower: all-steel skeletons, and all-concrete cores and slabs,
with composite mixes of both. The lineage starts at Chicago's 1885 Home Insurance Building,
a ten-story near-all-metal frame raised on expensive land. The sequence repeats floor by
floor: clear the site, pile the foundation, raise core and columns, pour each slab, hang the
curtain wall, fit elevators and services — topping out the frame first, cladding and spire
after, opening only when fully dressed. Speed is the legend: the 102-story Empire State rose
in 410 days, January 1930 to May 1931; today a high-rise still takes two to three years, with
a concrete pour cycle near five days a floor.

Sources: Britannica steel-frame pages; Skyscraper Center criteria pages; History.com Empire construction pages.

## How it interacts with the others

Towers meet the street through a podium: two or three tall ground floors of shops and lobbies
— about 15 ft floor-to-floor against 10 ft for homes above — with continuous glass, frequent
doors, canopies, and light at human scale. Mies set the 860–880 lobbies back behind 17-ft
glass walls to form an arcade rather than a wall of slabs. Above, the uses stack: retail at
grade, offices mid-tower on 30,000 sq ft plates, homes on 18,000; a building counts
mixed-use when each use holds roughly 15 percent or more. Sixty percent of a block's life
happens at its base, where cafes, lobbies, and transit stops trade with the sidewalk.

Sources: Portland height/FAR memo pages; SPUR ground-level pages; Chicago landmarks pages.

## Typical size and mass

Tall starts at 14 stories or 50 m; supertall at 300 m, of which 173 stand worldwide, and
megatall at 600 m, of which three. Homes stack 2.7–3.0 m floor-to-floor, offices 3.3–4.2 m,
ground retail about 4.6 m. The Empire State carries 102 floors to 381 m architectural
(443 m to tip) with 208,879 m2 of floor and 73 elevators running 7.1 m/s at top speed; the
Lake Shore slabs rise 26 stories to 82 m on 21-ft bays.

Sources: Skyscraper Center criteria and Empire State pages; Portland height/FAR memo pages.

## Example objects

- Empire State Building, New York — 1931, 102 stories, 381 m, all-steel Art Deco over limestone, 6,500 windows, tallest in the world 1931–72.
- 860–880 Lake Shore Drive, Chicago — 1949–51 Mies van der Rohe twin residential slabs, 26 stories, 82 m, the glass-steel prototype for the postwar tower.
- Willis (Sears) Tower, Chicago — 1973–74, 110 stories, 442 m bundled-tube steel in black aluminum and bronze glass, 4.5M sq ft of office.

Sources: Skyscraper Center Empire State and Willis pages; Chicago landmarks and Structurae 860–880 pages.
