## Shelters before houses — caves, huts, thatch (before ~10000 BCE)

The first buildings are found, not made: caves and rock overhangs, then huts of branches and
thatch — dried grasses and reeds tied in overlapping courses. Walls are branches, timbers,
then brick and stone as the materials allow; roofs run gabled, flat, domed, and vaulted from
the start. Nothing is dated to a year; the techniques persist into every later era as the
vernacular under the monuments.

Sources: Britannica construction and roof pages.

## First permanent houses (~10000–3000 BCE)

Farming fixes people in place and the house becomes permanent: mudbrick rectangles in the
first towns, rooms added as families grow, entry over the roof and down a ladder in the
earliest dense settlements. The house, the storehouse, and the shrine separate into distinct
buildings — the first civic and commercial parts. Walls divide into the two families that
persist today: load-bearing walls that carry floors and roofs, and nonbearing walls that
merely divide.

Sources: Britannica house and architecture-methods pages.

## Ancient masonry — temples, houses, first blocks (~3000 BCE–500 CE)

Stone and fired brick raise the first monuments and the first cities: courtyard houses,
palaces, temples, and Rome's stacked rental insulae — the first apartment blocks, homes
piled floor on floor over shops. Aqueducts, sewers, and paved streets wire the buildings
together into the first serviced city. The civic center appears as a building type: forum,
basilica, baths, and the meeting hall.

Sources: Britannica house and construction pages; HUD systems-history pages.

## Medieval timber and stone (~500–1500)

Timber framing fills the towns — oak skeletons with wattle-and-daub panels — beside stone
churches, guildhalls, and walls. The great halls stretch past 150 ft in stone that survives
fire and bombing; market halls double as government, like Faneuil Hall's 1742 ancestors.
Houses stay one or two rooms for most, with the chimney stack arriving to carry smoke above
the roof. Cathedrals push masonry to its limit while homes stay timber.

Sources: Britannica architecture and Guildhall pages; NPS Faneuil Hall pages.

## Industrial brick and steel (1760–1914)

Mills and factories multiply on coal, iron, and the rail siding; warehouses adopt the dock,
the leveller, and the pallet. Steel framing frees the wall from carrying weight: Chicago's
1885 Home Insurance Building stacks ten stories on a near-all-metal frame, Cincinnati pours
the 16-story Ingalls in concrete in 1903, and the first all-glass faces appear — Kansas
City's Boley in 1908, San Francisco's Hallidie in 1918. Houses industrialize too: balloon
framing, manufactured ornament, Victorian rows, and the first suburbs on the streetcar.
Biltmore rises 1889–95, the largest private house in America, while tenements stack the
workers.

Sources: Britannica steel-frame pages; Skyscraper Center criteria pages; Biltmore history pages.

## Modern concrete and glass (1918–2000)

The curtain wall wins: Equitable Portland 1948 seals the first fully air-conditioned tower,
Mies raises the Lake Shore glass slabs 1949–51, the Empire State retrofits 6,514 windows
toward LEED Gold. Commerce invents its own buildings: Southdale 1956 encloses the first
fully air-conditioned mall, big boxes colonize the highways. Schools and hospitals accrete
into campuses; Boston pours its concrete City Hall in 1968. Crowds get roofs: the Astrodome
1963–65 domes 70,000 seats under a record 642-ft truss, Maracana holds 200,000 standing in
1950, Michigan fills 107,601 from 1927. Suburbs peak in floor area — 2,466 sq ft median in
2015 — on lots shrinking toward a fifth of an acre.

Sources: Skyscraper Center Empire pages; Chicago landmarks pages; StadiumDB and Library of Congress stadium pages; Census and NAHB housing pages.

## Present — you are here (2026)

Ninety-four percent of new US single-family homes are wood-framed, raised in 9.1 months
permit to keys, at a median 2,142 sq ft. Towers are composite steel and concrete, reclad as
often as raised; offices convert to apartments where the plates are narrow. Malls convert to
data centers, clinics, and senior housing; power stations convert to galleries and
neighborhoods — Bankside to Tate Modern in 2000, Battersea in 2022. Stadiums rebuild on
decade beats: Houston's Reliant opens 2002 with the first retractable NFL roof, Wembley's arch
since 2007, Brasilia rebuilt 2010–13, the 1965 dome awaiting
its second life since the teams left. Supertalls pass 300 m, 173 and counting.

Sources: Census new-housing highlights; NAHB framing pages; EIA warehouse pages; Tate and Battersea pages.

## Far-future fate — growth, climate, abandonment

Three trajectories share the century. Growth: towers add floors, suburbs infill, warehouses
swell past 500,000 sq ft, stadiums rebuild again. Climate: roofs whiten and open, cooling
loads rise, flood lines redraw the ground floor, and the 9-percent energy share of hospitals
forces the retrofit first. Abandonment: the Astrodome's proposals are the template — mothball,
repurpose, or demolish — and every dead mall and dark factory rehearses the choice. The
buildings that survive will be the adaptable ones: high bays, clear spans, narrow plates,
and stone meant to outlast its builders.

Sources: EIA energy pages; Tate and Battersea reuse pages; NASA Reliant caption pages.

## Cross-check of aligned timescales

- Houses: thatch/huts (undated) → mudbrick towns (~10000 BCE) → insulae/courtyards (ancient) → timber framing (medieval) → balloon frame/Victorian (industrial) → Ranch/suburb (1950s–2015) → wood-frame present (94%, 9.1 mo, 2,142 sq ft).
- Blocks: insulae (ancient) → steel frame 1885 → concrete 1903 → glass curtain 1908–51 → reclad/retrofit/convert (present).
- Commerce: market hall 1742 → department store 1852 → open mall 1922 → enclosed 1956 → big box/power center → dead-mall reuse (present).
- Civic: forum/basilica (ancient) → guildhall (medieval) → municipal precinct (1869–1968) → campus accretion (present).
- Works: mill/forge (industrial) → Rouge 1915–28 → PEMB/tilt-up (present) → station-to-gallery reuse 2000/2022.
- Bowls: Maracana 1950 → Astrodome 1965 → Big House 1927 onward → Wembley 2007 → Brasilia 2013 → next rebuild (future).
