# Scattered disc — the thrown and the detached

Loop doc for Issue #224.

## How it looks

Beyond Neptune, past the flat Kuiper doughnut (see `kuiper.md`), the orbits get wild. The scattered disc is a thin, tilted spray of icy bodies on long, stretched loops: they swing in near Neptune at about 30–40 AU and fly far out past 50–100 AU before falling back. The detached objects sit even farther out and never come back in: their closest point stays well beyond Neptune, so Neptune cannot touch them now. The group named for Sedna — the sednoids — is the prototype of this detached class.

## How it changes over time

Scattered bodies keep moving. Each pass near Neptune can tug the orbit a little wider, narrower, or more tilted, so the spray slowly leaks: some fall inward to become Centaurs and then short-period comets (see `comets.md`), some are thrown outward, some survive billions of years on stable tilted loops. Detached bodies like Sedna barely change at all — one lap takes about 11,400 years, so a human lifetime sees only a short arc. Eris laps every 557 years and is currently near its far point, slowly falling back inward.

Sources: NASA Eris page (557-year period); NASA Planet X page (`https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planet-x/`); Caltech Brown Sedna page.

## How it is created

Both groups started in the giant-planets zone (see `giants.md`). When Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune moved early on, their gravity threw leftover ice chunks outward. Most captured into the Kuiper Belt (see `kuiper.md`); the ones Neptune kept kicking became the scattered disc — still tied to Neptune by gravity. The detached ones needed one more push: a passing star, the crowded birth cluster, or a far unseen planet lifting their closest point out of Neptune's reach. Once lifted, the orbit freezes in place. That extra push is why Sedna's closest point sits at 76 AU, far beyond anything Neptune can reach.

Sources: NASA Oort cloud facts (`https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/oort-cloud/facts/`); NASA Kuiper Belt facts (`https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/kuiper-belt/facts/`).

## How it interacts with the others

The scattered disc is the middle link. Inside sit the giant planets (see `giants.md`), whose gravity — mainly Neptune's — still steers it. Beside it sits the Kuiper Belt (see `kuiper.md`), the calmer cousin it is often confused with. Outside sits the Oort cloud (see `oort.md`), the far sphere it does not reach. Falling inward runs the comet traffic (see `comets.md`): scattered bodies become Centaurs crossing between the giants, then short-period comets near the Sun. The debated Planet Nine would live in this gap region and, if real, would be the shepherd holding the most distant orbits lined up.

Sources: NASA Planet X page (`https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planet-x/`); NASA Eris and dwarf-planets pages.

## Size and mass

The bodies run from kilometer-scale comet nuclei up to dwarf planets. Eris, the largest known here, is about 2,326 km across — about the same size as Pluto but more massive than Pluto — with one known moon, Dysnomia, whose 16-day loop lets astronomers weigh the pair. Sedna is much smaller, roughly 900–1,000 km across, with no known moon, so its mass is not directly measured. The debated Planet Nine, if it exists, would be far bigger: about 5–10 Earth masses on a 10,000–20,000-year loop far beyond everything named here.

Sources: NASA Eris page; NASA dwarf-planets overview; NASA Planet X page (5–10 Earth masses, 10,000–20,000 years, unobserved).

## Example objects

- Eris — scattered disc dwarf planet. Closest 37.9 AU, farthest 97.6 AU, period 557 years, diameter about 2,326 km, more massive than Pluto. Moon Dysnomia. Found in 2005 from 2003 Palomar data; its Pluto-size bulk forced the 2006 dwarf-planet class.
- Sedna — detached prototype. Closest 76 AU, farthest about 936 AU, period about 11,400 years. Roughly 900–1,000 km across, very red, no known moon. Found in 2003, announced 2004.
- 2012 VP113 and Leleakuhonua — further sednoids with closest points beyond 65–80 AU, lining up the detached class behind Sedna.
- Planet Nine (hypothesis, unobserved, contested) — predicted world near 10 Earth masses on a 10,000–20,000-year loop, proposed in 2016 to explain lined-up distant orbits; searches have not found it.

Sources: NASA Eris page (`https://science.nasa.gov/dwarf-planets/eris/`); NASA dwarf-planets overview; NASA Planet X page.
