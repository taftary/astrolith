# Oort cloud — the spherical reservoir

Loop doc for Issue #224.

## How it looks

Almost nothing — which is the point. The Oort cloud is a vast, thin shell of icy bodies around the Sun, round in every direction, not flat like the belts inside. Pictures show it as a pale bubble with the planets, the Kuiper Belt, and the scattered disc as a tiny bright center (see `entry.md`). Inside sits a flatter, thicker doughnut — the inner Hills cloud, about 2,000–20,000 AU out — and outside sits the true sphere — the outer cloud, about 20,000–100,000 AU out, a quarter to halfway to the next star. No body in it has ever been seen directly; we know it only by the comets that fall from it.

## How it changes over time

Each body creeps around the Sun over millions of years. Change comes from outside: the slow pull of the galaxy, passing stars, and giant gas clouds nudge the orbits until a few bodies per year drop toward the planets and become long-period comets (see `comets.md`). Near the Sun they sprout tails, loop once, and leave for thousands or millions of years — or break apart. The shell itself lasts the age of the solar system, slowly thinned by ejections and star passages. In about 1.3 million years the star Gliese 710 will pass through the outer edge and shake fresh comets loose (see `lifetime.md`).

Sources: NASA Oort cloud facts (galactic tide and star-passage nudges, long-period source); NASA Voyager mission pages.

## How it is created

The cloud did not form out there. After the planets formed 4.6 billion years ago, Jupiter and the other giants threw leftover ice chunks outward (see `giants.md`). Some left the Sun entirely; the rest parked in far loops where the galaxy's pull beat the planets' pull and rounded the spray into a sphere. The inner Hills part stayed flatter because it sits closer and remembers the flat birth disk. The cloud may also have caught a few wanderers born around other stars.

Sources: NASA Oort cloud facts (planet scattering plus galactic settling); NASA Kuiper Belt facts.

## How it interacts with the others

The Oort cloud is the source, not the neighbor. It feeds the long-period comets that cross everything inside — giants, belts, and inner system (see `comets.md` and `giants.md`). Short-period comets mostly come from closer in: the Kuiper Belt and scattered disc (see `kuiper.md`, `scattered.md`). The galaxy pulls from outside; the planets guard inside. The heliosphere edge near 120 AU, where Voyager 1 crossed in 2012 and Voyager 2 in 2018, sits far inside the cloud's inner rim — the craft will not reach the cloud for about 300 years and will need tens of thousands of years to cross it.

Sources: NASA Oort cloud facts (long- vs short-period split, Voyager timescales); NASA Voyager mission overview (`https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/mission-overview/`).

## Size and mass

Distances: inner edge about 2,000–5,000 AU, inner Hills cloud about 2,000–20,000 AU as a thick disc, outer sphere about 20,000–100,000 AU — the L7 anchor at 100,000 AU (1.50 x 10^16 m). Count: perhaps hundreds of billions to 0.1–2 trillion icy bodies, each kilometers across, adding up to a few Earth masses spread unimaginably thin. Seen: zero directly.

Sources: NASA Oort cloud facts (5,000–100,000 AU framing, hundreds of billions to trillions); ladder `docs/universes/ladder.md` (L7 anchor).

## Example objects

- Inner Hills cloud (2,000–20,000 AU) — flatter torus, the bridge between the scattered disc and the sphere; Sedna-like orbits may brush its inner rim (see `scattered.md`).
- Outer Oort cloud (20,000–100,000 AU) — the true sphere, home of the long-period reservoir.
- Comet Siding Spring (C/2013 A1) — Oort visitor that passed Mars in 2014 and will not return for about 740,000 years.
- Comet ISON (C/2012 S1) — Oort visitor that broke apart passing the Sun.
- Voyager 1 and 2 (contrast) — human craft near 120 AU, about 300 years from even entering the cloud at current speed.

Sources: NASA Oort cloud facts; NASA Voyager mission pages.
