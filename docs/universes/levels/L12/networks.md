## How it looks

Roads read as thin grey and tan lines, high contrast by day: grids in cities, long curves across
countryside, interchanges as knots and cloverleafs. At night they are lit ribbons. Rails read
thinner, darker, with straighter curves; railyards read as fans and combs. Corridors for power and
pipelines read as straight clearings often parallel to roads and rails, best in vegetation bands.
Sentinel-2 at 10 m resolves highways and wide arterials, not lane markings; the 290 km swath with
5-day revisit tracks change. The Mexicali view shows city grids meeting farm grids with canals as
thin lines beside roads.

## How it changes over time

Networks change slowly by growth — new bypasses, widenings, interchanges — and resurfacing changes
brightness. They change fast by use — jams, construction closures, floods and snow cover. Rails
change by abandonment versus siding addition; corridors change by vegetation regrowth after clearing.
Night lights dim and brighten with use.

Sources: FHWA pages; USGS pages.

## How it is created

Roads are planned, graded, and paved to Interstate standards: minimum four 12-foot lanes, 10-foot
shoulders, full access control, 50–70 mph design speed. Rails are graded bed with ballast, ties, and
steel rails at 1.435 m standard gauge in the US. Corridors are co-located for permitting — highway,
rail, power, and pipeline sharing valleys and passes; I-90 follows the Erie Canal and lake shores
across New York, with passes like Snoqualmie and Homestake (6,329 ft, highest on I-90).

Sources: FHWA Route Log pages; USGS National Map transportation pages.

## How it interacts with the others

Networks interact as the links that cities, towns, farmland, and landscapes share. They fragment
habitats, fields, and cities; they bridge rivers — I-90 over the Columbia at Vantage and over the
Missouri — tunnel under hills and cross lakes on causeways. They create runoff, heat, and impervious
surface with wildlife barrier effects. They feed suburbs, ports, and airports: I-90 ends at the
Seattle port and Boston Logan. USGS models them as separate interacting layers: controlled-access
highway, secondary highway, local road, ramp, tunnel, railroad, siding, ferry route, trail.

Sources: FHWA pages; USGS transportation pages.

## Typical size and mass

Interstate paved width runs 24–50 m with right-of-way 50–100 m; urban streets about 18 m; rail beds
5–10 m with corridors 15–30 m; power corridors 20–50 m. Length dominates: US public roads exceed
4 million miles; the Interstate runs about 49,699 miles including Alaska, Hawaii, and Puerto Rico
(42,793 Interstate-funded). Mass is negligible beside geology; L12 states length and width, not mass.
I-90 runs 3,020.44 miles FHWA official (Seattle to Boston, 13 states, longest Interstate); street
blocks run 0.1–1 km — Manhattan 80 by 274 m, Chicago 100 by 200 m, Portland 61 m, Salt Lake 200 m.

Sources: FHWA Route Finder; encyclopedia city-block and I-90 pages.

## Example objects

- Example: I-90, 3,020 miles Seattle to Boston — the longest Interstate; busiest at Chicago
  (306,000 vehicles per day), quietest in rural Wyoming.
- Example: Manhattan blocks, 80 by 274 m — archetype dense grid; Chicago 100 by 200 m, 16 per mile.
- Example: Erie Canal with Thruway and rail in parallel — archetype shared corridor across New York.
