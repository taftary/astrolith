# Layout — how the pieces sit in 1–10 m, circulation, the 1 m end point

## How it looks

Layout reads as zones plus circulation: named activity centers — sleep, work, eat, keep, move —
with clear floor space and passageways between them, not just furniture footprints. Two orders
divide the history: cellular rooms, each with one function behind a door off a corridor, and
open plans, where living, dining, and kitchen flow without dividing walls. Wright's Willey
House (1933–34) drew the break: no walls or doors dividing the non-private areas, living and
dining combined, kitchen exposed through built-in cabinets and open shelving. The 1 m end point
is reach plus shelf plus door swing: unobstructed forward and side reach runs 15 in low to 48
in high; over an obstruction the max drops — 44 in forward over a 20-to-25-in counter, 46 in
side at 10-to-24-in depth over a low obstruction.

## How it changes over time

Single-function rooms were not original. From the 17th century rooms acquired one function each
and went reachable only from corridors and halls; central fireplaces moved to walls; the great
chamber as best dining room gave way to the saloon by the early 18th century. The open plan
arrived in December 1933, when Wright drew the Willey single-level inline-ell: kitchen
communicating with dining and living through a glass screened partition — the first full
exposure of kitchen to formal space — consolidated in Jacobs I (1936–37), the first built
Usonian near 1,500 sq ft for $5,500 with land, open living-dining-kitchen flow, prototype for
140-plus Usonians and the postwar ranch. Small-house planning standardized with the FHA: the
National Housing Act signed 27 June 1934, the 1936 Principles of Planning Small Houses
(maximum accommodation within minimum means) the gold standard — the basic minimum house
living plus kitchen plus two bedrooms plus bath. Change comes by wall removal and zone merger,
and by code minimums ratcheting what a legal room must hold.

Sources: domestic-interiors encyclopedia pages; Wright Willey and Jacobs House pages; National Housing Act pages.

## How it is created

Layouts are drawn from clearance numbers. Walkways run 36 in minimum where no work happens;
work aisles 42 in for one cook, 48 for several, measured face to face. Dining pull-out needs
32 in from the table edge with nobody passing behind, 36 to squeeze past, 44 to walk past.
Hallways hold 36 in minimum per the dwelling code; egress doors clear 32 in open at 90
degrees; doorways clear 32 in minimum (34 the access standard). The work triangle sums sink,
cook, and fridge center-to-center: at most 26 ft total, each leg 4 to 9 ft, no leg crossing an
island by more than 12 in, no major traffic through it. Bed sides take about 24 in tight
(trade guidance; 30 to 36 comfortable). The code floor under everything: habitable rooms at
least 70 sq ft (kitchens excepted) and 7 ft in every horizontal direction. Every clearance
touches two parts — a 42-in aisle is cabinet face to cabinet face, a passageway 36 wide by 80
high with projections capped at 4 in, clear floor space 30 by 48 in never blocked by door
swings or cabinetry.

Sources: NKBA kitchen-planning guidelines; IRC R311 egress and hallway pages; IRC R304 room-size pages.

## How it interacts with the others

Layout binds all five other parts into one plan. The shell's doors swing through it, its
windows light it, its outlets ring it (see `shell.md`). The furniture's pull-outs and
walk-behinds size it (see `furniture.md`). The storage's triangle and reach band anchor it
(see `storage.md`). The lamps layer it and the vents cross it (see `light-power.md`). The rugs
zone it (see `textiles-decor.md`). Nothing in a room stands alone: move the table and the
walkway moves, move the walkway and the rug moves, move the rug and the lamp moves. The
reading guide for the whole story runs through `lifetime.md`.

Sources: NKBA guideline pages; part docs named above.

## Typical size and mass

A legal room starts at 70 sq ft and 7 ft across; a Usonian open plan near 1,500 sq ft; a
capsule endpoint near 2.3 by 3.8 m by 2.1 high. The layout itself masses nothing — it is lines
on paper and clearances in air — but it commands every kilogram in the room: one 11-by-12-ft
bedroom's plan places roughly half a ton of shell, furniture, storage, and textiles.

Sources: IRC R304 pages; Jacobs House pages; Nakagin Capsule Tower pages.

## Example objects

- The Frankfurt Kitchen, 1926–27 — 1.9 by 3.4 m double-file one-worker plan, entrance opposite the window, modeled on rail dining cars and time-motion studies; some 10,000 installed across New Frankfurt.
- The Willey House open plan, 1933–34 — first full kitchen-to-formal-space exposure through a glass screened partition; template for Jacobs I and every open plan after.
- The Nakagin Capsule Tower capsule, Tokyo, 1972 — 2.3 by 3.8 m by 2.1 m high with a single porthole, 140 plug-in units on two cores, built-in furniture and compact bath: the small-room plan at its endpoint.

Sources: Frankfurt kitchen pages; Wright Willey and Jacobs House pages; Nakagin Capsule Tower pages.
