# Shell — floors, walls, ceilings, doors, windows

Part doc for Issue #231 (Round 2 loop). One of six L14 parts: the room shell at 1–10 m.

## How it looks

A room shell is the enclosure you stand inside: a floor underfoot, walls within reach, a ceiling
just overhead, a door to enter, a window to the outside. Floors run hardwood, carpet, or tile —
the three common American choices, with wood in about half of homes, carpet in three-quarters,
tile in well over half. Walls are most often gypsum panel: a gypsum rock core sandwiched between
two layers of paper, fire-resistant types mixing glass fibre into the core. Older walls are wet
plaster — lime or gypsum with water and sand, hardening as it dries, one of the most ancient
building techniques, with 4,000-year-old Egyptian gypsum plaster still hard. Ceilings sit
conventionally at 96 in (8 ft), the height of two 4-ft drywall sheets stacked; suspended
minimums run 90 in. Doors are stock 30 or 32 in wide by 80 in tall (the 6/8 door), 1-3/8 in
thick, in six-panel molded or flush-slab styles. Windows divide into double-hung (two sashes
sliding up and down), casement (hinged at the side, swinging outward), and picture (fixed,
never opening), stock widths 24 to 48 in. Bedroom windows double as escape: net clear opening
at least 5.7 sq ft (5.0 at grade), at least 20 in wide and 24 in high, sill no more than 44 in
above the floor.

## How it changes over time

Shell ages on different clocks. Interior paint lasts 15 years or more but homeowners repaint far
more often — trade guidance runs 5 to 10 years for living rooms and bedrooms, 3 to 5 for
kitchens and baths, 2 to 4 for hallways. Carpet runs 8 to 10 years with normal traffic and
maintenance; vinyl reaches 50, linoleum about 25. Hardwood runs a century or more: a maintenance
coat (clean, light sand, fresh finish) renews the surface, a full sand to bare wood rescues it
when the finish wears through — floors centuries old are still in service. Whole eras of shell
succeed each other: lath-and-plaster (the modern form of wattle-and-daub) before drywall, then
the gypsum panel taking over in the postwar tract-housing boom — faster, with fewer laborers.
Change comes by recoat and refinish, and by era turnover from plaster to panel.

Sources: NAHB life-expectancy study; NWFA refinishing pages; Atlantic drywall history; USG history pages.

## How it is created

The predominant method is light frame with panel skin. Load-bearing walls run studs 16 in
on-center; drywall sheets fasten to the frame with nails, screws, or adhesive (or over furring),
joints filled and feathered with a joint tool until the joint disappears under paint, nailheads
dimpled then compounded. The panel itself was invented as Sackett Board, patented 1894 — gypsum
plaster between paper, installed in a single day against weeks for wet plaster; USG bought
Sackett in 1909, folded edges arrived 1910, solid core 1913, flush square edges 1916, rebranded
Sheetrock 1917, marketed from 1921 as a fireproof wall with no drying wait. Interior finishes
run in a fixed order per the home-building sequence: drywall, then paint and stain, bath tile,
flooring, cabinetry, interior carpentry, countertops, fixtures. The average new American house
carries over 6,000 ft of drywall; a standard sheet covers 4 by 8 ft.

Sources: Hunker stud guide; Britannica drywall-construction page; USG history and Sheetrock centennial pages; Atlantic drywall history; NAHB home-building process overview; National Inventors Hall of Fame Sackett page.

## How it interacts with the others

The shell is what everything else fastens to, plugs into, and moves through. Heavy pieces must
anchor to wood studs or a weight-bearing wall — screws in hollow drywall alone fail, which is
why electrical boxes nailed to a stud's side double as stud finders. Power follows the 12-ft /
6-ft rule: no point along any wall's floor line sits more than 6 ft from a receptacle, so
receptacles land at most 12 ft apart and only those under 5-and-a-half ft count — the intent is
to kill extension cords. Light and air are code-shaped too: habitable rooms need aggregate
glazing of at least 8 percent of floor area, which constrains where furniture, door swings, and
vents may go. The shell sets the legal minimums the layout must respect — 70 sq ft rooms, 7 ft
in every direction, 7 ft ceilings — and the furniture answers inside them (see `furniture.md`
for the pieces, `storage.md` for the keeping, `layout.md` for the plan).

Sources: Allen drywall stud guide; IRC E3901 receptacle rules; IRC R304/R305 room rules; ICC R303 glazing pages.

## Typical size and mass

A standard drywall sheet is 4 by 8 ft (32 sq ft), with 4 by 10 and 4 by 12 ft for long runs;
half-inch panel suits walls and ceilings on 16-in centers. Stock interior doors run 24 to 36 in
wide (30 and 32 most common) by 80, 84, or 96 in tall. Stock double-hung windows run about 24
to 48 in wide. The shell of one room — a bedroom of about 11 by 12 ft with 8-ft ceilings —
carries roughly 500 sq ft of wall and ceiling panel, a few hundred pounds of gypsum and paper
before paint and trim.

Sources: BuildFigure sheet-size guide; Doors.com and Pella size pages; Wharton ceilings paper.

## Example objects

- Fallingwater, Mill Run, Pennsylvania — Frank Lloyd Wright's 1935–39 weekend house where stone floors run out to the terraces, corner windows open outward to break the box, and plate glass frames the woods; no exterior wall faces the falls, only the stone core.
- Monticello dome room and oculus, Charlottesville, Virginia — Jefferson's octagonal skyroom lit by a 4-ft-diameter, 49-lb mouth-blown bull's-eye glass oculus, restored December 1989 to his specifications; the dome idea came home from his France years 1784–89.
- The Gropius House living room, Lincoln, Massachusetts — 1938 modern shell holding annotated seating and a fireplace wall, the furnished-box ideal in one frame.

Sources: Fallingwater design history pages; Monticello oculus-restoration pages; HABS Gropius House photo MASS,9-LIN,16-33.
