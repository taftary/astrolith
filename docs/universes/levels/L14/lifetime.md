## Single-room huts (before ~2500 BCE)

The first rooms are one room: Neolithic houses near 5 by 5 m, chalk walls and floors, central
hearth, thatched roof — reconstructions from the 2006–07 Durrington Walls digs, radiocarbon-set
to the Stonehenge sarsen phase. Rectangular long houses of mudbrick, timber, and reeds follow:
one door, no windows, the hearth heating and cooking at once. Nothing is dated to a year; the
one-room plan persists into every later era as the vernacular under the monuments.

Sources: English Heritage Neolithic-houses pages.

## Ancient rooms (~2500 BCE–500 CE)

Walls divide and rooms multiply. The Roman domus, best preserved where Vesuvius buried Pompeii
and Herculaneum in 79 AD, names them: atrium (open-roof central hall with rain basin),
cubicula (bedrooms), tablinum (reception and office), triclinium (dining room), culina
(kitchen), peristyle garden — often over two storeys. Mudbrick rectangles grow rooms as
families grow; storehouse and shrine split off from the house — the first storage and civic
parts. Walls settle into the two families still standing: load-bearing walls carrying floors
and roofs, nonbearing walls merely dividing.

Sources: Met Roman-housing essay; Britannica house pages.

## Medieval halls (~500–1600)

One great room holds the household: the great hall — dining, receiving guests, sleeping,
holding court. Owners withdraw over centuries to private chambers (withdrawing rooms, solars);
by the 17th century many halls demote to servants' halls or entrance vestibules. Timber framing
fills the towns beside stone churches and guildhalls; the chimney stack arrives to carry smoke
above the roof; market halls double as government. The chamber as a private room is born here.

Sources: BBC great-hall pages; Britannica construction pages.

## Early modern specialization (1600s–1800s)

Rooms take single functions. In the 1600s the hall splits into kitchen, parlor, and bedchamber;
Plymouth inventories 1670–85 relabel the hall as kitchen, the parlor becoming the best
multi-purpose room. Kitchens move away from living areas for smell and privacy once surplus
allows. The Victorian peak proliferates billiard rooms, morning rooms, parlours, studies —
later unaffordable, never repeated. Monticello's 1809 kitchen runs state-of-the-art French
stew-stove technology under enslaved chefs; its dome room keeps 8 round windows and a 4-ft
oculus over a 26-ft circle.

Sources: Plymouth folk-house pages; BBC kitchen-history pages; Monticello kitchen and dome-room pages.

## Industrial furniture and light (1850s–1920s)

The room industrializes. Thonet's Chair No.14 (1859) makes furniture by factory: bent beech,
flat-pack, 50 million by 1930. Swan's house goes electric (first lit house), Newcastle's hall
goes electric February 1879 (first lit public building), Edison's team holds 13-plus hours
October 1879 and bamboo past 1,200; Pearl Street starts central power September 1882 and the
Savoy plays under 1,200 Swan lamps from 1881. Central heating matures — hot-air 1793,
high-pressure hot water 1830s, San Galli radiators 1855–57, American Radiator 1892 — and
indoor plumbing standardizes near 1900. Drywall patents 1894, Sheetrock 1917; the Frankfurt
Kitchen (1926–27) fits the kitchen into 1.9 by 3.4 m for 10,000 flats.

Sources: V&A Thonet pages; DOE light-bulb history; Science Museum electric-lighting pages; central-heating pages; USG history; MoMA Frankfurt Kitchen interactive.

## Modern fitted rooms (1930s–2000s)

The plan opens and the box standardizes. Willey 1933 exposes the kitchen through glass; Jacobs
I 1936–37 builds the Usonian open flow; the FHA (1934) and its 1936 small-house principles set
the minimum house — living, kitchen, two bedrooms, bath. Drywall covers half of new American
homes by 1955, nearly all today; the 8-ft ceiling becomes two stacked sheets. Frankfurt
kitchens mostly discard in the 1960s–70s for easy-clean surfaces; Eames shells change ply and
veneer across decades; mattresses settle on the 7-to-10-year rule; carpets on 8 to 10. The
soft layer turns over fastest, the shell slowest.

Sources: Wright Willey and Jacobs pages; HUD history timeline; USG Sheetrock history; Eames pages; Sleep Foundation and NAHB life pages.

## Present and far future (2000s–)

Today's room layers daylight with LED past 200 lumens per watt, heats through panels and floors,
keeps in frameless boxes, dresses in washable softness — and the capsule endpoint waits at 2.3
by 3.8 m: Nakagin's 1972 plug-in rooms with porthole, built-in furniture, compact bath. The
labeled trends (not facts): smart rooms integrating light, shade, heat, and voice since the
1990s open standards; sustainable, minimal, warm-natural material direction in the 2020s. The
fate, if growth, material change, and abandonment run on: shells stripped to panel and stud,
furniture to frame and foam, textiles first to go — rooms reverting to enclosures awaiting
their next soft layer.

Sources: LED lamp pages; Nakagin Capsule Tower pages; smart-home history pages.

## Timescale cross-check

- Hut to domus: ~2500 BCE → 79 AD (two-plus millennia, one room → named rooms).
- Hall to chamber: ~500 → 1600s (a millennium, one hall → withdrawing rooms).
- Specialization to peak: 1600s → 1800s Victorian (two centuries, split → proliferation).
- Industrial room: 1859 → 1927 (seventy years, chair → fitted kitchen).
- Modern room: 1917 → 1955 (forty years, Sheetrock → half of new homes).
- Soft turnover inside one shell: carpets 8–10 yr, mattresses 7–10 yr, paint 5–15 yr.

Sources: pages named in each section above.
