# Textiles and small objects — rugs, curtains, art, decor

Part doc for Issue #231 (Round 2 loop). One of six L14 parts: the soft layer at 1–10 m.

## How it looks

Textiles are the soft layer over the hard room: rugs on the floor, curtains at the window, art
on the wall, small objects on the shelf. Standard rectangular rugs sell at 2-by-3, 3-by-5,
4-by-6, 5-by-7, 5-by-8, 6-by-9, 8-by-10, 9-by-12, 10-by-14, and 12-by-15 ft — living rooms
mostly buy 5-by-8, 8-by-10, 9-by-12. In inches: 5-by-8 is 60 by 96 (152 by 244 cm, 40 sq ft),
8-by-10 is 96 by 120 (the most-bought, most versatile, 80 sq ft), 9-by-12 is 108 by 144 (108
sq ft). Runners run 2 to 3 ft wide by 6 to 14 long; round rugs 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 ft across. Pile
is trimmed knotted yarn standing up — about 2 mm on the oldest survivor — knotted
symmetrical-double (Turkish) at thousands of knots per square decimeter for density. Placement
follows the furniture: 8-by-10 holds the front legs of sofa and chairs (the conversation
zone), 9-by-12 and up hold all four legs of every piece, 12 to 18 in of bare floor left to the
wall; a dining rug extends 24 to 30 in past the table edge so chairs stay on the rug.

## How it changes over time

Textiles age fastest in the room. Carpet keeps 8 to 10 years with maintenance and normal
traffic — against vinyl's 50, linoleum's 25, wood's century-plus. Dyed wool fades: the oldest
pile carpet's once-bright yellows, blues, and reds read faded now but must have glowed.
Sun takes curtains and south-facing rugs first; feet take the traffic lanes; whole rugs retire
to smaller rooms before they retire outright. Change comes by rotation and cleaning, by fading
into dimmer versions of themselves, and by replacement on the decade — the soft layer turns
over several times inside one shell's life.

Sources: NAHB life-expectancy study; Hermitage Pazyryk pages.

## How it is created

The oldest pile carpet known is the Pazyryk, 5th–4th century BC: wool, symmetrical-double
knots, found in Pazyryk Barrow No.5 (Rudenko's 1949 Altai dig), 183 by 200 cm, over 1,250,000
knots, origin unknown — Iranian, Central Asian, or a copy of a Persian original. It sits in the
State Hermitage, St. Petersburg, inventory 1687-93. The method persists into every machine era:
knot, trim, lay flat — only the loom and the dye change. Curtain and shade hardware follows the
window: rails, tracks, and rods sized to the opening, hung to clear the glazing and the egress
path. Small objects arrive last in the build sequence, after fixtures — the room is finished
before it is dressed.

Sources: Hermitage Pazyryk pages; Central Asia Program Pazyryk pages.

## How it interacts with the others

Rugs anchor furniture groupings the way tables anchor rooms: legs on or off the rug decide the
zone, and the bare-floor border to the wall keeps the shell visible. Curtains answer the shell's
windows — glazing area, sill height, egress clear opening — softening daylight by day and
framing the lamp glow by night. Art answers the wall: its weight meets the stud rule from
`shell.md`, its subject the room's use from `layout.md`. Small objects live on the storage's
shelves at the reach band's heights (see `storage.md`), dusted where the vents from
`light-power.md` blow. The soft layer is the cheapest redo in the room — new rug, new
curtains, same shell.

Sources: rug-size placement guides; shell outlet and stud rules (see `shell.md`).

## Typical size and mass

The working numbers: 8-by-10 at 80 sq ft (28 less than 9-by-12's 108), runners to 14 ft, rounds
to 12 ft across, Pazyryk at 183 by 200 cm. One parlor's soft set — a 9-by-12 rug, four curtain
panels, six framed pieces, a dozen small objects — covers about 10 m2 of floor and wall and
masses a few dozen kilograms of wool, cotton, glass, and wood.

Sources: rug-size guides; Hermitage Pazyryk pages.

## Example objects

- The Pazyryk carpet, 5th–4th century BC — oldest known pile carpet, 183 by 200 cm of knotted wool, State Hermitage St. Petersburg; pile near 2 mm, dyes faded from bright yellow, blue, and red.
- The Gresham House parlor, Galveston, Texas — Victorian furnished parlor with patterned rug, tall curtained windows, upholstered settee and chairs, overmantel mirror, and chandelier, photographed as HABS found it.
- The Smithsonian Castle parlor (Room 201), Washington, DC — furnished secretary's anteroom in the east wing's second floor, landscape of kept things in a federal interior.

Sources: Hermitage Pazyryk pages; HABS Gresham House photo TEX,84-GALV,26-9; HABS Smithsonian photo DC,WASH,520B-77.
