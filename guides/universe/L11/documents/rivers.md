# Rivers — the flow of regions

This page describes rivers, lakes,
valleys, and deltas at L11 scale (10^5–10^6 m, 100–1,000 km frames): looks,
changes, creation, and interactions with
land, ranges, plains, and seas. Bridge in `previous-l10.md`, dive in `entry.md`,
timeline in `lifetime.md`. Siblings: [continents](./continents.md) (base),
[mountains](./mountains.md) (lift), [plains](./plains.md) (flat),
[oceans](./oceans.md) (blue).

## How it looks

From orbit a river reads as a thin dark-blue thread winding through green-brown
land, widening to lakes as still blue spots and to deltas as fans where it meets
the sea. Valleys cut V-lines with terraces; canyons cut sharp slots (Lake Powell
frame: drowned canyon with blue water threading red rock, low levels showing pale
bathtub rings). Deltas fan brown-green into blue. Towns line the threads.
Lakes sort by look and origin: glacial scour lakes (the Great Lakes, thousands
of feet deep in places) read as broad blue sheets with plume edges; rift lakes
(Baikal — oldest, largest, deepest freshwater lake at nearly a mile deep and
over 23,000 km3, a volume equal to all the Great Lakes combined — Tanganyika)
read as long straight slots; reservoir lakes (Powell, Mead) read as drowned
branching canyons, water level written in pale bathtub rings; saline lakes
(Great Salt Lake, Dead Sea) read pale or rimmed with salt where no outlet lets
fresh water escape.

## How it changes over time

Rivers cut and fill daily to yearly. Floods move banks m in a day; droughts drop
lakes m in a year (Lake Powell record lows show rings tens of m high). Valleys
deepen mm per year; deltas switch lobes every 1,000–1,500 years (with
decadal lobe growth and storm-driven loss between switches); lakes fill with
silt then dry to salt over thousands of years. Ice dams break in days; dams
built by people hold back decades of silt. A river is never the same twice:
thread moves, lake levels breathe, deltas grow then wash.
Dated watch (2020–2026): the Southwest megadrought — ongoing since about 2000 —
kept Powell falling through the decade. Powell dipped below its April 13, 2023
record (3,519.92 ft) in mid-August 2026 and reached 3,517.24 ft on September 10,
2026, still above the 3,490-ft minimum power pool; the cause was the 2025–2026
Upper Basin snow drought plus record warmth, so spring melt never arrived. The
Bureau of Reclamation answered in April 2026 with Flaming Gorge releases into
Powell, reduced Powell-to-Mead releases, and cancelled spring and summer
ecological releases. Powell feeds Mead downstream and together they water over
40 million people and about 5 million acres of farmland; Mead hit record lows
in August 2026 alongside Powell.
Sources: USGS stream pages; NASA drought stories.

## How it is created

Rivers gather rain and snowmelt: high ranges shed water, slopes join threads,
threads join trunks to the sea. Valleys carve by water and ice; lakes pool behind
moraines, landslides, lava, or dams; deltas build where rivers slow at seas.
Lake Powell pools behind Glen Canyon Dam in drowned canyon rock — water stored in
desert stone. The water cycle drives all: evaporate seas, rain on land, run to sea.
Deltas grow by the delta cycle: the river deposits sand, clay, and silt at its
outfall into a lobe, then avulses — jumps to a shorter path to the sea about
every 1,000–1,500 years — abandoning the old lobe to subside and erode while a
new lobe builds. The modern Mississippi birdfoot delta formed this way over
about 4,500 years (six complexes, Maringouin through Plaquemines-Balize plus
the mid-20th-century Atchafalaya and Wax Lake outlets); basin-wide levees since
the 1930s and upriver dams now starve most lobes of sediment, so loss outruns
gain. The Ganges–Brahmaputra builds the largest submarine fan on Earth (about
3.9 million km2 in Bengal) at about 1 billion tonnes of sediment per year, 95
percent of it moved in the May–October monsoon.
Sources: USGS water-cycle pages; NASA lake pages.

## How it interacts with the others

A river interacts with everything it touches. It drains continents
(`continents.md`) to the sea; it cuts ranges (`mountains.md`) into valleys; it
waters plains (`plains.md`) or floods them; it builds coasts (`oceans.md`) into
deltas. Air feeds it by rain; ice feeds it by melt; life drinks and roots along it.
Rivers carry mountains to the sea grain by grain.
Watersheds set the budget: every drop inside the divide runs to one outlet, so
streamflow and water quality reflect everything happening upstream — rainfall,
infiltration, evaporation, storage in reservoirs, and withdrawals. The
Mississippi drains about 3.2 million km2 (about 40 percent of the continental
United States, 31 states and two Canadian provinces, fourth-largest watershed
on Earth) through one delta; the Amazon drains about 7.1 million km2 of
rainforest through one mouth. That sediment builds coasts: the Ganges–Brahmaputra
at about 1 billion tonnes per year feeds the Bengal Fan, the Amazon at about
the same order feeds a plume carried northwest along the Guianas coast, and the
Mississippi at about 13,000 m3 per second mean flow (470,000 cu ft per second)
once built 12,000 km2 of delta wetlands — now starved by levees. Key numbers
are collected below; the river-meets-sea counterpart is the Rupert Bay frame
in [oceans](./oceans.md#example-objects).
Sources: USGS watershed pages;
NASA water pages.

## Typical size and mass

- Size: Nile about 6,650 km (4,132 mi) — conventionally the longest; Amazon
  about 6,400 km (4,000 mi, Amazon-Ucayali-Apurimac) — longest by discharge
  and drainage, with 2007–2008 claims of about 6,992 km via the southern
  outlet and Para estuary still unsettled because source, mouth, and
  measurement-scale definitions differ. Yangtze about 6,300 km (3,915 mi)
  third; Mississippi–Missouri–Red Rock about 5,971 km (3,710 mi) fourth;
  Colorado about 2,330 km (1,450 mi). Lake Powell about 300 km of winding
  water when full. L11 frames 100–1,000 km — whole tributary nets, lake
  systems, delta fans. Valleys 100 m to 1,000 m deep; deltas tens of km across.
- Mass: Amazon pours about 209,000 m3 per second (about 15 percent of all
  river water reaching the oceans, some 6,000 km3 per year); Mississippi about
  16,792 m3 per second (15th by discharge); Mississippi delta mean flow about
  13,000 m3 per second at the outlet. Lakes scale the mass: Baikal over 23,000
  km3 (as much as all the Great Lakes combined, nearly a mile deep); Powell
  about 30 km3 at full pool. A 100-km lake 50 m deep holds on the order of
  10^14 kg of water. Silt loads percent by weight in floods.
- Levels: Lake Powell full pool 3,700 ft, record low 3,517.24 ft on September
  10, 2026 (previous record 3,519.92 ft on April 13, 2023; minimum power pool
  3,490 ft) — about 180 ft / 55 m down after the 2025–2026 snow drought;
  rivers rise m in floods; deltas grow m per year.

Sources: USGS flow pages; NASA lake pages.

## Example objects

Example: Lake Powell (target image) — dammed Colorado dreaming in desert canyon,
record-low rings marking drought (3,517.24 ft on September 10, 2026, the lowest
on record; previous record 3,519.92 ft on April 13, 2023). Example: Amazon —
trunk with thousand-thread net draining about 7.1 million km2 of rainforest,
pouring about 209,000 m3 per second with about a billion tonnes of sediment
per year riding the plume northwest along the Guianas coast, and a huge delta
fan at the Atlantic. Example: Nile — desert thread with green delta and fan
into the Mediterranean, its valley the line of night lights through Egypt;
about 6,650 km conventionally longest, shared by eleven countries. Example:
Mississippi — continent-draining system (Missouri 2,540 mi plus Mississippi
2,340 mi; combined 3,710 mi / 5,971 km, fourth-longest) moving about 500
million tons of cargo per year through its delta ports, seventh-largest delta
on Earth at about 12,000 km2. Example: Ganges–Brahmaputra — monsoon-fed
sediment engine at about 1 billion tonnes per year building the Bengal Fan
(about 3.9 million km2, largest submarine fan on Earth) and the most
populous delta plain. Example: Baikal — rift slot, oldest and deepest
freshwater lake, holding about 20 percent of fresh surface water. Example:
Grand Canyon — river-cut slot about 446 km through the canyon reach (277 mi,
about 2,000 ft of drop), up to about 1,800 m deep, carved over the past 5–6
million years by downcutting, uplift of the Colorado Plateau (Laramide, about
70–40 million years ago), and widening by tributaries; pre-dam spring floods
to about 300,000 cu ft per second did the cutting work now held back by Glen
Canyon Dam. Sources: USGS pages;
NASA frames.

## Key numbers

- L11 frames: 100–1,000 km; Nile 6,650 km; Amazon 6,400 km; Yangtze 6,300 km;
  Mississippi–Missouri 5,971 km; Powell 300 km winding.
- Flows: Amazon 209,000 m3/s (about 15 percent of global river discharge);
  Mississippi 16,792 m3/s (15th); delta outlet mean about 13,000 m3/s;
  floods rise m in hours; Colorado pre-dam floods to 300,000 cu ft per second.
- Lakes: Baikal over 23,000 km3 (about 20 percent of fresh surface water;
  Great Lakes about the same); Powell about 30 km3 at full pool, 3,700 ft
  full, 3,517.24 ft record low (September 10, 2026), 3,490 ft minimum power
  pool; deltas grow m per year.
- Valleys: Grand Canyon about 446 km through-canyon, 1,800 m deep, 5–6 Ma;
  Mississippi delta about 12,000 km2 (seventh-largest); Bengal Fan about
  3.9 million km2; watersheds Mississippi 3.2 million km2, Amazon 7.1
  million km2.
- Water context: oceans hold about 96.5 percent of all water; freshwater
  about 2.5 percent; surface water barely over 1.2 percent of freshwater;
  rivers about 0.49 percent of surface freshwater — yet rivers move most
  of the water people use.

Sources: ladder L11 row; USGS; NASA.
