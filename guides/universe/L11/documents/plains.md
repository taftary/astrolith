# Plains — the flat of regions

This page describes plains, basins, lowlands, and deserts
at L11 scale (10^5–10^6 m): how they look from orbit, how they change through time,
how they are created, and how they interact with ranges, continents, waters, and
rivers. The flat is the most lived-on landform: grasslands feed herds and crops,
basins hold lakes and salt, deserts set the dry limit. The bridge behind us is in
`previous-l10.md`; the dive that brings you here is in `entry.md`; the dated
timeline runs through `lifetime.md`.

## How it looks

From orbit a plain reads as a broad flat fill — green farmland, tan desert, pale
basin floors — cut by thin river lines and dotted with towns. Natural grasslands
grade by rainfall: tallgrass prairie (North American Great Plains east) runs lush
green in summer; mid-latitude steppe (Eurasian steppe, western Great Plains) runs
pale gold-green, treeless and windswept; tropical savanna (Serengeti, Sahel edge,
Indian Deccan fringe) mixes grass with scattered trees and reads patchy
green-tan with the rains. Lowlands lie near sea level, smooth and farmed
(Indo-Gangetic plain, coastal plains); basins dip below surrounding land with
salt flats or shallow lakes at the center (Badwater, Great Salt Lake relic
shorelines); deserts glow tan with dune stripes and dark rock pavements between
the sand seas. The frame below shows farmland and towns on flat ground with
valleys at the edge — the settled flat at 100-km scale.

## How it changes over time

Plains fill and empty. Rivers lay silt mm per year across floodplains and deltas;
wind lays dust that compacts into loess, tens of m thick across the Chinese Loess
Plateau and the American Midwest; ice sheets flatten the ground and leave till
and outwash when they retreat. Basins sink as ground stretches or loads, then
fill with lakes that dry to salt — Badwater's crust re-dissolves in floods and
recrystallizes after storms. Deserts grow and shrink with climate: the Sahara
greens and browns with each African Humid Period, roughly every 20,000 years with
orbital precession (the last green Sahara ran about 11,000–5,000 years ago), and
its dust still rides the trade winds today — about 28 million tonnes a year reach
the Amazon basin by NASA CALIPSO satellite estimate, fertilizing its soils with
phosphorus. Farmland changes plains fastest: fields, roads, and towns in decades —
and wind takes back bare soil in years, as the 1930s Dust Bowl showed when deep
plowing plus drought stripped the southern Great Plains. A plain is a worn range
or a filled basin: build flat, hold flat, or dry flat. Eruptions reset the clock
in days where ash falls: Hunga Tonga 2022 buried nearby flats in centimeters of
ash in a single event. Dated watch (2020–2026): the Southwest megadrought held
through the decade — Lake Powell record lows in 2023 then again in August–September
2026; Texas High Plains drought and aquifer strain peaked in Landsat's September
2026 cotton-country pair; Australia's arid west swung the other way, with the
September 2026 desert-bloom report calling the Murchison wildflower display the
best in nearly two decades after wetter-than-normal winter rains. Sources: USGS landform and aquifer pages; NASA land-change and
Earth Observatory dust stories; NPS Death Valley salt-flats page; NASA Earth
Observatory 2026 lake, drought, and bloom reports.

## How it is created

Plains are made by wearing high ground flat or filling low ground up: erosion from
ranges, deposition by rivers and wind, lava flooding flat, glacial scouring smooth,
tectonic stretching dropping basins. The Great Plains wear from the Rockies —
streams carried their debris east for tens of millions of years, capping the
plains with the Ogallala Formation that now holds the High Plains aquifer;
the Amazon fills from the Andes, a foreland basin sagging under the mountain
load and stacked kilometers deep with river sediment; Sahara sands blow from old
lake beds such as Mega-Chad. Basins drop along
faults (Great Basin, Death Valley — pulling apart for about 3 million years) or
sag under loads. Coasts add their own flats: the Atlantic coastal plain is the
passive trailing edge left as North America drifted from the rift, layered flat
with Mesozoic and Cenozoic sediment. Ice sheets plane the far north smooth
(West Siberian Plain, Canadian prairies on glacial till), and flood basalts pave
flats in a geologic instant (Deccan, Columbia Plateau). Salt flats need three
things at once (NPS Badwater recipe): a source of salts from a large drainage,
an enclosed basin with no outlet to the sea, and an arid climate where
evaporation beats precipitation — Badwater's 9,000-square-mile drainage,
larger than New Hampshire, concentrates table salt plus calcite, gypsum, and
borax into its crust. Sources: USGS landform
and Pangea pages; USGS High Plains aquifer page; NPS Death Valley geology page;
NASA Observatory.

## How it interacts with the others

A plain interacts with every part. It wears from ranges (`mountains.md`) that shed
it — the Rockies feed the Great Plains, the Andes feed the Amazon basin, and the
Himalaya runoff builds the Indo-Gangetic plain; ranges also starve plains of rain
in their shadow (Atacama, Gobi, Patagonia). It sits on continents (`continents.md`)
as their cover, thickest over old craton interiors and passive margins. It drains
by rivers (`rivers.md`) that water or flood it — the Mississippi threads the
plains to the Gulf, the Amazon threads its basin to the Atlantic carrying about
a billion tonnes of sediment a year; it meets seas at flat coasts with wide beaches
(`oceans.md`), where deltas grow and storm surges flood furthest inland. Air dries
it to desert or rains it to farm; ice rims its cold edge with tundra flats; life
roots or grazes it — grasslands co-evolved with grazers from mammoths to bison to
cattle. Plains hold people, rivers feed plains, seas take plains at the edge. Sources:
USGS water pages; NASA climate and water-cycle pages; UNCCD drought overview.

## Breadbaskets and water

Plains grow the world's staple calories — wheat across the steppes and Great
Plains, maize across the US Midwest, rice across the Indo-Gangetic plain and
Chinese lowlands — because flat ground plus deep soils (chernozem, mollisols,
loess) plus summer rain makes mechanized farming possible. The water behind the
crops is often fossil: the High Plains (Ogallala) aquifer under about 174,000
square miles of eight states waters one of the largest farm regions on Earth,
and USGS records show the water table across much of Lubbock County down 25–100
ft since large-scale irrigation began about 1950 (150 ft or more in counties to
the north). The October 2026 NASA Earth Observatory Texas cotton-country story
caught the consequence from orbit: Lubbock-area fields parched brown in September
2026 against green September 2025, dryland cotton dead on farms whose families
had worked the land for generations (72 percent abandoned in 2022, about 90
percent dead on some 2026 dryland fields), wells "pumping air," and center
pivots watering halves and quarters of their circles. Playa lakes — over 19,300
small clay-lined basins in the Texas High Plains — are the recharge path, but
about 80 percent are tilled or silted and many no longer drain to the aquifer.
Globally the same strain repeats: UNCCD counts drought as touching at least 1.5
billion people at US$125 billion cost in the decade to 2017, with three-quarters
of humanity projected exposed by 2050; the Southwest megadrought pushed Lake
Powell to its record low (3,517.24 ft, September 2026) on Colorado water that
feeds 40 million people and 5 million acres of farmland. Sources: USGS High
Plains aquifer page and SIR 2023/5143 water-level report; NASA Earth Observatory
Texas cotton country (October 8, 2026) and Lake Powell record-low (September 18,
2026) stories; U.S. Drought Monitor analyses; UNCCD drought overview.

## Typical size and mass

- Size: Great Plains about 2,800 km long, 500–800 km wide, sloping gently east
  at about 1–2 m per km from the Rockies' foot (~1,800 m) toward the Mississippi
  (~300 m); Eurasian steppe about 8,000 km from Hungary to Manchuria in one
  nearly unbroken belt; Amazon basin about 7 million km2; West Siberian Plain
  about 2.6 million km2, among the flattest large surfaces on Earth; Sahara
  about 9 million km2. L11 frames 100–1,000 km sections —
  whole basins, dune fields, farm belts.
- Mass: a 500 x 500 km plain with 1 km of fill holds on the order of 10^17 kg
  of sediment; the Amazon foreland holds kilometers of stacked fill. Dune sand
  meters thick and marching m per year; loess tens of m thick, over 100 m in
  the thickest Chinese sections; High Plains aquifer water in pore space of the
  Ogallala and younger fill across about 174,000 square miles (USGS: almost
  112 million acres, drinking water for 2.3 million people).
- Relief: plains under 200 m variation over 100 km; basins to 100 m below sea
  level (Death Valley −86 m at Badwater, 282 ft below sea level, under more than
  11,000 ft of accumulated sediment and salts per NPS); dunes to 100 m high;
  salt flats near 200 square miles at Badwater alone, fed by a 9,000-square-mile
  drainage (NPS).

Sources: USGS pages (High Plains aquifer extent and atlas); NPS Death Valley
geology and salt-flats pages; NASA elevation pages.

## Example objects

Example: Great Plains — worn from the Rockies, farmed belt hundreds of km wide,
tallgrass in the wetter east grading to shortgrass steppe in the dry west.
Example: Eurasian steppe — the largest temperate grassland belt on Earth,
Hungary to Manchuria, the classic windswept plain. Example: Serengeti savanna —
tropical grass with scattered trees, migrating herds on seasonal rains.
Example: Amazon basin — filled from the Andes, river-threaded flat, about
7 million km2 of lowland forest on deep sediment. Example:
Sahara and Sahel — wind-built sand seas over old rock and lake beds, with the
semi-arid Sahel transition belt at the southern edge where farm meets desert.
Example: Indo-Gangetic plain — Himalaya runoff farmland for hundreds of millions
of people. Example: Death Valley and the Great Basin — stretched basins with
salt flats (Badwater: nearly 200 square miles of salt crust) and lakes that come
and go. Example: Elizabethtown frame area —
farmed plain with valleys (target image, NASA Earth Observatory record 150482).
Sources: USGS regional and aquifer pages; NPS Death Valley pages; NASA frames.

## Key numbers

- L11 frames: 100–1,000 km; Great Plains 2,800 km long; Eurasian steppe
  8,000 km belt; Sahara 9 million km2; Amazon basin 7 million km2.
- Fill: rivers lay mm per year; dunes move m per year; basins sink mm per year;
  Saharan dust to Amazon about 28 million tonnes per year (NASA CALIPSO).
- Relief: plains under 200 m per 100 km; Death Valley −86 m (Badwater, 282 ft
  below sea level); dunes to 100 m high; Badwater salt flats near 200
  square miles.
- Climate: deserts under 250 mm rain per year; plains farms 500–1,000 mm;
  savannas 500–1,500 mm seasonal.
- Water and drought: High Plains aquifer about 174,000 square miles, water table
  down 25–100 ft since 1950 across much of Lubbock County; Lake Powell record low
  3,517.24 ft (September 2026); drought touched at least 1.5 billion people in the
  decade to 2017 (UNCCD).

Sources: ladder L11 row; USGS High Plains aquifer page; NPS Death Valley pages;
NASA Earth Observatory 2026 reports; UNCCD drought overview.
