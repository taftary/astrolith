# Continents — the base of regions

This page describes continents and landmasses
at L11 scale (10^5–10^6 m): how they look from orbit, how they change through time,
how they are created, and how they interact with ranges, plains, waters, and rivers.
The bridge behind us is in `previous-l10.md`; the dive that brings you here is in
`entry.md`; the dated timeline runs through `lifetime.md`.

## How it looks

From 100 to 1,000 km up, a continent reads as a large brown-green mass against blue
seas, edged by a bright coastline. Cratons — the old cores — show as flat, pale
interiors with ancient river threads; shields expose dark rock where ice or soil
is thin. Young margins look sharper: straight coasts where breakup is fresh, jagged
where erosion has worked long. In natural color, land is brown-green, ice white,
clouds white and moving; the night side shows town lights along coasts and valleys.
The target frame below shows the whole base at once — brown-green continents
against blue seas, the pattern regions cut from.

## How it changes over time

Continents grow, break, and rejoin. New crust adds at mid-ocean ridges and arcs;
old edges wear by erosion; collisions thicken them into ranges. Over hundreds of
millions of years, landmasses gather into supercontinents, then split again as
new oceans open. The last full assembly, Pangea, gathered through the
Carboniferous (about 335–300 million years ago) from Gondwana, Laurussia, and
Siberia, held through the Triassic, and began breaking up about 225–200 million
years ago: first the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province dikes (about 200–190 Ma),
then central-Atlantic spreading (about 195–170 Ma), splitting Laurasia north from
Gondwana south; the South Atlantic and Indian oceans followed in the Cretaceous.
Earlier in the cycle came Nuna/Columbia (about 1.8–1.3 Ga), Rodinia (about
1.3 Ga–750 Ma, Grenville ranges about 1.0 Ga), and Gondwana (complete by about
530 Ma). Ice ages whiten continents in pulses; seas flood their edges when
climate warms, exposing shelves when it cools. Second pass: craton roots reach
200–250 km deep (keels to about 350 km in the thickest Archean cores); hotspots
punch through without moving the craton; Acasta 4.03 Ga and Jack Hills 4.4 Ga pin
the oldest base. A continent you see today is a frame in a slow film: the
Atlantic widens about 2.5 cm per year at the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (about 4 cm per
year total Americas–Europe/Africa separation including far-field plate motion);
Africa splits at the Rift; Australia drifts north about 7 cm per year, the
fastest continent ride today.
Live breakup runs in East Africa now: the Afar triple junction (Arabian, Nubian,
Somalian plates) rifts at about 16–20 mm per year in the Red Sea arm and about
7 mm per year in the Main Ethiopian Rift, with the 2005 35-mile Afar fissure as
the visible crack. 2024–2025 studies find a single asymmetric pulsing mantle
upwelling under Afar feeding all three arms, and magma reaching mid-crust only
weeks to months before intrusive events — plate thinning, not just the plume,
controls where volcanism localizes. Full split and a new ocean basin lie 1–10
million years out, fastest in the north.
Sources: USGS plate-tectonic and Pangea pages; NOAA ridge pages; UNESCO ridge
summary; Southampton 2021 mantle-upwelling study; Australian datum (Geoscience
Australia); Watts et al. 2025 Nature Geoscience (Afar upwelling); NASA Earth
Observatory change stories.

## How it is created

Continents are built from oceanic crust processed through subduction and volcanism
into lighter granitic rock that floats high and resists sinking. Arcs collide,
terranes attach, and cratons — stable cores, many Archean, anchoring over 60
percent of continental landmass — anchor the growth. Oldest anchors: Acasta
Gneiss, Northwest Territories, 4.03 Ga (oldest dated rock, Slave craton); Jack
Hills zircons, Western Australia, to 4.404 Ga (oldest dated earthly material,
with oxygen-isotope hints of surface water that early). Seafloor by contrast is
young — oldest surviving crust about 180 million years — because oceanic plates
recycle at subduction zones while buoyant continental crust persists. The
bimodal Earth elevation (continents high, ocean floors low) comes from this
density split — granitic continental rock near 2.7–2.8 g/cm3 vs basaltic oceanic
rock near 3.0 g/cm3 over mantle near 3.3 g/cm3 — plus sea level cutting at about
71 percent ocean cover: mean land height about 840 m, mean ocean depth about
3.7 km (3682 m modern estimate; 3.6 km in round NASA fact pages). Other rocky
bodies lack this split and stay single-peaked. Sources: USGS crust and geologic-
time pages; NASA Earth facts page; UCLA/NOAA hypsometry references.

## How it interacts with the others

A continent interacts with every other part. It pushes into mountain ranges where
it collides (`mountains.md`); it flattens into plains where it stretches or wears
(`plains.md`); it meets oceans at coasts where waves, tides, and deltas trade
sediment (`oceans.md`); it is threaded by rivers that drain it to the sea
(`rivers.md`). The atmosphere rains on it, ice caps grind it, life roots it —
and the Moon's tides tug its seas twice daily. Mostly-submerged Zealandia is the
edge case: with only islands above water, its interaction with air, ice, and
rivers runs through a small land fraction while most of its crust trades
sediment directly with the sea floor. No part stands alone: ranges rise
from continents, plains wear from ranges, coasts cut continents, rivers carry
continents to the sea. Sources: USGS interaction summaries; NASA water-cycle pages.

## Typical size and mass

- Size: continents thousands of km across; L11 sees 100–1,000 km patches — craton
  blocks, shields, peninsulas, large islands. Africa about 8,000 km north-south
  (about 30.4 million km2); Madagascar about 1,580 km long, 587,041 km2, the
  fourth-largest island; a shield block a few hundred km across. Seven-continent
  areas (Encyclopaedia Britannica / standard references): Asia about 44.6,
  Africa about 30.4, North America about 24.7 (24.2–24.7 by accounting), South
  America about 17.8, Antarctica 14.2, Europe about 10.0–10.2, Australia about
  7.7 as a country and about 8.5–8.6 as Oceania. Total land about 149 million
  km2 — about 29 percent of Earth's 510 million km2 face.
- Shields and cratons: Canadian Shield about 8 million km2, the largest exposed
  Precambrian mass (regions 2.5–4.2 Ga); Australian Shield over half the
  continent (Pilbara to 3.8 Ga, Yilgarn nucleus collapsed about 2.65–2.6 Ga);
  Pilbara craton about 250,000 km2. Cratons underlie the flat pale interiors seen
  from orbit; thin soil over scraped bedrock reads dark where ice or cover is thin.
- Mass: continental crust about 30–50 km thick (weighted global mean about
  41 km; to 70 km under ranges; 16–80 km full range), density
  about 2.7 g/cm3, floating on denser mantle. Oceanic crust only about 6–7 km
  thick. A 500 x 500 km block 40 km thick
  holds on the order of 10^19 kg of rock.
- Relief: craton interiors near sea level to a few hundred m; margins to 1,000 m
  before ranges take over.

Sources: USGS crust-thickness pages; NASA Earth facts page; Britannica continent
and shield entries; CIA World Factbook areas.

## Example objects

Example: Africa — the craton core with Rift splitting it, Sahara flat on top,
Cape ranges at the tip. Example: North America — Canadian Shield old core,
Appalachians worn range at the edge, coastal plains meeting the Atlantic. Example:
Madagascar — island micro-continent on the Somali plate (split from Africa about
165 Ma, from India about 88 Ma), with the Saririaky anorthosite massif
(Moon-like rock, about 100 km2 of late-Precambrian intrusive rock in a ductile
shear zone recording the Gondwana collision — see the `lifetime.md` target image),
sheared zones recording collision. Example: Australia —
whole-country craton drifting north about 7 cm per year, Great Dividing Range at
the edge. Eighth-continent note: Zealandia (Te Riu-a-Māui), proposed 2017 and
fully mapped to its submarine edges in 2023 (Tectonics), is about 5 million km2
and about 95 percent submerged — a continent that barely breaks the surface, so
at L11 it reads as islands (New Zealand, New Caledonia) over shallow shelves
rather than a brown-green mass. Sources:
USGS regional pages; NASA Earth Observatory frames; GNS Science Zealandia maps.

## Key numbers

- L11 span: 10^5–10^6 m; continent patches 100–1,000 km across.
- Earth land: about 29 percent of the face (about 149 of 510 million km2);
  oceans about 71 percent; mean land height about 840 m; mean ocean depth about
  3.7 km (3.6 km rounded).
- Crust: oceanic about 6–7 km thick, continental 30–50 km (mean about 41 km; to
  70 km under ranges, 16–80 km extremes); craton roots 200–250 km deep on
  average (craton-average LAB about 228 km, 2025 seismic thermography; keels to
  about 350 km in the thickest Archean cores); oldest rock 4.03 Ga (Acasta),
  oldest mineral 4.404 Ga (Jack Hills zircon).
- Drift: plates move cm per year; Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads about 2–2.5 cm per
  year (Americas–Europe/Africa separation about 4 cm per year); Australia north
  about 7 cm per year; Main Ethiopian Rift extends about 5–7 mm per year, Red
  Sea arm about 16–20 mm per year.
- Supercontinents: Nuna/Columbia about 1.8–1.3 Ga, Rodinia about 1.3 Ga–750 Ma,
  Gondwana complete by about 530 Ma, Pangea about 335–300 Ma assembly with
  breakup from about 200 Ma; next (Pangea Proxima/Ultima or Amasia) in
  about 160–250 Ma depending on model — Scotese ~250 Ma by Atlantic closure,
  2022 geodynamic models favor Pacific closure (Amasia), 2023 climate models warn
  of a hothouse interior hostile to mammals under the equatorial assembly.

Sources: ladder L11 row; USGS pages; NASA Earth facts page; NOAA ocean volume
(mean depth 3682 m); Farnsworth et al. 2023 Nature Geoscience.
