# Weather — the storms at regional scale

This page is the L11 part doc for weather systems at
100–1,000 km: hurricanes, fronts, monsoons, dust storms, and
lake snows — the scale where one frame holds a whole storm
with its surroundings. The global winds live in L10
(`clouds-weather.md`); the layered air in `atmosphere.md`;
the water in `hydrosphere.md`; the dated spine in
`lifetime.md`. Single storm cells and city skies at 1–100 km
will live in the planned L12 sky page.

From a region frame, weather has anatomy. A hurricane is a
perfect spiral with a calm eye hundreds of kilometers
across; a mid-latitude low is a comma of cloud trailing a
cold front's sharp line; monsoon rain arrives not as a
storm but as a season; dust turns day into orange night
across whole countries; lake snow falls in narrow white
bands while the sun shines a county away. Each fills an L11
view exactly — which is why this page, not L10, is their
portrait gallery.

## Hurricanes — the great spirals

Born over warm ocean water in late summer, a tropical
cyclone is heat escaping in the most organized way: air
spirals inward at the surface, rises in the eyewall's
towering ring, and flows outward high up, while the eye in
the middle sinks calm and clear. Inflow at the bottom,
outflow at the top, spin from the planet's rotation — no
spin at the equator, so none are born there. At 500–1,000
km across they are the largest storms that fit an L11
frame; from L10 they were white commas, from here they are
machines with parts: eye, eyewall, rainbands, outflow shield.

The same machine has three names — hurricane (Atlantic,
northeast Pacific), typhoon (northwest Pacific), cyclone
(south Pacific, Indian Ocean) — and the Pacific's strongest
earn "super typhoon" past 150 mph. Winds are graded 1–5 on
the Saffir-Simpson scale, a pure wind scale: damage roughly
quadruples per category, and Category 3+ counts as major:

| Cat | Winds | Damage |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 74–95 mph (119–153 km/h) | Roof, siding, branches; outages days |
| 2 | 96–110 mph (154–177 km/h) | Major roofs, trees down; outages weeks |
| 3 | 111–129 mph (178–208 km/h) | Homes damaged, roads blocked; weeks |
| 4 | 130–156 mph (209–251 km/h) | Roofs gone, areas isolated; months |
| 5 | 157+ mph (252+ km/h) | Homes destroyed; uninhabitable months |

Wind is only a third of the killing. Storm surge — the sea
mounded by wind and low pressure — drowns coasts regardless
of category: Ike landed Texas as Category 2 in 2008 with
125-mile-wide winds and 20-ft surge, while tiny Category-4
Charley in 2004 managed only 7 ft. Rain flooding reaches
hundreds of kilometers inland, and tornadoes spin out of
the rainbands. Forecasters warn surge and rain separately
from the number — the number is wind alone.

Sources: NHC Saffir-Simpson scale pages; NWS hurricane
pages.

## Fronts — the middle-latitude machine

Outside the tropics, weather is made at boundaries. Cold
dense air wedges under warm air along a cold front's sharp
line — squalls, then clearing; warm air slides over cold
along a warm front's long slope — days of grey drizzle.
Between them a low-pressure comma 1,000–3,000 km across
marches with the jet (see L10 `clouds-weather.md`), and most
of a temperate year's weather is one of these passing over.
Nor'easters are the Atlantic's winter version, wrapped
around a low off New England; atmospheric rivers are
firehose vapor streams — one can carry more water than the
Amazon's mouth — slamming the Pacific coast all winter.
The close-up of a single front belongs at L12; the marching
lows are here.

Sources: NOAA JetStream air-mass and front pages.

## Monsoon — the breathing continent

The Hadley cell breathes in and out with the tilt (see L10
`clouds-weather.md`), and where ocean meets heated land the
breath becomes a season. South Asia's summer monsoon:
southwest winds pile moisture against the Himalayas and the
Western Ghats for four months, watering crops for more than
a billion people — and failing, some years, into drought
and famine. West Africa's monsoon greens the Sahel; the
North American monsoon bursts over the desert southwest;
Australia's reverses with the southern summer. Winter
reverses every flow: dry continental air pours seaward and
the rains move south. From an L11 frame the monsoon is not
a spiral but a grey lid with daily tower eruptions along
the mountain walls (`mountains.md:89-96`).

## Dust — the land in the air

Where sinking air and bare ground meet, wind lifts the
ground itself. Saharan dust crosses the Atlantic yearly to
fertilize the Amazon (see L10 `atmosphere.md`); Asian dust
from the Gobi and Taklamakan hazes the Pacific; haboob
walls roll off thunderstorm outflows across Sudan and
Arizona. Dust feeds and chokes: phosphorus for rainforests,
silica for lungs, salt from the Aralkum for failing fields
(see `lakes.md`). On Mars the same physics scales past all
Earthly limits into planet-swallowing veils (see L10
`clouds-weather.md`).

## Lake snow — winter's narrow bands

Cold air crossing warmer open water makes its own weather
downwind. Arctic air spilling over the still-unfrozen Great
Lakes picks up heat and vapor, rises in streets and bands,
and dumps snow on the lee shores — Buffalo's meters-deep
days — while sun shines a county away. The recipe: a
surface-to-850-mb temperature gap of at least ~13°C, long
fetch over open water, and wind aligned with the lake's
axis for single intense bands (3+ inches per hour) versus
multiple weaker rows. The season runs roughly November to
February, ending when ice caps the lake and cuts the fuel;
the same physics snows the Caspian, Black, and Japan seas.
Fog, the stratus that touches ground, and the city heat
island steering its own storms wait at L12.

Sources: NOAA NESDIS and NWS lake-effect pages; NOAA
repository lake-effect studies.

## How regional weather changes over time

Storm tracks wander with the jets, oceans flip their
decadal moods (El Nino/La Nina move the whole tropical
pattern; see L10 `clouds-weather.md`), and monsoons fail in
clusters across centuries — the droughts behind fallen
kingdoms. Humans now load the dice: warmer oceans feed
heavier hurricane rain, hotter air wrings harder downpours
from the same fronts, and the ice whose loss ends lake
season early shrinks each decade (see L10 `surface.md` and
`atmosphere.md`). The patterns keep their shapes; the
extremes grow teeth.

## How it interacts with the others

Mountains make rain and deserts (`mountains.md`): forced
ascent on one side, shadow on the other, monsoon walls
along the Himalayas. Plains dry or drown (`plains.md`):
dust source and flood receptor, aquifers recharged by the
same storms that flatten crops. Coasts take the surge
(`oceans.md`): surge, surf, and breached barriers — bays
funnel what the open beach spreads. Rivers carry the
aftermath (`rivers.md`): inland flood crests days after
landfall, deltas rebuilt by cyclone mud. Lakes seed their
own snow (`lakes.md`): bands downwind until ice shuts them
off. Fire and shaking share the season (`volcanoes.md`,
`earthquakes.md`): ash reroutes jets for a year, quakes
trigger the tsunamis hurricanes resemble. The global
ledgers — tilt, shield, Moon, engine — run the machine
from L10.

## Key numbers

- Cyclones: 500–1,000 km; eye tens of km; Cat 1 74–95 mph
to Cat 5 157+; damage ~4x per category; surge kills beyond
the number (Ike Cat-2 landfall, 20-ft surge).
- Fronts: lows 1,000–3,000 km; rivers-in-the-sky rivaling
great rivers.
- Monsoon: four wet months; waters crops for 1B+ people;
reverses with the tilt.
- Dust: trans-Atlantic yearly; haboobs from storm outflow;
Mars veils for months.
- Lake snow: needs ~13°C lake-air gap; bands to 3+/hr;
range ~25–100 miles downwind; season ends with ice.
- L11 frame: 100–1,000 km — one hurricane, one low, one
monsoon wall, one dust veil, one snow band system per view.

Sources: NHC, NWS, and NOAA pages named above.

## Example objects

A major hurricane is the tropical standard: eye, eyewall,
surge beyond its number — the machine complete.

A nor'easter is the temperate standard: comma low, sharp
front, days of grey — the week's weather in one frame.

The South Asian monsoon is the seasonal standard: four
months of towers on mountain walls, a billion fed or
failed.

A Saharan outbreak is the dust standard: ground crossing
an ocean to feed a forest.

Buffalo snow is the lake standard: meters from one lake's
breath, sun a county away.

Sources: NHC, NWS, NOAA, and NASA pages named above.
