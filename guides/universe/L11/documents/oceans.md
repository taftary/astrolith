# Oceans — the blue of regions

This page describes oceans, seas,
coasts, and islands at regional scale: looks, changes, creation, and interactions
with land, ranges, plains, and rivers. Bridge in `previous-l10.md`, dive in
`entry.md`, timeline in `lifetime.md`.

## How it looks

From 100-km scale the sea reads as flat blue with brown-green swirls where rivers
bring mud and life. Coasts draw a sharp line — sandy beaches pale, rocky cliffs dark
— with islands as small green dots offshore. Island arcs string volcanoes along
trenches (Aleutians, Marianas); coral atolls ring lagoons where reefs kept pace
with sinking volcanoes; barrier islands and spits shift with every storm. Bays trap
brown water (the target frame: Rupert Bay brown with sediment and plant matter);
open seas run deep blue. Ice rims
white in winter — and the white is shrinking: Arctic sea ice hit its 2026 minimum
on September 12 at 4.6 million km2 (tied 10th-lowest), the March 2026 maximum
tied 2025 for the lowest winter extent, and every summer since 2007 ranks among
the 20 lowest (record: 2012); what remains is younger, thinner first-year ice.
Ships and wakes thread the blue at full zoom.

## How it changes over time

Seas rise and fall, open and close. Ice ages lock water on land and drop seas about
120 m; warming melts and floods coasts. The satellite record (TOPEX/Poseidon
1992 onward) shows the rise more than doubling: about 10 cm added since 1993,
a long-term expectation near 4.3–4.4 mm per year, with year-to-year swings —
2024 ran hot at 5.9 mm (thermal expansion in the hottest year on record),
while mild La Nina in 2025 held the rise to 0.8 mm as extra rain ponded over
the Amazon basin. Local shores feel more or less than the global mean
(subsidence, currents, rebound from Ice Age ice). Rivers build deltas outward m per year;
waves cut cliffs back cm per year. New oceans open as continents split (Atlantic
still widening); old ones close as arcs collide. Watching it all is the
NASA–CNES SWOT satellite (launched December 2022): its Ka-band radar
interferometer maps sea-surface height to fractions of a cm, resolving eddies
down to about 20 km and — with PACE ocean-color data since 2024 — tying swirls
directly to plankton blooms; 2025 analyses used it to map Arctic surface
circulation at 2-km detail. A coast you see is a moment:
beaches move seasonally, deltas switch lobes decadally, sea level climbs mm per
year today. Second pass: Dungey cycle drives storms; Gannon May 2024 storm shows
space weather reaching coasts via auroras; SAA weakens shield regionally. The
water itself keeps score of warming: the oceans hold about 90 percent of the
excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases, and upper-ocean heat content set new
records in both 2024 and 2025 — nine record years in a row, with 2025 adding
about 23 zettajoules. 2024 was the warmest year in NOAA's 1850–2024 record on
land and sea; 2025 ranked third, while Arctic sea ice logged its second-lowest
annual extent (lowest March maximum in the 47-year record). The heat bit reefs
hardest: the fourth global coral-bleaching event (April 2024 to mid-2025)
stressed about 84 percent of reef area in 83 countries, including the Great
Barrier Reef's fifth and sixth mass bleachings since 2016 — the 2024 one the
largest footprint ever recorded there, cutting coral cover sharply from its
recent highs. Sources: NOAA sea-level pages; USGS coastal pages; NOAA NCEI
global-climate assessments 2024–2025; NOAA Coral Reef Watch.

## How it is created

Oceans fill low crust: heavy oceanic crust sits about 5 km below continents, so
water gathers there. Seas open by rifting (Red Sea today, Atlantic 200 million
years ago); coasts shape by waves, tides, and rivers; islands build by volcanoes,
coral, or drowning ridges. New floor is paved at the seams: the mid-ocean ridge
system — nearly 65,000 km of underwater volcanoes, the longest mountain range
on Earth — splits plates apart and freezes basalt into fresh crust (Mid-Atlantic
Ridge spreading 2–5 cm per year with a Grand-Canyon-scale rift valley; East
Pacific Rise 6–16 cm per year and smooth). The Atlantic still widens about
2.5 cm per year at its ridge (about 4 cm per year of total Americas–Europe/Africa
separation); the Pacific shrinks as its rim subducts faster than its rise can
pave. Oldest surviving seafloor is only about 180–200 million years old — the
rest has dived back into trenches — while continental rock persists for billions.
The deepest cuts form the opposite way, by
subduction: where one plate dives under another, the sea floor bends into a
trench — the Mariana Trench, a 2,550-km crescent east of the Philippines
(about 69 km wide on average), holds Challenger Deep, the deepest known
point on Earth's face. The 71-percent ocean cover comes from water volume vs
bimodal heights — fill to sea level and continents stand out. Sources: NOAA ocean
basin pages; USGS coastal formation pages; NOAA NCEI Mariana Trench record.

## How it interacts with the others

The sea interacts with all land parts. It cuts continents (`continents.md`) into
cliffs and beaches; it takes sediment from rivers (`rivers.md`) to build deltas;
it floods plains (`plains.md`) into marshes; it is steered by ranges
(`mountains.md`) that make rain and wind. Air evaporates it to rain; ice locks it;
life blooms where rivers feed it. Example: Rupert Bay brown water shows river meets
sea — sediment plus plant matter mixing at the coast. Coasts trade with seas daily
by tides the Moon pulls. Currents keep the whole body moving: wind-driven surface
gyres turn clockwise in the northern basins and counterclockwise in the southern
ones, western boundary currents (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio) run fast and warm along
continental edges, and cold dense water sinks near the poles to creep along the
deep floor for centuries (thermohaline circulation, with the Atlantic overturning
watched closely in 2024–2026 studies). The Antarctic Circumpolar Current — the
only current that rings the planet unbroken, about 173 Sverdrups (million m3
per second) — links all three major basins through the Southern Ocean. Sources: NOAA interaction pages; NASA water-cycle pages;
NOAA Ocean Exploration currents explainer; NOAA AOML Atlantic overturning and
ACC pages.

## Typical size and mass

- One global ocean in five basins: the single world ocean (71 percent of
  Earth's face) is divided by convention into the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian,
  Southern, and Arctic basins — Pacific largest, then Atlantic, Indian,
  Southern, Arctic smallest. The Pacific alone covers more than 155 million
  km2 (over 30 percent of Earth's surface, larger than all land combined),
  holds more than half of the free water, averages about 4,000 m deep, and
  sits on the oldest surviving seafloor (about 200 million years); all of the
  continents could fit inside it.
- Basin areas and mean depths (NOAA NCEI ETOPO1 volumes, Southern Ocean
  south of 60 S): Pacific about 161.8 million km2 at 4,080 m mean;
  Atlantic about 85.1 million km2 at 3,646 m; Indian about 70.6 million km2
  at 3,741 m; Southern about 22.0 million km2 at 3,270 m; Arctic about
  15.6 million km2 at 1,205 m. Whole-ocean totals: about 361.9 million km2
  of sea, about 1.335 billion km3 of water (about 97 percent of Earth's
  water), mean depth 3,682–3,688 m (3.6 km rounded on NASA fact pages).
- Size: oceans thousands of km; L11 sees 100–1,000 km sections — bays, seas,
  straits, island chains. Rupert Bay tens of km across; Mediterranean 3,800 km long;
  Great Barrier Reef 2,300 km of islands and reefs.
- Mass: mean ocean depth about 3.6 km; a 500 x 500 km sea block 1 km deep holds
  on the order of 10^17 kg of water. Tides move m vertically, km horizontally.
- Relief: sea level flat to cm; waves m high (Storm Eddie, December 2024,
  threw open-ocean swells near 20 m that satellites tracked over 24,000 km
  to the tropical Atlantic); tides to about 16 m (Bay of Fundy, the world's
  highest, at Burntcoat Head, Nova Scotia — funnel-shaped, resonant bays
  amplify the Moon-and-Sun pull);
  coasts from flat beaches to 100-m cliffs.

Sources: NOAA depth pages; NASA Earth facts page.

## Example objects

Example: Rupert Bay (target image) — river sediment and tea-colored dissolved
organic matter from boreal forests and bogs browning the southern James Bay,
stirred with suspended sediment over postglacial-rebound mudflats; the Moose,
Nottaway, and Harricanaw rivers feed the brown swirls (Landsat 9, September 27,
2026, NASA Earth Observatory October 6, 2026). The Cree call the hue
pahtaaunaakun — "the color of singed beaver": humic-stained river water (CDOM
absorbing blue and ultraviolet) crossing the shallow flats of Hannah and Rupert
bays — the southernmost arms of James Bay — past Charlton Island and little
Stag Rock, while tides stir pale plumes of mud off the rebound-lifted flats.
Example: Mediterranean — nearly closed sea with straits and
islands. Example: Great Barrier Reef — reef islands along a coast (2,300 km of
reefs; fifth mass bleaching 2024 with the largest footprint on record, sixth in
2025 back-to-back; hard coral cover fell steeply from 2024 highs though most
regions stayed near long-term averages; the fourth global event likely ended
mid-2025). Example: Bay of
Fundy — extreme tides from shape. Sources: NOAA regional pages; NASA frames;
AIMS long-term monitoring 2024–2025; NOAA Coral Reef Watch.

## Key numbers

- L11 frames: 100–1,000 km; bays tens of km; seas hundreds to thousands of km.
- Ocean: 71 percent of Earth; mean depth 3.6 km (3,682 m modern estimate;
  2010 NOAA–Woods Hole satellite figure; only about 26 percent of the sea
  floor mapped in high resolution as of June 2024, so the mean stays an
  estimate); deepest 10,935 m at Challenger Deep, southern Mariana Trench
  southwest of Guam (NOAA standard; a 2010 multibeam survey read 10,994 m
  ± 40 m — treat the last tens of m as survey uncertainty). Other basins'
  deepest points (2018–2019 Five Deeps high-resolution surveys): Atlantic —
  Brownson Deep, Puerto Rico Trench, 8,378 m ± 5 m; Southern Ocean —
  Factorian Deep, South Sandwich Trench south of 60 S, 7,434 m ± 13 m
  (the trench's Atlantic-side maximum reaches about 8,266 m). The trench is
  named for HMS Challenger, which first sounded it in 1875.
- Sea level: ice-age low −120 m (about −120 to −134 m at the Last Glacial
  Maximum, 26–19 ka); satellite era +10 cm since 1993, rate more than doubled,
  long-term about 4.3–4.4 mm per year with 5.9 mm in 2024 and 0.8 mm in 2025
  (La Nina); tides to about 16 m (Bay of Fundy).
- Rivers to sea: Amazon pours about 209,000 m3 per second (about 15 percent of
  all river water reaching the oceans, some 6,000 km3 per year, with about
  a billion tonnes of sediment per year riding the plume northwest along the
  Guianas coast); sediment builds deltas m per year.
- Currents: surface gyres turn with the winds (clockwise north, counterclockwise
  south); Gulf Stream and Kuroshio carry warm water poleward along western basin
  edges; deep water returns along the floor over centuries; Antarctic Circumpolar
  Current about 173 Sv, the strongest on Earth.

Sources: ladder L11 row; NOAA; NASA.
