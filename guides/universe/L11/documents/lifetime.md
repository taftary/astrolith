# Lifetime — dated stages of regions

This page runs the dated timeline relevant to L11 regions like ours: from
crust and first continents through plate-tectonic onset, supercontinents,
mountain building, erosion, ice, and seas to today, plus the far-future fate.
Consistent with [continents](./continents.md), [mountains](./mountains.md),
[plains](./plains.md), [oceans](./oceans.md), [rivers](./rivers.md); bridge in
[previous-l10](./previous-l10.md), dive in [entry](./entry.md).

Reading guide: each stage names its part doc — crust to continents, plates to
continents and mountains, gatherings to continents, collisions to mountains,
wearing to plains, seas to oceans, threads to rivers. Cross-check: no stage
contradicts part numbers; dates carry sources.

## Early crust and first continents (4.51–3.0 billion years ago)

Earth's story opens with the Moon-forming impact about 4.51–4.47 Ga, roughly
60 million years after Solar System condensation at 4.567 Ga, when a Mars-sized
body (Theia) struck the proto-Earth, melted both bodies, and flung debris that
became the Moon [S-MOON]. The newborn Earth cooled from a magma ocean; the
Moon's own anorthosite crust floated out of its magma ocean around 4.4 Ga. On
Earth, Jack Hills zircons in Western Australia pin the oldest surviving crust:
a concordant grain at 4404 +/- 8 Ma, with populations at 4.3–4.0 Ga, carrying
high oxygen-isotope values and granitic chemistry that imply evolved melts from
supracrustal rock weathered under liquid water — the cool-early-Earth signal
for oceans before 4.3 Ga [S-JACK]. By 4.03 Ga, Acasta Gneiss in the Slave
craton records the oldest dated rock; by 4.0–3.5 Ga, small protocontinents
floated; by 3.0 Ga, cratons stabilized (Canadian Shield cores, Pilbara to
3.8 Ga, Yilgarn nucleus). Oceans already covered most of the face; the impact
set spin, tilt, and tides that still widen the Moon's orbit about 3.8 cm per
year. See [continents](./continents.md) (cratons), [oceans](./oceans.md)
(early seas). Sources: NASA Moon-formation pages; Wilde et al. 2001 Nature;
Valley et al. 2014 Nature Geoscience; USGS deep-time pages.

## When plates began to move (4.4–2.5 billion years ago)

Plate tectonics did not switch on at once; it emerged stepwise, and the start
date is still debated from Hadean (>4.0 Ga) to Neoproterozoic (<1.0 Ga)
depending on what counts as proof [S-PLATE]. The working picture used here:

- Hadean episodic recycling: 2025 Barberton komatiite melt-inclusion Sr–Nb–U
  data imply 40–70 percent of present continental-crust mass was already
  extracted and recycled by late Hadean, best fit by plume-triggered episodic
  subduction — short-lived sinking of whole-lid pieces, not yet stable modern
  subduction [S-BARB].
- Mesoarchean transition: crust chemistry shifts about 3.3–3.0 Ga (TTG to
  sanukitoid to potassic granite; arc-like basalts increase), dome-and-keel
  vertical tectonics gives way to linear thrust belts, and passive-margin and
  foreland-basin sediments appear — the gestational 3.2–2.5 Ga change from
  stagnant/squishy lid to sustained plates [S-ARCH].
- Neoarchean mobilism confirmed: 2024 Wyoming–Superior paleomagnetism shows
  about 3,000 km of relative motion at 4–5 cm per year during 2.71–2.48 Ga
  assembly, the oldest robust two-craton drift record; 2025 Yilgarn shear-zone
  metamorphism shows about 10 km burial–exhumation at Phanerozoic rates,
  implying rigid blocks by late Archean [S-PALEO].
- Modern-style cold subduction (blueschists, ultrahigh-pressure rocks) only
  becomes widespread after about 0.8 Ga, so Archean recycling was hotter and
  more episodic than today.

Assumption: this page treats sustained global plates as established by about
2.5 Ga, with localized subduction before that. That choice lets supercontinent
cycles below start at Kenorland without claiming modern plates at 4.4 Ga.
See [continents](./continents.md) (growth), [mountains](./mountains.md)
(orogenies).

## Supercontinent cycles (2.7 billion–200 million years ago)

Land gathers and splits on a roughly 300–500-million-year beat once sustained
plates are running. The earliest candidate, Kenorland (~2.7 Ga, Kenoran
orogeny), is debated — some reconstructions split early land into separate
supercratons (Superia, Sclavia, Vaalbara) rather than one global lid [S-SUPER].
Firm cycles then run: Nuna/Columbia assembled about 1.9–1.75 Ga, tenure to
about 1.6–1.46 Ga in 2024 full-plate models, breakup 1.5–1.2 Ga; Rodinia
assembled about 1.3–1.0 Ga (Grenville ranges about 1.0 Ga), breakup in stages
about 0.8–0.52 Ga; Gondwana complete by about 530 Ma; Pangea assembled about
335–300 Ma (Carboniferous collision of Gondwana, Laurussia, Siberia), held
through the Triassic, and broke up from about 201.5 Ma when Central Atlantic
Magmatic Province dikes and sills (peak 201.6–201.2 Ma U–Pb) opened the central
Atlantic — the same pulse linked to the end-Triassic extinction [S-CAMP].
Each assembly builds ranges (Grenville 1.0 Ga, Appalachian ancestors 400–300
Ma); each breakup opens seas (Atlantic spreading from about 200–185 Ma south
to north). Between, plains fill and wear. The Saririaky anorthosite massif in
southern Madagascar pins Gondwana assembly in rock: astronaut photo
ISS075-E-85249 (Expedition 75, Nikon Z9 400 mm, 28 August 2026; story
5 October 2026) shows a bright late-Precambrian intrusive lens about 100 km2,
at least 600 Ma old, in a ductile shear zone with north-south lineation where
Africa, India, Madagascar, Australia, and Antarctica collided; a second massif
about 60 km north may be its boudinaged pair [S-MADA]. Lunar anorthosites top
4.0 Ga from the Moon's magma ocean make it a Moon analog, with Beartooth
Montana the closest compositional match. See
[mountains](./mountains.md) (orogenies), [plains](./plains.md) (fills).
Sources: NASA Earth Observatory Moon-like Madagascar frame; Ashwal et al.
1998; Martelat et al. 2000.

## Mountain building and wearing (1.0 billion–50 million years ago)

Ranges rise where plates meet and wear where rivers and ice cut. Dated
backbone, oldest to youngest:

- Grenville (1.3–1.0 Ga, peak 1.05–1.01 Ga granulites): Rodinia assembly
  suture, basement under most Appalachians.
- Appalachian pulses: Taconic 470–440 Ma (accretionary wedge, Blue Ridge
  Barrovian core 450 Ma), Acadian/Neoacadian 395–340 Ma (Catskill delta),
  Alleghanian 335–260 Ma (Pangea collision, fold-thrust belt) — once
  Himalaya-high, now 1,000–2,037 m ridges.
- Atlantic opening 230–170 Ma (rift basins, CAMP 201.5 Ma, spreading
  propagating south-to-north) splits Pangea; Alps start about 100 Ma.
- Himalayas from 50–40 Ma as India (north at 9–20 cm per year Cretaceous,
  slowing by half at contact) hits Asia, still rising over 1 cm per year with
  Tibetan crust 50–70 km thick; modern steady state since about 40–25 Ma in
  the northwest. Andes build as ocean dives under South America. Debate note:
  some models place first India–Asia contact at 65–59 Ma or hard collision at
  25–20 Ma; this page uses the canonical 50–40 Ma rapid-uplift onset.

Ranges shed to plains; rivers thread valleys; coasts take sediment. See
[mountains](./mountains.md), [rivers](./rivers.md). Sources: USGS Himalaya and
Piedmont-Blue Ridge pages; Ding et al. Tibetan uplift review.

## Ice, seas, and modern regions (34 million years ago–today)

Antarctica ices at the Eocene–Oligocene transition about 34 Ma: stepwise
growth from mountain glaciers (precursor 34.4 Ma) to a continental marine-
calving sheet by about 32.8 Ma once CO2 fell below about 600 ppm, dropping
seas about 70–80 m and reorganizing ocean circulation [S-EOT]. Northern ice
pulses follow from 2.6 Ma, with the last peak 26–19 ka (LGM seas about
120–134 m below present by Barbados-coral and far-field stacks; rapid
deglaciation 16.5–8.2 ka at about 12 m per thousand years, Meltwater Pulse 1A
about 14.5–14.0 ka) [S-LGM]. Ice carves valleys, drops till to plains, dams
lakes that burst. Seas flood to today (about +120 m from the ice low); deltas
build (Nile, Amazon fans); deserts pulse (African Humid Period about 11.5–5.5 ka,
peak 9–6 ka, Green Sahara to 31 N with an 8 ka pause and abrupt 5.8–4.8 ka
termination; 2026 Lake Yoa varves add decadal droughts at about 9.3 and
8.2 ka [S-AHP]). Climate spikes punctuate the run: faint young Sun offset by greenhouse,
Great Oxidation, Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum, and Holocene stability
with no global oscillation over about 15–20 cm for 6–0.15 ka before modern
rise. Today seas rise about 3–4 mm per year (satellite era); towns line rivers
and coasts; Lake Powell (Glen Canyon Dam 1963) hit record-low 3,517 ft in
September 2026 after the 2025–2026 snow drought. Today's regions — Himalaya
sharp, Appalachians worn, Great Plains farmed, Rupert Bay brown with rivers —
are one frame. No contradictions with part docs. See all part docs. Sources:
NOAA sea-level and NCEI coral stacks; USGS ice pages; NASA drought frames
(2026).

## Far-future fate

The fate ahead is drift plus Sun, on two very different clocks. On the
tectonic clock (tens to hundreds of millions of years), plates keep moving:
Atlantic widens about 2.5 cm per year, Africa splits at the Rift (Red Sea arm
16–20 mm per year, Main Ethiopian Rift about 7 mm per year, Afar pulsing
upwelling 2025), Australia moves north about 7 cm per year. Four published
next-supercontinent paths span 200–300 Ma: Pangea Proxima/Ultima by Atlantic
closure (Scotese, introversion), Amasia by Pacific closure (2022 geodynamic
models favor this as oceanic lithosphere weakens), Aurica (both oceans), and
Novopangea — all assembled in GPlates reconstructions [S-NEXT]. Erosion wears
all ranges flat in tens of millions of years unless rebuilt. The 2023
Farnsworth et al. climate run for an equatorial Pangea Ultima warns of a
hothouse interior (means to about 28 C, large areas over 40 C) hostile to
mammals. On the solar clock, the Sun brightens about 1 percent per 110 million
years: in about 600 Ma CO2 starvation stresses C3 plants (2024 work extends
survival to 1.6–1.86 Ga by overheating instead), in about 1.0–1.5 Ga a moist
greenhouse evaporates oceans and plates slow, regions bake to desert then lose
air; in about 5–7.6 Ga the Sun swells and Earth's face melts. Regions end when
oceans leave. See fate note in every part doc. Sources: Scotese PALEOMAP;
Huang et al. 2022 National Science Review; Farnsworth et al. 2023 Nature
Geoscience; Wolf and Toon; NASA Sun-fate pages.

## Key numbers (timeline)

- 4.567 Ga Solar System; 4.51–4.47 Ga Moon-forming impact; 4.404 Ga oldest
  zircons; 4.03 Ga Acasta rock; 4.0–3.0 Ga first cratons; plates sustained by
  about 2.5 Ga.
- 2.7 Ga Kenorland debated; 1.9–1.75 Ga Nuna assembly; 1.3–1.0 Ga Rodinia and
  Grenville ranges; 530 Ma Gondwana complete; 335–300 Ma Pangea; 201.5 Ma CAMP
  breakup.
- 230–170 Ma Atlantic rift-to-drift; 50–40 Ma Himalayas start; 34 Ma Antarctica
  ices (continental sheet 32.8 Ma); 2.6 Ma northern ice; 26–19 ka LGM (−120 to
  −134 m); deglaciation 16.5–8.2 ka; today about 3.3 mm per year mean rise
  (4.5 in 2023); Powell 3,517.24 ft record low 10 Sept 2026.
- Fate: 200–300 Ma next supercontinent (Proxima/Amasia/Aurica/Novopangea);
  about 0.6 Ga plant CO2 stress; about 1.0–1.5 Ga moist-greenhouse oceans lost;
  about 5–7.6 Ga Sun swells.

Sources: USGS This Dynamic Earth; NOAA NCEI coral stacks; NASA sea-level
portal (Hamlington et al. 2024; NASA 2025–2026 analyses).
