# Earthquakes — the belts that shake

This page is the L11 part doc for earthquake country: fault
zones, seismic belts, and tsunami coasts at 100–1,000 km —
the scale where one frame holds a whole rupture system. The
global mechanics live in L10 (`tectonics.md` for plates,
`hydrosphere.md` for the sea that tsunamis cross); the fire
on the same boundaries lives in `volcanoes.md`; the dated
spine runs through `lifetime.md`. Single ruptures and fault
scarps at 1–100 km will live in the planned L12 pages.

From a region frame, earthquake country reads as lines and
belts: a sharp valley slicing 1,300 km through mountains and
cities, a coastline with drowned forests standing dead in
tidal mud, a trench offshore with an arc of volcanoes
inland, a Himalayan front where the range meets the plain in
one thrust. The dots of decades of seismometers trace every
line — shallow quakes along the faults, a sloping curtain of
deeper ones diving under arcs and continents.

## The three belts

Almost all quakes live in three neighborhoods that ring the
planet:

**The Ring of Fire — the subduction belt.** Three quarters
of the world's great quakes circle the Pacific where ocean
plates dive: Chile, Alaska, Kamchatka, Japan, Indonesia, the
Andes front. Every M9 ever recorded — Chile 1960, Alaska
1964, Sumatra 2004, Tohoku 2011 (see L10 `tectonics.md`) —
ruptured here. Volcanoes stand on the same line (see
`volcanoes.md`); quakes and eruptions are two voices of one
diving slab.

**The Alpide belt — the collision belt.** From the Azores
through the Mediterranean, Türkiye, Iran, the Himalayas, and
Southeast Asia, continents crumple instead of diving. Quakes
are shallower, more scattered, and crueler to cities built
on soft basins: the February 2023 Türkiye–Syria doublet on
the East Anatolian Fault killed tens of thousands across a
rupture hundreds of kilometers long; the 2015 Gorkha quake
(M7.8) shook Kathmandu from a thrust under the Himalayan
front. No ocean swallows the strain here, so it piles into
folds, thrusts, and strike-slip faults across whole
countries.

**The ridge belt — the spreading murmur.** Mid-ocean ridges
and continental rifts hum constantly with small shallow
quakes as new crust cracks open — Iceland's swarm-filled
weeks, Afar's tearing episodes (see L10 `tectonics.md`).
Rarely deadly, always busy: the sound of the planet
resurfacing.

Beyond the belts, old scars still snap. The 1811–12 New
Madrid sequence in the Mississippi Valley — far from any
plate edge — rang church bells in Boston and briefly ran the
river backward over sand blows; ancient failed rifts buried
under plains can stay dangerous for hundreds of millions of
years. Intraplate quakes are few but blind: no mapped front
warns the cities above.

Sources: USGS earthquake-hazards and subduction-zone pages;
USGS Cascadia pages.

## Anatomy of a fault zone at regional scale

A great fault is never one crack but a system hundreds of
kilometers long and tens wide. The San Andreas stretches
about 1,300 km through two thirds of California, the
Pacific plate grinding past North America at ~5 cm/year
averaged over 10 million years — 18–35 mm/year by segment
today, the rest shared with sister faults (San Jacinto at
~14 mm/year). Its last great southern rupture, Fort Tejon
1857 at M7.9, predates Los Angeles as a metropolis; the
system can make about M8.2, and every city in the state
sits within its family of faults (see L10 `tectonics.md`).

Subduction zones stack three quake kinds in one frame, and
Cascadia — 1,130 km from Cape Mendocino to Vancouver
Island — is the textbook. Shallow crustal quakes crack the
overriding plate (Seattle Fault, 923 CE); deep intraslab
quakes break inside the diving Juan de Fuca slab (Olympia
1949 M6.8, Seattle 1965 M6.5, Nisqually 2001 M6.8); and the
megathrust interface itself, stuck for centuries, lets go in
magnitude 8–9 ruptures — at least 19 in the last 10,000
years, most recently January 1700, read in drowned ghost
forests whose rings stop at the winter of 1699–1700 and in
an orphan tsunami recorded across the Pacific in Japan.
Odds of a full-margin ~M9 in the next 50 years run 10–15%.
Below the locked zone, slow slip and tremor creep in weeks-
long silent episodes — the fault talking without shaking.

Sources: USGS Cascadia fact sheet (FS 2025-3050) and
subduction-zone pages; USGS San Andreas pages; Pacific
Northwest Seismic Network Cascadia pages.

## Depth — the sloping curtain

Plot quakes by depth across any subduction frame and a
curtain appears: shallow shocks at the trench, deepening
inland along the diving slab to ~700 km — the Wadati-
Benioff zone, the slab's glowing outline in seismic ink.
Deep quakes cannot make tsunamis (nothing moves the
seafloor) but shake wide areas gently; the 2013 Sea of
Okhotsk M8.3 at ~600 km was felt from Moscow to Tokyo.
Shallow megathrust quakes do the opposite: one side of the
fault lurches meters over hundreds of kilometers, shoving
the ocean above (see L10 `tectonics.md` and
`hydrosphere.md`).

Where slabs collide with continents instead of diving, the
curtain crumples into thrust stacks: the Himalayan front
rides one great ramp — the Main Frontal Thrust system —
that has hosted M8+ quakes for centuries and will again;
each one lifts the range millimeters and drops valleys,
building the mountains of `mountains.md` in jerks.

## Tsunami coasts — where the sea answers

A coast above a locked megathrust lives a double life:
centuries of quiet growth, then subsidence, flooding, and a
wave train in a single morning. 2004 Sumatra (280,000+
dead), 2011 Tohoku (15,000+ dead, 130,000 displaced), 1964
Alaska (landslide waves killing most) are the modern
types (see L10 `tectonics.md`). The 1700 Cascadia wave
crossed to Japan with no felt quake there — the orphan that
dated Cascadia's last rupture. Warning today is minutes for
near coasts (shake = go uphill) and hours for far ones via
ocean buoys; the close-up of bays, run-up, and seawalls
waits at L12.

Secondary effects finish what shaking starts: 1964
Anchorage slid on liquefied clay, ports burned, harbors
emptied — ground failure, fire, and waves each with their
own geography inside the shaken region.

Sources: USGS tsunami and 1964 Alaska pages; NOAA tsunami
program pages.

## How quake country changes over time

Strain builds at centimeters per year and releases in
seconds, so a fault zone alternates long silence with
violent revision. Cascadia's 19 great ruptures in 10,000
years average centuries apart but arrive irregularly;
Cascadia-quiet coasts grow forests on marshes the last wave
buried. Cities rewrite the risk between events: Anchorage
rebuilt on mapped landslides, San Francisco on 1906's
lessons, Tokyo on 1923's — each quake a survey of the next
one's damage. Over millions of years the belts migrate with
the plates: today's quiet interior was once a rift, today's
trench will one day be a suture inside a supercontinent
(see `continents.md` and `lifetime.md`).

## How it interacts with the others

The base decides where belts run (`continents.md`): old
rifts hide under plains and rupture blind; Afar's split
will be a subduction-free shore in millions of years. The
lift is built in jerks (`mountains.md`): Himalayas and
Andes rise on the same thrusts that shake them. The flat
amplifies (`plains.md`): soft basins shake longer and
liquefy — Mexico City on a drained lakebed, Kathmandu in
its bowl. The blue transmits (`oceans.md`): trenches offshore,
waves across whole basins, bays funneling run-up. The flow
reroutes (`rivers.md`): New Madrid changed the Mississippi's
course; landslide dams burst into downstream floods.

Fire shares every boundary (`volcanoes.md`): arcs wear both
cones and quakes, rifts both fissures and swarms — seven of
Cascadia's volcanoes erupted since 1700 while its fault
stayed locked. The global engine, blanket, shield, and
companion set the stage from L10 (`interior.md`,
`atmosphere.md`, `magnetosphere.md`, `moons.md`).

## Key numbers

- Belts: Ring of Fire (~75% of great quakes), Alpide
collision belt, ridge/rift murmur; intraplate surprises
(New Madrid 1811–12).
- San Andreas: ~1,300 km; ~5 cm/year over 10 Myr;
18–35 mm/year by segment; capable ~M8.2; last great
southern 1857 M7.9.
- Cascadia: 1,130 km; 19+ M≥8 in 10 kyr; last January
1700; 10–15% of ~M9 in 50 years; three quake kinds plus
slow slip.
- Depth curtain to ~700 km; 2013 Okhotsk M8.3 at ~600 km
felt Moscow-to-Tokyo.
- Gorkha 2015 M7.8; Türkiye–Syria 2023 doublet, tens of
thousands dead.
- Tsunami memory: 2004, 2011, 1964, 1700 orphan in Japan.
- L11 frame: 100–1,000 km — one fault system, one locked
margin, one shaken basin per view.

Sources: USGS pages named above; L10 `tectonics.md`.

## Example objects

Cascadia is the locked standard: 1,130 km of quiet,
ghost forests, 19 past giants, odds posted for the next.

The San Andreas is the grinding standard: 1,300 km of
creep and snap through cities, sisters sharing the slip.

The Himalayan front is the collision standard: a whole
range riding one thrust, Kathmandu in its bowl.

New Madrid is the blind standard: a buried rift shaking a
continent's interior, no trench offshore, no warning line.

Japan Trench is the demonstrated standard: 2011's M9.1
and its wave — what a locked margin does when it goes.

Sources: USGS Cascadia, San Andreas, and event pages.
