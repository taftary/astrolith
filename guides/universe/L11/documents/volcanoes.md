# Volcanoes — the burning regions

This page is the L11 part doc for volcanic country: shields,
calderas, arcs, rift fields, and lava provinces at 100–1,000
km — the scale where one frame holds a whole volcanic
system. The global physics lives in L10
(`volcanism.md` for fire, `tectonics.md` for cracks); the
land these systems grow on lives in `continents.md`, the
ranges they join in `mountains.md`, the flats they pave in
`plains.md`, the island chains they raise in `oceans.md`;
the dated story runs through `lifetime.md`. Single cones and
vents at 1–100 km will live in the planned L12 volcanoes
page.

From orbit, volcanic regions read as texture with an
address. A lone broad bump with a white cap rising off a
chain of islands; a pale lens of geyser basins with no
mountain at all where the biggest explosions happened;
a straight cordillera studded with perfect cones; a dark
wrinkled plain with no cones anywhere; a rust-red bulge
600 km across with a bite taken out of its top. Each is the
signature of a different plumbing — hotspot, collapsed
chamber, diving slab, splitting continent, flood crack, or
unmoving crust.

## Shield country — Hawaii

The Hawaiian Islands are the type example of hotspot
country: a 2,500-km trail of islands and seamounts aging
northwest as the Pacific plate slides over a deep hot
column, from the growing Big Island (under 0.7 million
years) back through Kauai (5.5 million) to drowned
seamounts tens of millions of years old that bend with an
old plate-direction change (see L10 `volcanism.md`).

Mauna Loa, the largest volcano on Earth at about 120 km
across and 9 km from seafloor base to summit, is a broad
dome so gentle its slopes average single digits — from 100
km up, an island, not a mountain. Kilauea on its flank is
the restless child, erupting almost continuously in living
memory. Every island repeats the life cycle: shield growth,
a cap of steeper lavas, erosion into knife ridges, then
sinking beneath new coral — the whole biography inside one
L11 frame.

Sources: USGS hotspot and Hawaii pages; L10 `volcanism.md`.

## Caldera country — Yellowstone

Yellowstone is the opposite signature: no mountain, just a
high plateau of 17,000 square km pocked with geysers and hot
springs — the steam of a magma reservoir 5–10 km down. Three
collapse cycles in 2.1 million years wrote it: Huckleberry
Ridge at 2.1 Ma (85 by 50 km, 2,450 cubic km — one of the
five largest eruptions known), Henry's Fork at 1.3 Ma (16 km
across), and the Yellowstone Caldera at 640,000 years (85 by
45 km, 1,000 cubic km — a thousand Mount St. Helens).
Ash from the latest reached the Mississippi in Louisiana;
later rhyolite flows, the world's largest, paved the
caldera floor between 170,000 and 70,000 years ago, and two
resurgent domes — Sour Creek and Mallard Lake, each over 10
by 20 km — bulge where the chamber refilled. The hotspot's
older trail runs southwest down the Snake River Plain; the
plate moves on, the fire stays.

From a region frame the caldera is nearly invisible — filled
to the brim with its own lavas — which is exactly the
lesson: the most violent volcanic country looks the
calmest. Geysers are its tell, and no eruption is expected
soon; the current 70,000-year quiet may run thousands more.

Sources: NPS resurgent calderas; USGS Yellowstone fact
sheets (FS 2005-3024, FS 100-03) and YVO pages.

## Arc country — the Andes and the island chains

The Andes run 8,900 km down South America — the longest
mountain chain on land — because the Nazca plate dives
beneath the continent along its whole length (see
`mountains.md:71-75`). The volcanic front rides inland of
the trench: stratocone after stratocone — Cotopaxi,
Villarrica, Lascar — added in months where the slab melts
at depth (`mountains.md:50`), feeding the Amazon basin with
ash and the Altiplano-Puna with one of Earth's great
ignimbrite fields. From orbit the arc reads as a dotted
line of snow-capped cones parallel to a brown trench
offshore — subduction drawn in mountains.

Island arcs are the same physics without a continent:
Japan, the Aleutians, Indonesia — curved chains where the
diving slab's melts punch through ocean crust. Tambora's
1815 VEI-7 blast (see L10 `volcanism.md`) was arc country
speaking at its loudest. Atolls and reef-rimmed islands in
`oceans.md` are arcs in old age, sinking as their fire
dies.

Sources: USGS arc and subduction pages; Smithsonian GVP.

## Rift country — Iceland and Afar

Iceland is where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge breaches the sea,
split by pulling plates over a hotspot: fissure swarms,
lava fields younger than the towns, and eruptions that
matter out of all proportion — the 2010 Eyjafjallajokull ash
closed European airspace for a week from a mountain most
travelers had never heard of. From 100 km up, Iceland is a
green-brown island with no single edifice, striped with
cracks — a ridge address, not a cone one.

Afar and the East African Rift are the continental version:
a continent unzipping in public, with Erta Ale's lava lake,
the Dabbahu fissure that tore open in days in 2005, and
stratocones along the rift shoulders (see L10
`tectonics.md`). Given 1–10 million more years the split
becomes a sea (`continents.md:64`); volcanic fields are its
birth announcement.

Sources: USGS ridge and Iceland pages; Smithsonian GVP.

## Flood country — the paved provinces

Some regions have no center at all: cracks that flooded
whole countrysides in flow-on-flow sheets. India's Deccan,
Siberia's Traps, the Columbia Plateau of the American
northwest — dark, flat-to-rolling provinces hundreds of
kilometers across, stepped at their eroded edges. The
Siberian outpouring came with the worst extinction on
record 252 million years ago; the Deccan with the
dinosaurs' end (see L10 `volcanism.md` and `lifetime.md`).
From orbit they read as sombre plains against older, paler
rock — fire as geography, not mountain. The Moon's maria
are the same landform on another world (see L10
`moons.md` and `impacts.md`).

## Olympus Mons — the region that ate the scale

Mars kept one volcanic region bigger than most countries.
Olympus Mons stands 22 km high and over 600 km across —
five Earth shields in one — with a 65-by-85 km caldera
complex, 2–10 km basal scarps, and flank flows possibly
younger than a million years. It grew over an unmoving
hotspot on crust with no plates (see L10 `volcanism.md`),
and it does not stand alone: the Tharsis rise behind it
lifts a continent-sized swell studded with three more great
shields in a row. An L11 frame centered on Olympus holds
one mountain; the same frame on Tharsis holds a volcanic
continent. Venus's planet-wide resurfacing is the
alternative end-member — provinces everywhere, plates
nowhere.

Sources: USGS Olympus Mons topography; NASA JPL PIA00300;
LPI Tharsis resources.

## How volcanic country changes over time

Arcs live tens of millions of years while their ocean
lasts, then die into folded ranges when the sea closes —
the Andes are building now (`lifetime.md:111`); older arcs
are the granite spines inside continents (`mountains.md`).
Hotspots write tracks: the Snake River Plain's calderas
march southwest with age toward Yellowstone; Hawaii's
islands march northwest. Flood provinces erupt in
geologically brief pulses — under a couple of million years
— then sit quiet for hundreds of millions, eroding into
steps and soils (see `plains.md:73-74`). Calderas resurge,
domes bulge, geysers mark the cooling; single cones at L12
live years to millennia, but the region outlives them all.

## How it interacts with the others

Arcs build the lift (`mountains.md`): cones in months,
ranges in millions of years, granite roots forever.
Rifts split the base (`continents.md`): Afar today, new
ocean tomorrow. Ash and lava pave the flat (`plains.md`):
flood basalts, ash soils among the richest farmland on
Earth, droughts where the rain shadow falls. Island arcs
seed the blue (`oceans.md`): new land for reefs, then
atolls as the fire dies and the island sinks. Rivers cut
every cone and caldera rim, and lakes pool in collapsed
craters (`rivers.md` and the planned L11 lakes page) —
Crater Lake's Wizard Island cone is a caldera holding a
lake holding a cone.

The global engine and blanket set the terms (L10
`interior.md`, `tectonics.md`, `atmosphere.md`): mantle
plumes feed the hotspots, slabs feed the arcs, and great
blasts cool the planet for years. The Moon-steadied tilt
keeps the monsoons that water the ash soils regular (L10
`moons.md`).

## Key numbers

- Hawaii: chain ~2,500 km; Kauai 5.5 Ma to Big Island
under 0.7 Ma; Mauna Loa ~120 km across, 9 km base-to-summit.
- Yellowstone: plateau 17,000 km2; collapses at 2.1 Ma
(85x50 km, 2,450 km3), 1.3 Ma (16 km), 640 ka (85x45 km,
1,000 km3); reservoir 5–10 km down; quiet 70,000 years.
- Andes: 8,900 km long; cones added in months; Altiplano
ignimbrites among Earth's largest.
- Iceland: ridge breaching sea; 2010 ash closed European
airspace for a week.
- Flood provinces: hundreds of km across; Siberian 252 Ma
with the great dying; Deccan with the dinosaurs' end.
- Olympus Mons: 22 km high, 600+ km across, caldera
65x85 km; flank flows maybe under 1 Ma.
- L11 frame: 100–1,000 km — one shield, one caldera, one
arc segment, or one flood province per view.

Sources: NPS, USGS, Smithsonian, and NASA pages named
above; L10 `volcanism.md` and `tectonics.md`.

## Example objects

Hawaii is the hotspot standard: chain aging with the plate,
giant gentle shields, eruptions in living memory.

Yellowstone is the caldera standard: three collapses, no
mountain, geysers as the tell, ash to the Mississippi.

The Andes are the arc standard: 8,900 km of diving slab
written in cones, feeding basins and ignimbrite fields.

Iceland is the rift standard: an ocean ridge walking on
land, fissures instead of cones, small eruptions with
continental reach.

Olympus Mons is the no-plates standard: one hotspot, one
unmoving crust, 600 km of piled lava — what fire builds
when nothing moves.

Sources: USGS, NPS, Smithsonian GVP, NASA JPL pages.
