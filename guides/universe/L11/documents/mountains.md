# Mountains — the lift of regions

This page describes mountain ranges and
highlands at L11 scale: how they look, how they change, how they are created, and how
they interact with continents, plains, waters, and rivers. Bridge in `previous-l10.md`,
dive in `entry.md`, timeline in `lifetime.md`.

## How it looks

From orbit a range reads as a long raised shadow with snow-white tops and dark
valleys between. Young ranges (Himalayas, Andes) run straight and sharp,
thousands of m high, snow year-round; old ranges (Appalachians) run low and
rounded, green with forest. Highlands spread as broad pale lifts with cut
valleys — the Tibetan Plateau (averaging about 5 km elevation over about
3 million km2, the Roof of the World) and the Altiplano-Puna of the Central
Andes (about 3,700 m over about 200,000 km2, the largest highland outside
Tibet) read as tan upland sheets rather than sharp lines. The oblique frame
below shows ridge-and-valley lines — parallel lifts with towns in the flats,
the classic worn-range pattern at 100-km scale. NASA's ISS view of the same
province (ISS033-E-22378, November 2012, over 500 km from northeast
Pennsylvania to southern West Virginia) explains the striping: dark forested
ridges over erosion-resistant sandstone, pale farmed valleys over soft shale
and limestone where rivers, soils, and towns gather — 540- to 300-million-year
rocks folded at right angles to the coming of Gondwana, now eroded stumps.

## How it changes over time

Ranges rise fast and wear slow. Collision lifts rock mm to cm per year; erosion
cuts it back by rivers, ice, and landslides. A young sharp range softens over
tens to hundreds of millions of years into hills, then plains — the Appalachians
were built by the Alleghanian collision of Africa with North America about
325–260 million years ago (forming Pangaea), standing once as high as today's
Alps and Rockies, then worn over 200-plus million years after Atlantic opening;
by the end of the Mesozoic they were nearly a flat plain before Cenozoic uplift
rejuvenated their streams into the present ridge-and-valley relief, now
1,000–2,000 m. Glaciers carve U-valleys in cold pulses — and the gorges survive
them: a 2011 Nature Geoscience mapping of over 1,000 Alpine inner gorges
(Montgomery and Korup) shows river-cut V-gorges persisting through multiple
glacial advances, buried under sediment beneath the ice and re-excavated after
retreat, so the Alps carry both signatures at once. Modern ice loss sharpens
the fluvial turn: NASA Earth Observatory's August 2026 Pasterze report shows
Austria's largest glacier reduced toward a single linchpin icefall. Volcanic
arcs add new cones in months. A range lives one orogeny: build, peak,
wear, bury. The Alps show the balanced case: a 2009 Tectonophysics study finds
uplift and glacial plus river erosion in equilibrium, holding elevation
near-constant while up to 2–2.5 mm per year of rock uplift continues in the
Western and Central Alps (part rebound after ice loss, part deep mantle
forcing). Second pass:
D-double-prime layer feeds plumes; inner-core slowdown marks time; trenches
(Mariana 10,935 m) mirror ranges in reverse. Sources: USGS Birth of the Mountains
and This Dynamic Earth; NASA Earth Observatory Appalachians (2013) and Alps (2011);
Battaglia et al. 2022 review.

## How it is created

Mountains are made by thickening crust: ocean-continent collision (Andes),
continent-continent collision (Himalayas, Alps), rifting shoulders, volcanic
arcs, and hotspot domes. Thrust faults stack rock; folding bends it; granite
intrudes below. The Himalayas rise as India pushes into Asia about 5 cm per
year, colliding from about 40–50 million years ago after a 6,000-km-plus
northward journey from off Australia across the closing Tethys Sea — with
about 2,500 km of Indian crust subducted or stacked, and neither plate
subducting cleanly because both are buoyant continental rock, so the collision
zone thrust skyward instead. The Andes rise where the oceanic Nazca plate dives
under South America at about 7–8 cm per year (about 70 mm per year in the north
to 80 mm per year in the south, USGS), marked at the surface by the Peru-Chile
(Atacama) trench; water carried down lowers the mantle melting point and feeds
the andesite volcanic chain, with scraped-off sediment accreted onto the
continent. The Andes at about 8,900 km are the longest continental range on
Earth (Britannica), averaging about 4,000 m with over fifty 6,000-m peaks and
200–700 km wide. Highlands lift whole regions without folding.
Sources: USGS This Dynamic Earth (Himalayas); USGS Nazca–South America seismicity
(Rhea et al. 2010); Geological Society ocean-continent margins; NASA Earth
Observatory.

## How it interacts with the others

A range interacts with all parts. It rises from continents (`continents.md`)
where plates meet; it sheds sediment to plains (`plains.md`) by rivers; it
blocks rain, making deserts on one side and forests on the other; it feeds
rivers (`rivers.md`) from snowmelt; its coasts meet seas where deltas build
(`oceans.md`). The monsoon makes the mechanism visible: 80 percent of Nepal
Himalaya rain falls June–September, with 1–3 m on the foreland and under
0.5 m on Tibet beyond the crest — a ten-fold drop over 40–50 km across the
Marsyandi transect (about 4 m per year on the wet southern flank to the
semi-arid northern shadow), driving a twenty-fold erosion gradient from about
2 mm per year in the wet south to about 0.1 mm per year in the shadowed north.
That sediment feeds the Ganges–Brahmaputra system at about 1 billion tonnes
per year, 95 percent of it moved in the May–October monsoon, building the
Bengal Fan — the largest submarine fan on Earth at about 3.9 million km2.
The Andes show the mirror: the range blocks Amazon moisture from the Pacific
side, and with the Chilean Coast Range forms the two-sided shadow that keeps
the Atacama at about 15 mm per year (hyper-arid since at least the Miocene),
while its wet eastern flank feeds Andean-origin whitewater rivers carrying
about 90 percent of the Amazon's sediment to the Atlantic. Air climbs ranges
and rains; ice grinds them; life zones stack on slopes. Ranges make weather,
rivers carry ranges away. Sources: Bookhagen and Burbank Marsyandi network;
Wulf et al. NW Himalaya; Ganges–Brahmaputra delta sediment budget (2023);
PNAS Andes–Amazon–Atlantic pathway (2024); Houston and Hartley Atacama
rainshadow; USGS water-cycle pages; NASA climate pages.

## Typical size and mass

- Size: Himalayas about 2,400 km long in the arc proper (about 2,900 km
  with the Tibetan Plateau front in USGS This Dynamic Earth), 200–400 km
  wide, peaks over 8,000 m; Andes about 8,900 km long, 200–700 km wide,
  averaging about 4,000 m; Appalachian orogen over 3,000 km (US Highlands
  portion about 2,400 km), peaks to 2,037 m (Mount Mitchell, 6,684 ft).
  L11 frames 100–1,000 km sections — whole sub-ranges, passes, valley systems.
- Mass: a 500 x 200 km range block 10 km thick holds on the order of 10^18 kg.
  Crust under young ranges is double-thick: about 70–75 km under the Tethyan
  Himalaya (receiver functions, INDEPTH/HIMNT), deeper than 65 km across most
  of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and to 79–82 km maxima in western Tibet (GOCE
  gravity inversion), vs 30–40 km normal continental and 6–7 km oceanic.
- Relief: young ranges 4,000–8,848.86 m (Everest, 2020 Nepal–China joint
  survey; 8,848 m was the 1954 Survey of India value used until 2020);
  old ranges 1,000–2,000 m; highlands 1,000–4,000 m plateaus.

Sources: USGS This Dynamic Earth (Himalayas); USGS peak pages;
NASA elevation pages.

## Example objects

Example: Himalayas — India-Asia collision from about 40–50 million years ago,
Everest 8,848.86 m (2020 Nepal–China joint figure, snow cap included),
still rising over 1 cm per year with erosion cutting at about the same rate.
2024 Nature Geoscience finds Everest (Chomolungma, 8,849 m) stands about 250 m
above its peers with GPS uplift near 2 mm per year vs about 1 mm per year
long-term: an Arun River capture about 89 thousand years ago (50–100 ka fits)
steepened the gorge (7 km drop over 35 km), and the flexural isostatic rebound
adds 0.16–0.53 mm per year and 15–50 m of the anomaly (Han et al. 2024; Author
Correction January 2025; Matters Arising July 2026 notes debate, so the
capture share is an active research result, not settled fact).
Example: Andes — ocean-continent subduction at 7–8 cm per year, the longest
continental range at about 8,900 km, averaging about 4,000 m with over fifty
6,000-m peaks; Aconcagua 6,961 m commonly quoted (Argentine official 6,959 m;
2012 university remeasurement 6,960.8 m), highest outside Asia; Altiplano-Puna
highland and andesite volcanic chain included.
Example: Appalachians — Alleghanian collision worn flat over 200-plus million
years (full orogen over 3,000 km; US Highlands portion about 2,400 km),
Mount Mitchell 2,037 m (6,684 ft, highest east of the Mississippi),
ridge-and-valley in the target image (State College oblique; ISS province view
ISS033-E-22378). Example: Alps — Africa–Europe collision closing the Alpine
Tethys, main building from about 35 million years ago; Mont Blanc 4,808 m;
sharp horns and U-valleys with inner V-gorges persisting through glaciations;
uplift to 2.5 mm per year balanced by erosion. Sources: USGS This Dynamic
Earth and Birth of the Mountains; USGS Nazca–South America seismicity;
NPS Mount Mitchell; NASA Earth Observatory Appalachians (2013) and Alps
(2011, 2026 Pasterze); Britannica Andes and Alps.

## Key numbers

- L11 frames: 100–1,000 km; whole Himalayas 2,400 km; Andes 8,900 km.
- Heights: Everest 8,848.86 m (2020 joint survey); Andes to 6,961 m
  commonly quoted (Aconcagua; Argentine official 6,959 m); Appalachians
  to 2,037 m (Mount Mitchell).
- Rates: uplift mm–cm per year (Himalayas over 1 cm per year; Alps to 2.5 mm
  per year); river incision to 10–15 mm per year in the Sub-Himalaya, 4–8 mm
  per year in the High Himalaya, under 0.5 mm per year in the Lesser Himalaya
  (Lavé and Avouac terraces; Garhwal 10Be 0.1–2 mm per year); long-term
  exhumation 2–5 mm per year; erosion removes 60–70 percent of uplifted rock
  (2025 coupled landscape–orographic model); orogeny tens of millions of years.
- Crust: 70–75 km under the Himalaya (to 79–82 km maxima in western Tibet);
  30–40 km normal continental; 6–7 km oceanic.

Sources: ladder L11 row; USGS; NASA.
