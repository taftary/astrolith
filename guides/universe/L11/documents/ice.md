# Ice — the frozen regions

This page is the L11 part doc for ice at regional scale:
ice sheets, ice streams, shelves, mountain ice fields,
permafrost, and the polar regions that hold them — the
scale where one frame covers a whole ice system with its
coast. The global ice accounts live in L10 (`surface.md`
for the caps, `hydrosphere.md` for the water ledger);
glacial valleys sit in `mountains.md:43-50`; the dated ice
ages run through `lifetime.md:121-128`. Single glaciers,
moraines, and kettles at 1–100 km will live in the planned
L12 glaciers page.

From a region frame, ice is a white country with plumbing.
A sheet bigger than countries pouring to the sea through a
few fast rivers of ice; a floating shelf the size of Spain
calving bergs the size of states; a glacier nicknamed
Doomsday draining a basin the size of Florida; patterned
ground and slumping coasts where frozen soil thaws. White
from orbit, but every shade of motion inside.

## The two great sheets

**East Antarctica — the stable giant.** The largest ice
mass on Earth, kilometers thick over a continent mostly
above sea level, holding the great majority of the ~58 m
of sea-level rise locked in all Antarctic ice. Cold,
high, and slow — changing, so far, more slowly than
anywhere else (see L10 `surface.md`).

**West Antarctica — the marine gamble.** Smaller
(Greenland-sized) but grounded largely below sea level on
beds sloping inland — the geometry that makes retreat
self-feeding once started. Thwaites and Pine Island drain
it to the Amundsen Sea, and what happens here decides
meters of ocean (see below).

**Greenland — the melting island.** About 1.7 million
square km holding 2.9 million cubic km — 7.4 m of seas —
shedding mass every year since 1998 as warming air and
ocean eat at it. A 2026 NASA-led bedrock map traced 1,943
subglacial valleys, a third newly found, with long straight
troughs hinting tectonic guidance for where meltwater ran
and where ice will funnel (see L10 `surface.md`). Its fast
outlet glaciers have doubled and tripled speed as their
ice tongues thinned — the Arctic version of Larsen's
lesson.

Sources: NSIDC ice-sheet pages; NASA Earth Observatory
Greenland valleys (2026) and ice pages.

## Ice streams — the rivers inside the ice

Sheets barely move; streams race. A few corridors carry
most of a sheet's discharge to the sea at kilometers per
year: Pine Island and Thwaites in the west, the great
northeast Greenland stream, Antarctica's Lambert system
feeding the Amery shelf. Streams are lubricated at the bed
by meltwater and soft sediment — including water from
hundreds of subglacial lakes, seven of which drained at
once beneath Thwaites in 2013, sending Loch-Ness-worths of
fresh water to the sea and doubling downstream melt.
Where a stream meets the ocean it goes afloat — and the
boundary, the grounding line, is where the sheet's future
is decided: retreat it inland down a deepening bed and the
flow speeds itself up.

Sources: ESA CryoSat subglacial-lakes reporting; NASA
Thwaites pages.

## Thwaites — the watched glacier

Thwaites drains a Florida-sized basin through a 75-mile-
wide front, already contributing ~4% of global sea-level
rise with outflow nearly doubled in three decades. Its bed
lies largely below sea level and deepens inland; warm water
melts it from below; the grounding line has retreated
nearly 9 miles since the 1990s in pulses — twice as fast at
times as satellites showed. If it went entirely, ~65 cm
(over two feet) of seas over centuries — and as the
keystone holding back its neighbors, its loss could unlock
3+ meters. The 2018–2024 international collaboration's
preliminary read: steady retreat, not this-century
collapse, up to ~6 cm by 2100 — but rapid bursts remain
possible, and the U.S. East Coast stands in the line of
whatever comes.

Sources: NASA Earth Observatory Thwaites (2020); Science
AAAS ITGC reporting (2024); AMS land-ice education module.

## Shelves — the floating gatekeepers

Where ice meets sea it spreads afloat: shelves fringe ~45%
of Antarctica, and they hold back everything behind them.
The Ross — ~500,000 square km, nearly Spain-sized — and
the Filchner-Ronne buttress whole sectors of both sheets;
lose one and its glaciers surge 3–8 times faster within
months, as Larsen B proved. In 35 days of early 2002 the
3,250-square-km Larsen B shattered into thousands of bergs
after warm summers ponded meltwater that wedged its
crevasses open; its glaciers accelerated up to eightfold
and never slowed. Larsen A went in 1995; the Delaware-sized
A68 calved from Larsen C in 2017 as routine business —
calving is normal, disintegration is not. Collapse of a
Ross-scale shelf would eventually cost meters of sea level;
even one meter redraws every coastline on Earth.

Sources: NSIDC Larsen and ice-shelf pages; NASA Larsen B
World of Change; NASA SOP Larsen retrospective.

## Mountain ice fields and permafrost

Beyond the poles, ice rules the heights and the far north.
Patagonia's ice fields — South America's largest temperate
ice — spill tidewater glaciers into fjords; Himalayan and
Karakoram glaciers water a billion people and carve the
troughs of `mountains.md:43-50`; Kilimanjaro's and the
Alps' remnants measure warming in decades. Around the
Arctic, permafrost — ground frozen two years and more —
underlies a quarter of the northern land, locking carbon
and methane from ages of buried life; where it thaws, coasts
slump, lakes drain overnight, and the ground polygons
itself into nets and wedges. Sea ice versus land ice is the
rule that matters: melting sea ice changes no sea level;
every centimeter of rise comes from land ice reaching the
sea.

Sources: NSIDC permafrost pages; NSIDC and NOAA sea-ice
pages.

## The polar regions

The poles are opposites. Antarctica is a continent wearing
ice, ringed by sea ice that doubles its white area each
winter; the Arctic is an ocean wearing ice, ringed by
continents — sea ice, Greenland, permafrost shores. Both
endure months of night and day; both amplify warming as
white gives way to dark water and land. The Arctic's
September minimum tied tenth-lowest in 2026 at 4.6 million
square km, its ice young and thin (see L10 `surface.md`).
Polar night, polar day, and auroras over both belong with
L10 (`magnetosphere.md`); the ice on the ground is here.

## How ice country changes over time

Ice ages swing the whole map: sheets spread from the poles,
sea level falls a hundred meters, then warming shrinks the
ice and drowns the coasts — the pulses in
`lifetime.md:121-128`. Between ages, the drama is local:
shelves calve and regrow, streams surge and settle,
Thwaites pulses, Larsen shatters. Humans now force the
pace from both sides — warm air ponds the shelves, warm
water gnaws the grounding lines — and the record in ice
cores keeps the receipts (see L10 `hydrosphere.md`).

## How it interacts with the others

Ranges hold and feed the ice (`mountains.md`): troughs,
cirques, and the rain-shadow line — every valley glacier
below this page's frame. Plains inherit its work
(`plains.md`): till, loess, outwash, kettle lakes, the
Mega-Chad beds of wetter ages. Coasts drown or rebound
(`oceans.md`): seas rise with melt, shores spring back
where ice weight lifts. Rivers run on its schedule
(`rivers.md`): spring melt floods, glacial flour blues the
water, dammed lakes burst. Lakes are its children
(`lakes.md`): the Great Lakes are scour basins, Baikal a
rift lake amid ranges. Fire meets it (`volcanoes.md`):
Iceland's eruptions melt floods (jokulhlaups) from under
ice; ash darkens sheets and speeds melt. Shaking moves it
(`earthquakes.md`): Alaska's 1964 quake dropped shores and
raised seabed in one morning. Storms feed and starve it
(`weather.md`): snowfall builds sheets, rain and foehn
winds destroy shelves. The global engine, air, ocean, and
shield set the climate from L10.

## Key numbers

- Antarctica: 14M km2, 26.5–30M km3, ~58 m of seas; East
stable and high, West marine and vulnerable.
- Greenland: 1.7M km2, 2.9M km3, ~7.4 m; losing mass since
1998; 1,943 subglacial valleys mapped 2026.
- Ross shelf: ~500,000 km2 (Spain-sized); fringes ~45% of
Antarctica; collapse costs meters.
- Larsen B: 3,250 km2 in 35 days (2002); glaciers to 8x
faster; A68 Delaware-sized (2017).
- Thwaites: Florida-sized, 75-mile front, ~4% of rise;
grounding line −9 miles since 1990s; full loss ~65 cm,
unlocked 3+ m; ~6 cm by 2100 in current modeling.
- Subglacial lakes: hundreds; seven drained at once (2013).
- L11 frame: 100–1,000 km — one ice stream system, one
shelf embayment, one ice field per view.

Sources: NSIDC, NASA, ESA, and AMS pages named above.

## Example objects

East Antarctica is the stable standard: the great white
reserve, high and slow.

West Antarctica is the vulnerable standard: below-sea-level
beds, Thwaites and Pine Island draining, meters at stake.

Greenland is the melting standard: losing since 1998,
valleys mapped under the ice, outlets racing.

Larsen is the collapsed standard: 35 days from shelf to
bergs, glaciers never slowing after.

Thwaites is the watched standard: Florida of ice, 4% of
rise, steadiness so far, bursts possible.
