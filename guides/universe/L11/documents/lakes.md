# Lakes — the still waters

This page is the L11 part doc for lakes: the great inland
seas and their kinds at 100–1,000 km — the scale where one
frame holds a whole lake with its basin. Rivers that feed
and drain them live in `rivers.md`; the basins that hold
them in `plains.md`; the coasts they resemble in
`oceans.md`; the global water accounts in L10
(`hydrosphere.md`); the dated story in `lifetime.md`.
Small lakes and bays at 1–100 km will live in the planned
L12 pages; Titan's seas close this page.

From a region frame, a great lake is a blue fact with a
story in its shape: a long straight slot between rift
walls, a broad sheet gouged by ice, a shrinking stain in a
desert basin, a crater bowl holding sky. Lakes are the
short-lived members of the waters — rivers carve for
millions of years and oceans for billions, but most lakes
fill, silt, and vanish in thousands — which makes them the
landscape's diary: every shoreline above the water is a
dated entry of a wetter past.

## How lakes are made

A lake is a basin plus water with nowhere to go. The basins
come in a few regional kinds (the full type table sits in
`rivers.md:18-25`; this page holds the great examples):

**Rift slots — Baikal, Tanganyika.** Continents splitting
make the deepest lakes: straight, narrow, walled. Baikal —
oldest, largest, deepest freshwater lake at nearly a mile
deep and over 23,000 km3, as much as all the Great Lakes
combined — is a living rift, widening yearly; Tanganyika
runs the same pattern down Africa.

**Glacial scour — the Great Lakes.** Ice sheets gouged
basins and the melt filled them: Superior, Michigan, Huron,
Erie, Ontario — broad blue sheets with plume edges,
thousands of feet deep in places, holding a fifth of the
world's surface fresh water between them. Superior's length
is L11-scale (`previous-l11.md:73`); the close-up of bays
and blooms sits at L12 (`landscapes.md`).

**Endorheic basins — Caspian, Aral, Great Salt Lake.**
Where rivers flow in and only evaporation flows out, water
ponds salty in desert bowls. The Caspian — 371,000 square
km, 1,030 km long, the world's largest lake by area and
volume at ~40% of all lake water — is an ocean's orphan,
landlocked with the Volga pouring in and sturgeon below.
Great Salt Lake is its shrinking American cousin, rimming
white with salt as it falls.

**Crater bowls — Toba, Crater Lake.** Explosions and
collapses leave perfect bowls that fill with sky: Toba's
caldera lake in Sumatra (see `volcanoes.md`), Crater Lake's
Wizard Island cone in a 10-km ring. Impact rings hold water
the same way — Manicouagan's reservoir circle is a crater
worn into a lake.

**Reservoirs — Powell, Mead.** Drowned canyons behind dams:
Lake Powell pools 300 winding km in desert stone, its pale
bathtub rings recording record lows (3,517 ft, September
2026). The dam wall belongs at L12; the stored water is
here.

Sources: USGS lake and reservoir pages; NASA Earth
Observatory lake pages; L11 `rivers.md` and `plains.md`.

## Portraits of the greats

**Caspian — the sea that is a lake.** Endorheic, saline,
1,030 km stem to stern with 7,000 km of shore across five
countries, surface 28 m below sea level, residence 250
years. Neither river nor ocean, it sets its own level with
the Volga's moods.

**Superior — the cold giant.** Largest freshwater lake by
area, deepest and coldest of its five, storm-synoptic in
itself — November gales sink ships on waters that behave
like a small sea. Its north shore is among the oldest rock
on Earth.

**Victoria — the shallow dish.** Africa's largest, broad
and shallow, filling a gentle downwarp between rift arms;
its level breathes with equatorial rains, and its cichlid
fishes evolved by the hundreds in isolation — evolution in
a basin.

**Baikal — the ancient.** Nearly a mile deep, a fifth of
fresh surface water, 25-million-year rift still opening,
seals and sponges found nowhere else. The oldest lake and
arguably the most alive.

**Titicaca — the high lake.** The highest great lake,
navigable waters above the clouds in the Altiplano,
terraced shores farmed for millennia — altitude as a lake's
character.

**Great Salt Lake — the vanishing.** America's inland sea
reduced toward salt flats and dust, its relic shorelines
(Bonneville's bathtub rings hundreds of meters above the
water) recording ice-age plenty against present drought.

**Aral — the warning.** Fourth-largest in 1960 at 68,000
square km; rivers diverted for cotton from the 1960s;
90 percent of volume lost, split into four bodies, eastern
lobe dry in 2014 for the first time in 600 years, Aralkum
desert on the exposed bed blowing salt onto fields. The
Kok-Aral dike (2005) raised the Small Aral 4 m and brought
fish back north — half a rescue, half a memorial. Tracked
frame by frame by Landsat, MODIS, and Sentinel.

Sources: NASA Earth Observatory Aral series (1973–2025);
ESA Sentinel Aral 2025; USGS lake pages.

## How lakes change over time

Lakes breathe with climate and die by plumbing. Wet decades
fill Chad and the Great Salt Lake; droughts empty them;
rivers pirate outlets and lower whole systems overnight in
geologic terms. Humans now rival climate: Powell and Mead
fall with Colorado demand, the Aral fell to cotton, and
dams turn rivers into staircases of reservoirs (`rivers.md:
60-61`). Titan's seas breathe methane seasons instead —
but the pattern, fill and expose, is the same physics under
orange smog.

A lake's life ends three ways: drained (an outlet cuts
down), filled (silt and deltas win — deltas grow in
`rivers.md`), or dried (evaporation wins — salt flats in
`plains.md:21`). Badwater, Bonneville, and Mega-Chad
(`plains.md:68`) are the three endings on display.

## Titan's seas — lakes of natural gas

Saturn's largest moon holds the only standing liquid besides
Earth's — and no water at all. Methane and ethane rain from
orange smog into polar seas hundreds of kilometers across,
fed by river channels cut in water-ice bedrock, evaporating
back into the haze (see L10 `atmospheres.md` and
`hydrosphere.md`). No tides from a moon, slow seasons of 7.5
Earth years, shorelines of drowned valleys like Powell's —
the same landforms, alien fluid. Dragonfly will walk these
shores in the 2030s.

Sources: NASA Titan and Dragonfly pages.

## How it interacts with the others

Rivers feed and drain every lake (`rivers.md`): threads
join trunks to the sea, deltas grow where they arrive,
canyon slots drown behind dams. Basins hold them
(`plains.md`): playa floors, salt flats, Mega-Chad beds.
Ranges dam and feed them (`mountains.md`): glacial troughs
(see :43-50), rain shadows starving the far side, ash
fertilizing the water. Coasts teach by contrast
(`oceans.md`): tides and surf absent, deltas and reefs the
shared vocabulary. Fire builds the bowls (`volcanoes.md`):
caldera lakes, crater rings, basalt-dammed valleys. Shaking
redraws shores (`earthquakes.md`): New Madrid rerouted the
Mississippi; landslide dams burst into floods. The global
ledgers — ocean, air, ice, shield — set the terms from L10
(`hydrosphere.md`, `atmosphere.md`, `magnetosphere.md`).

## Key numbers

- Caspian: 371,000 km2, 1,030 km long, ~40% of lake water;
surface −28 m; residence 250 years.
- Baikal: 23,000+ km3 (~20% of fresh surface water);
nearly a mile deep; ~25 million years old.
- Great Lakes: ~23,000 km3 combined; Superior L11-scale in
length.
- Aral: 68,000 km2 in 1960 (4th); −90% volume since;
split in four; east lobe dry 2014 (first in 600 years);
Kok-Aral +4 m in the north.
- Powell: full pool 3,700 ft; record low 3,517.24 ft
(September 2026).
- Titan: methane seas hundreds of km across; 7.5-year
seasons; Dragonfly 2030s.
- L11 frame: 100–1,000 km — one great lake with its basin
per view; bays and blooms at L12.

Sources: NASA Earth Observatory; ESA; USGS; L11 `rivers.md`.

## Example objects

Baikal is the ancient standard: oldest, deepest, most
alive — a rift still opening.

Caspian is the scale standard: sea-sized, river-fed,
self-leveling — neither lake nor sea behaved.

Aral is the warning standard: fourth to fragment in one
lifetime — the cost of diverted rivers, written in salt.

Superior is the freshwater standard: cold, deep,
storm-making — a sea that happens to be fresh.

Titan's seas are the alien standard: same shorelines, no
water — methane playing every role water plays here.

Sources: NASA, ESA, and USGS pages named above.
