# Globulars — ancient swarms of the halo

Globular clusters are the halo's ancient swarms: round gas-free balls of
a hundred thousand to ten million old stars, 11-13 billion years old,
looping far outside the star-forming disk. 47 Tucanae is the featured
example; Omega Centauri — the largest Milky Way globular — is the bridge.
How a globular relates to the other L5 parts runs through every section
below.

## What it looks

A globular is a ball of old stars, brightest at the middle and thinning
smoothly outward: thousands of yellow-red suns crowded so tight at the
core that neighbours sit a fraction of a light-year apart (in Omega
Centauri's core the mean spacing is estimated near 0.1 light-year),
against more than four light-years between the Sun and its nearest
neighbour. The
typical spacing between globular stars is about one light-year, falling
to roughly a third of a light-year in the core — about thirteen times
closer than Proxima Centauri is to the Sun; average densities run near
0.4 stars per cubic parsec, rising to hundreds or thousands in the core,
against about 0.1 near the Sun. No dust
lanes, no pink star-birth knots — the gas is long used up or blown out,
so what you see is pure old starlight. The two showcase clusters frame
the range: 47 Tucanae is a concentrated Shapley–Sawyer class III
cluster at [Fe/H] = -0.78, while Omega Centauri is a loose class VIII
cluster at [Fe/H] = -1.35, its spread of chemistries already hinting it
is no single batch of stars.

## How it changes over time

Nothing is born here anymore; the story is aging and sorting. Hubble
watched the central six light-years of 47 Tuc over nearly seven years —
ten image sets, positions for as many as 130,000 stars, precise
velocities for almost 15,000 — and caught mass segregation in the act:
heavy stars sink to the core while light ones speed outward. The slowest
movers were 23 blue stragglers, hot stars twice the average mass likely
built by collisions in the crush. A 2015 follow-up caught the other end
of the same sorting: young white dwarfs drifting outward, the stellar
exodus in action.

The crush also breeds exotica neither open clusters nor the field can
match. 47 Tuc holds at least 21 blue stragglers near its core — stars
bluer and twice as massive as the turnoff allows, first found by Hubble
in 1991 — with one spinning 75 times faster than the Sun, a collision
scar. Both formation channels operate at once: collisional stragglers
rule the core while mass-transfer stragglers, betrayed by
carbon/oxygen-depleted photospheres (Ferraro et al. 2006), live at the
outskirts. The same traffic builds millisecond pulsars — 35 known in 47
Tuc, second only to Terzan 5 — low-mass X-ray binaries and cataclysmic
variables, and made 47 Tuc the first globular detected in gamma rays by
Fermi.

Sorting ends in core collapse for about one Milky Way globular in five:
the core contracts, brightness climbing all the way in. Binaries hold
collapse back, soaking up passers-by and tossing them out faster; each
dive through the disk plane hits back with a tidal shock. Meanwhile the
cluster evaporates over some ten billion years while tides strip the
outskirts — torn Palomar 5 trails a 5,000-solar-mass stream 30,000
light-years along its orbit, and models predict the cluster itself will
dissolve within about a billion years, its black holes kicking the last
stars out (Gieles et al. 2021). Omega Centauri bleeds too: Gaia DR2
found its lost stars as the Fimbulthul stream, 309 same-age stars over
18 degrees of Hydra and Centaurus (Ibata et al. 2019) — one more receipt
for the devoured-dwarf story.

## How it is created

Globulars are born where star birth runs hottest and densest: starburst
regions and colliding galaxies, where whole clusters of clusters pile up
and merge — Hubble sees this now in the Antennae. Once thought single
batches from one cloud, nearly all ancient globulars carry multiple
populations: 47 Tuc holds at least two generations of different age
and metal content, and
NGC 2808 holds three — a triple main sequence born within 200 million
years (Piotto et al. 2007). One theory makes NGC 2808 the old core of
the Gaia-Enceladus dwarf, which would also explain its million-plus
stars; it remains one hypothesis, not a firm membership.

Many were not born here at all: some formed in dwarf galaxies and were
pulled in by tides, and Omega Centauri's spread of ages and metals plus
its backward orbit mark it as one such stripped dwarf core. No globular
shows active star formation today — these are among the oldest objects
anywhere, old enough to set a floor under the age of the universe.

The chemistry rules out a single burst. In nearly every ancient globular,
stars rich in sodium are poor in oxygen and vice versa, a pattern that
needs hot hydrogen burning in a first generation whose expelled gas then
formed a second generation. That ejecta-reuse picture is the leading
idea, but Bastian and Lardo (2017) show it fails a growing list of
constraints — no massive cluster younger than about 2 billion years
shows the pattern — so the puzzle of multiple populations remains
unsolved. A cluster that looks like one ball of
same-age starlight is therefore a stack of two or more populations
wearing the same shape.

## How it interacts with the others

- **With open clusters (see `open.md`):** cousins with opposite fates —
  young loose disk groups dissolve in hundreds of millions of years,
  while globulars stay bound for nearly a Hubble time in the halo.
- **With clouds (see `clouds.md`):** globulars carry no gas or dust, so
  no new stars ignite inside them — birth belongs to the clouds.
- **With the disk (see `disk.md`):** looping orbits punch through the
  plane again and again — each crossing shocks the cluster, stripping
  stars and speeding core collapse, while the disk's gas never refuels it.
- **With galaxy assembly:** metal-poor globulars map the halo, metal-rich
  ones the bulge, and orbits keep the receipts: at least eight came with
  the Gaia-Enceladus dwarf 8-11 billion years ago — Messier 2, Messier
  56, Messier 75, Messier 79, NGC 1851, NGC 2298 and NGC 5286 are the
  firm members — while the Sagittarius dwarf carries its own set with
  Messier 54 sitting at its core, plus Terzan 7, Terzan 8 and Arp 2 in
  its body and Palomar 12, Whiting 1 and others in its streams (with
  twenty more candidates from VVV and Gaia EDR3). About a quarter of
  Milky Way globulars overall were accreted this way.
- **With planets:** crowded metal-poor cores look hostile — Hubble found
  zero planets in 47 Tuc's core where ten to fifteen were expected, and
  a follow-up in the uncrowded outskirts also found none, pointing at
  low metallicity rather than crowding as the cause. The one exception
  proves the rule differently: the planet around pulsar PSR B1620-26 in
  Messier 4 likely formed after its host pulsar did.

### Open questions: middleweight black holes and Gaia distances

Gaia-era astrometry keeps sharpening the numbers without settling the
biggest question. Gaia DR2 parallaxes pinned 47 Tuc to 4.45 +/- 0.01
kpc (Shao & Li 2019; Chen et al. 2018), and proper-motion modelling
gives Omega Centauri 4.05 +/- 0.1 x 10^6 solar masses (D'Souza & Rix
2013). Whether either cluster hides an intermediate-mass black hole is
still open: a 2,200-solar-mass hole claimed in 47 Tuc from pulsar
accelerations (Kiziltan et al. 2017) found no support in fuller pulsar
timing (Freire et al. 2017), though a 2024 radio source at its heart
(ICRAR, ATCA imaging) reopened the case. In Omega Centauri, seven
fast-moving central stars fit a hole of at least 8,200 solar masses
(Haberle et al. 2024), already challenged by combined kinematics and
pulsar timing (Banares-Hernandez et al. 2025). The pattern matches the
wider field: no globular-cluster middleweight hole has yet survived
scrutiny, with the nearest comparable hint an 800-solar-mass central
excess in Messier 4 (Vitral et al. 2023) that could equally be a compact
clump of dead stars.

## Examples and key numbers

Typical globulars hold tens of thousands to several million stars across
10-300 light-years at about 25,000 solar luminosities on average; the
Milky Way owns 150-plus (160 with the VVV finds, more likely hidden
behind bulge dust), Andromeda up to 500, M87 up to 13,000.

Example objects:

| Cluster | Distance | Size | Stars / mass | Age | Naked eye |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 47 Tucanae (NGC 104) | ~14,500 ly (4.45 kpc, Gaia parallax) | ~120 ly across, 44 arcmin on the sky | ~1 million stars, 7.0 x 10^5 solar masses | ~13 Gyr, [Fe/H] = -0.78 | mag 4.1, second brightest after Omega Cen |
| Omega Centauri (NGC 5139) | ~15,800 ly (4.8 kpc dynamical) | ~150 ly across, near moon-sized | ~10 million stars, 4.05 x 10^6 solar masses | ~11.5 Gyr, [Fe/H] = -1.35 | mag 3.9, brightest and most massive Milky Way globular |

- **47 Tucanae (NGC 104)** — featured example, second brightest globular
  after Omega Centauri, ~14,500 light-years out in Tucana, ~120
  light-years across, about a million stars in 7.0 x 10^5 solar masses,
  ~13 billion years old, naked-eye at magnitude 4.1, Shapley–Sawyer
  class III. Gaia parallaxes pin the distance to 4.45 +/- 0.01 kpc.
  Source: Wikipedia
  "47 Tucanae"; ESA/Hubble heic0616a (million-star caption); Shao & Li
  2019 and Chen et al. 2018 (Gaia distances).
- **Omega Centauri (NGC 5139)** — the L4 bridge, largest and most massive
  Milky Way globular, ~150 light-years across, ~10 million stars in
  4.05 x 10^6 solar masses, ~15,800 light-years away, ~11.5 billion
  years old, naked-eye at magnitude 3.9, Shapley–Sawyer class VIII. Its
  retrograde orbit and spread of ages and metals mark it as a devoured
  dwarf's core. Source: Wikipedia "Omega Centauri"; D'Souza & Rix 2013
  (proper-motion mass).
