## What L4 is and how it looks

L4 is the island-universe scale at 10^20–10^21 m: single galaxies with
shapes of their own — barred spirals with dust-marbled disks and yellowish
bulges, smooth featureless ellipticals, ragged irregulars and faint dwarfs —
many with a supermassive black hole at the center (M87* at 6.5 billion Suns;
our own Sagittarius A* at about 4.3 million). Its anchor landmark is home:
the Milky Way, ~100,000 light-years across in the ladder anchor
(9.46 x 10^20 m; D25 isophote ~87,400 light-years), a barred spiral
(SBbc) of 100–400 billion stars whose disk we see edge-on as the glowing
band across our night sky. The far landmark is Andromeda (M31): the nearest
major spiral, about 2.5 million light-years away (765 kpc), a barred
spiral (SAB(s)b) of ~1 trillion stars whose bright disk spans ~152,000
light-years (D25 isophote) — slightly larger than home, and the galaxy
we are falling toward at ~110 km/s for a possible merger billions of
years out (now only ~50% within 10 Gyr in Gaia+HST Monte Carlo work,
so the far-future fate stays open; see [lifetime.md](./lifetime.md)). Around them sit the smaller
companions: Triangulum (M33), the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, and
dozens of fainter dwarf satellites. Our Local Group — 134+ known members
within 1 Mpc of the center, spanning ~16.7 million light-years tip to
tip (5.11 Mpc major axis) in a Milky Way–Andromeda dumbbell with lobes
~800 kpc apart — sits inside its larger currents, pulled toward
Virgo's 1,300 to 2,000 members and the clusters beyond.

## What carries over into L5

Everything L4 shows at galaxy scale is still there, only closer. The Milky
Way L4 frames as one tilted coin — disk, brighter core, faint halo — becomes
the L5 contents: the spiral arms that marble that disk, the giant molecular
clouds where new stars are being born right now, and the star clusters young
and ancient that record its history. The L5 anchor is the molecular-cloud
complex at 100 parsecs (3.09 x 10^18 m): a single star-forming cloud the
size of the Orion complex, the unit our home neighborhood is built from. The
near landmark is the Pleiades (M45): an open cluster of 1,000+ young blue
stars about 444 light-years away in Taurus (136.2 pc, Gaia DR3 135.74 pc),
about 115–125 million years old (124.5 Myr on the 2026 lithium-depletion
scale), core radius ~8 light-years inside a ~43-light-year tidal span —
among the nearest clusters to Earth and the nearest Messier object,
passing through a wisp of interstellar dust
that scatters blue light around its brightest members. That glow is not
leftover birth material: the reflection nebulosity is a foreground dust
cloud the cluster is moving through at about 18 km/s, and the grouping
should hold about 250 million years more before dispersing. (The very
nearest open cluster is the Hyades at ~153 light-years, the older face
met in [open.md](./open.md).) The far landmark is
the Eagle Nebula (M16/NGC 6611): a star-forming region in Serpens Cauda
in the Sagittarius Arm, ~7,000 light-years out on the ESO scale
(SIMBAD-based estimates run ~5,700 light-years), home of the Pillars of
Creation — the tallest pillar running about 4 light-years base to tip —
lit by the young massive cluster NGC 6611 (1–2 million years old,
~8,100 stars, brightest member HD 168076 near 80 Suns). Our own address resolves here too: the Sun sits
~26,000 light-years (~8 kpc; maser parallaxes 8.15 +/- 0.15 kpc,
geometric star-orbit fits 8.12-8.18 kpc) from the galactic center on
the inner edge of the Orion Spur — a ~20,000-light-year-long,
~3,000-light-year-wide segment between the Perseus and
Scutum–Centaurus arms (pitch ~10-12 degrees), read as a branch of
Perseus or an independent orphan segment — roughly halfway between
center and rim, inside the ~1,000-light-year-wide, ~14-million-year-old
Local Bubble cavity whose expansion drives nearly all star formation
near us today. Sources:
ladder R5 anchors; Wikipedia "Milky Way", "Pleiades", "Eagle Nebula"; ESO
Eagle Nebula eso0926a and Pleiades b11 pages; L04
[spirals.md](../../L04/documents/spirals.md) (Milky Way home) and
[dwarfs.md](../../L04/documents/dwarfs.md) (Magellanic companions).

## What changes at this zoom

**From coins to countryside.** At L4 a galaxy is one object with a type
label — barred spiral, elliptical, irregular. At L5 each spiral opens into
terrain: luminous arms a few thousand light-years wide winding around a
central bar and bulge, dark dust lanes edging the arms' inner rims, glowing
star-forming clouds studding them like lanterns, and clusters scattered
through — blue-white open clusters in the disk, red ancient globulars
swarming the halo (see [arms.md](./arms.md), [clouds.md](./clouds.md), [open.md](./open.md)).

**From one cell to hundreds of portals.** The L4 cell opens into L5 through
32 markers per cell for a poor group like ours, up to 2,000 for a rich one
like Virgo, each showing its L5 cell at a true size ratio of 3.24e-3
([ladder](../../../../docs/universes/ladder.md), R6 table: 10^(18.49 - 20.98)).
Populations (field objects shown as points) never open; only portals do
(ADR 0010). The crossing spans 2.49 decades (anchors e 20.98 down to
e 18.49) through two invisible magnification milestones — silent exact
no-ops the dive pushes by proximity to the targeted portal (ladder gap
note); the mechanics live in [entry.md](./entry.md). The parent's siblings
hang 1/ratio cells away (about 309 L5-cell widths); nothing shallower is
drawn. Our path runs through the home spur,
the Orion Spur, with the Eagle and Orion cloud complexes as the far
star-forming landmarks. Sources: ladder R6 amendment; ladder gap note; ADR 0010 (portal vs
population).

**From architecture to weather and work.** L4 shows why each galaxy looks
the way it does — disks built by settling gas, ellipticals puffed by
mergers, nuclei lit by infall. L5 shows the work in progress: clouds
collapsing into protostars, massive stars sculpting pillars and blowing
bubbles, clusters dispersing their siblings back into the field over
hundreds of millions of years while globulars keep their 12-billion-year
formation record (see [disk.md](./disk.md)). The black hole is still there, 26,000
light-years away at the center, but here the story is the building site,
not the building. Sources: NASA star-formation pages; L04
[nuclei.md](../../L04/documents/nuclei.md) (Sgr A* home shadow).

## How the parts fit together

One assembly line runs through the part docs. Spiral arms ([arms.md](./arms.md)) crowd
gas into ridges; the ridges collapse into giant molecular clouds
([clouds.md](./clouds.md) — Orion, Carina); clouds hatch open clusters ([open.md](./open.md) —
Pleiades, Hyades) that disperse over hundreds of millions of years; ancient
globulars ([globulars.md](./globulars.md) — 47 Tuc) keep the 12-billion-year formation
record; and the disk, bulge, bar and diffuse gas ([disk.md](./disk.md)) hold the whole
line. The dated version of this story runs through [lifetime.md](./lifetime.md), and the
dive that brings you here is in [entry.md](./entry.md).

The countryside below is surveyed, not scenery: Gaia's billion-star
velocity maps (DR3) trace the disk's rotation directly — young blue
stars drawing a crisp wavy run of proper motion with longitude while old
stars smear it — and Gaia open clusters still outline the arms, while
ALMA's PHANGS survey maps cold carbon-monoxide gas at ~100-parsec
resolution in ~90 nearby star-forming galaxies (~100,000 giant clouds),
showing the same cloud-arm patterns run everywhere. The survey currency
lives in [entry.md](./entry.md#survey-context), the arm fits in

## Key numbers

- L4 span: 10^20–10^21 m; anchor Milky Way 100 kly (9.46 x 10^20 m; D25 ~87,400 ly).
- L5 span: 10^17–10^19 m; anchor molecular-cloud complex 100 pc (3.09 x 10^18 m).
- Milky Way: ~100,000 ly across, 100–400 billion stars, barred spiral (SBbc).
- Andromeda: ~2.5 Mly away (765 kpc), bright disk ~152,000 ly; approach ~110 km/s; merger only ~50% within 10 Gyr (Sawala et al. 2025).
- Local Group: 134+ known members within 1 Mpc of center, ~16.7 Mly tip to tip (5.11 Mpc); lobes ~800 kpc apart.
- Sun: ~26,000 ly (~8 kpc) from center, inner edge of the Orion Spur (~20,000 ly long, ~3,000 ly wide).
- Eagle Nebula: ~7,000 ly away (ESO; SIMBAD-based estimates run ~5,700 ly); pillars lit by cluster NGC 6611 (1–2 Myr, ~8,100 stars).
- Pleiades: ~444 ly away, 1,000+ stars, ~115–125 Myr old, ~43 ly tidal span (Hyades at ~153 ly is the nearest open cluster).
- L4 to L5 ratio: 3.24e-3, 32 markers per L4 cell (2,000 rich); span 2.49 decades (e 20.98 to e 18.49); siblings ~309 L5-cell widths away.
