Giant molecular clouds are the
cold nurseries of the galaxy: dark, dusty masses that glow where newborn
stars light them up. This page covers what they look like, how they live
and break apart, and the three showcase regions for L5 — Orion, Carina
and the Eagle Nebula (M16).

# Clouds — giant molecular clouds and star-forming regions

A giant molecular cloud (GMC) is a cold (about 10-20 K), mostly
molecular-hydrogen congregation tens of parsecs across and weighing ten
thousand to a few million Suns. Only the densest parts are truly
molecular: cold gas traced by carbon monoxide under dust shielding, with
the coldest star-forming gas under one percent of the interstellar-medium
volume. Inside, gravity pulls the densest pockets down into protostars,
and the hottest newborn stars sculpt what is left into pillars, walls
and glowing bubbles. Every star in the disk — including the Sun —
started in one of these clouds. Sources: Wikipedia "Molecular cloud";
Wikipedia "H II region" (GMC precursors at 10-20 K).

## What it looks

In visible light a star-forming cloud is a split personality: black
dust lanes and globules silhouetted against pink and red glowing gas.
The glow is an H II region — hydrogen ionized by ultraviolet light from
young massive stars — while the dark parts are cold molecular gas and
dust thick enough to blot out the stars behind them. The red comes
mostly from H-alpha at 656.3 nm (with H-beta near one third as strong);
typical H II gas sits near 10,000 K, spans one to hundreds of
light-years, and ranges from ultra-compact cores under a light-year
across to giant complexes hundreds of light-years wide. Zoom closer and
the edges resolve into pillars and finger-like globules pointing away
from the brightest stars, dense tips that resist erosion while thinner
gas boils off around them. The bright rims are ionization fronts eating
into cold molecular material; the wavy red edges seen in infrared are
jets, outflows and bow shocks from stars still forming inside. In
infrared the same scene turns inside out: the dust glows with heat and
thousands of hidden newborn stars shine through. Sources: Wikipedia
"H II region" (H-alpha, 10,000 K, sizes, Strömgren spheres); NASA Webb
Pillars NIRCam 2022 (wavy red jet edges, semi-transparent pillars).

### Pillars, globules and photoevaporation

Pillars are the erosion balance made visible. Ultraviolet light and
winds from nearby O and B stars ionize the cloud face, and the ionized
gas boils off in photoevaporative flows. Dense knots resist longest and
stand out as columns pointing away from the ionizing cluster, with
bright ionized rims on the star-facing side. The tallest pillar in the
Eagle Nebula runs about 4 light-years base to tip; the Carina
"Defiant Finger" Bok globule holds at least 6 Suns and is expected to
evaporate in 200,000 to 1,000,000 years.

Tucked on pillar surfaces and tips are evaporating gaseous globules
(EGGs): dense knots where protostars keep accreting even as their
cocoons boil away. Chandra X-ray maps of the Eagle show young X-ray
stars scattered randomly rather than lined up on the pillars, so most
EGG protostars are still too cool to shine in X-rays. Radiation can both
destroy and trigger: squeezed globules can be tipped into collapse even
as the wider cloud is dispersed, which is why pillars host jets and
bow shocks side by side with erosion. Sources: NASA Hubble Eagle pillars;
Wikipedia "Eagle Nebula" (EGGs, 4-ly pillar, Chandra result); Wikipedia
"H II region" (Bok globules, triggered collapse); Wikipedia "Defiant
Finger" via "Carina Nebula" (mass, evaporation time).

## How it changes over time

A cloud lives fast by galactic standards: about ten to a few tens of
millions of years from assembly to dispersal. Only around one to two
percent of a cloud's mass ever becomes stars — about 2 percent in Milky
Way estimates, about 1 percent in the 2026 108,466-cloud PHANGS lifetime
study — once that fraction is reached, ultraviolet light and stellar
winds from the newborn massive stars ionize the surroundings and boil
the rest away in so-called champagne flows. Ionization fronts start supersonic, slow to subsonic, and are
overtaken by the expansion shock; radiation pressure, winds and then
supernovae after 1-2 million years finish the dispersal. The leftover
gas cools, mixes back into the between-arm gas,
and later gathers into new clouds — the recycling loop of the disk.
Old massive stars are the tell: stars older than about ten million
years are found with no cloud material left around them, because the
cloud that made them is already gone. H II lifetimes run a few million
years with under 10 percent efficiency before dispersal. Source:
Wikipedia "Molecular
cloud" (formation and destruction, lifespan); Wikipedia "H II region"
(origin and lifetime, under-10-percent efficiency); Bazzi et al. 2026
via Phys.org (1 percent over lifetime).

## How it is created

Clouds gather when large-scale gas flows collide or when a patch of
the galactic gas layer grows dense enough for its own gravity to take
over and pull in its neighbors — gravitational instability, the fast
route that fits inside a cloud's short lifetime. Shocks from supernovae,
cloud collisions and magnetic interactions can tip a stable cloud into
collapse. The contracting gas
cools, molecules (traced by carbon monoxide) form under the dust's
shielding from starlight, and the cloud fragments into clumps about a
parsec across, then into dense cores a tenth of that size. Each bound
core collapses, heats toward fusion, and becomes a protostar still
feeding from the envelope around it. The most massive cores finish
first and immediately start destroying the nursery that made them: the
ionizing star lights an H II region whose overpressured ionized volume
expands, and the first supernovae follow in 1-2 million years.
Sources: Wikipedia "Molecular cloud" (structure, formation, star
formation); Wikipedia "H II region" (GMC collapse, ionization-front
expansion).

## How it interacts with the others

- **With the arms:** clouds gather preferentially in and just past the
  spiral arms, where the gas piles up (see `arms.md`); the arm passage
  time of under ten million years sets the cloud clock. The Eagle (M16)
  in the Sagittarius Arm and the Orion complexes in the Local Spur are
  the spur-to-arm examples lit by the same compression.
- **With open clusters:** a cloud's bound cores hatch the young
  clusters — Trumpler 14 and 16 in Carina, NGC 6611 in the Eagle, the
  Trapezium in Orion — and the clusters' radiation then disperses the
  leftover gas (see `open.md`). At least half the Orion Nebula's young
  stars still hold gas-and-dust disks (proplyds), many massive enough
  for planetary systems.
- **With the disk gas:** dispersed cloud gas rejoins the diffuse disk
  and halo circulation, cools, and feeds the next generation of clouds
  (see `disk.md`).
- **With the timeline:** cloud birth, star birth and dispersal are the
  dated opening stages of the L5 story (see `lifetime.md`).

Sources: Wikipedia "Molecular cloud" (spiral-arm correlation, cluster
formation); L05 `previous-l4.md` (what carries over into L5).

## Examples and key numbers

GMCs as a class span roughly 5 to 200 parsecs across with typical
masses of ten thousand to several million Suns. Diameters of 15-600
light-years are the textbook GMC range. Each Example object below names
its Source.

- **Eagle Nebula (M16 / NGC 6611 / IC 4703)** — the pillar textbook,
  about 5,700 light-years (1,740 parsecs) away in Serpens in the
  Sagittarius Arm, some 70 by 55 light-years in gas with a 15
  light-year cluster core. The ionizing cluster holds about 8,100 stars
  concentrated in a gap northwest of the Pillars, dominated by the
  binary HD 168076 (O3.5V plus O7.5V, about 80 Suns, up to a million
  solar luminosities), at 1-2 million years old. The H II emission
  region is catalogued IC 4703; a northeastern spire runs about 9.5
  light-years long. Hubble made the three Pillars of Creation famous in
  1995 and revisited them in 2014; Webb's 2022 NIRCam view turned them
  semi-transparent, counted far more embedded stars, and caught
  supersonic jet bow shocks glowing red in molecular hydrogen. A past
  supernova shattering the Pillars was suggested from Spitzer hot gas in
  2007, but the 20-year Hubble baseline shows steady evaporation with
  at least 100,000 years of pillar lifetime left. Sources: Wikipedia
  "Eagle Nebula"; NASA Webb Pillars NIRCam 2022; ESA/Hubble heic0506b
  (stellar spire).
- **Orion complex** — the nearest major star-forming region,
  1,000–1,400 light-years away (head of Orion A about 400 parsecs,
  tail out to about 470), hundreds of light-years across, with stellar
  ages up to 12 million years. Orion A alone weighs on the order of
  100,000 Suns and has made some 3,000 young stars in the last few
  million years (about 190 protostars plus about 2,600 pre-main-sequence
  stars in the VISION census). Its bright face is the Orion Nebula (M42/M43), lit by
  the Trapezium's four heavy stars. Behind the visible nebula sit OMC-1
  through OMC-4 with the Becklin-Neugebauer object and Kleinmann-Low
  Nebula; Orion B (about 420 parsecs, 100,000 Suns) holds the Flame
  Nebula, Horsehead and M78; the OB1 association steps from 8-10 Myr
  (OB1a) down to under 2 Myr (OB1d); and the whole complex is rimmed by
  the Orion-Eridanus superbubble with Barnard's Loop. True Orion A
  length is about 290 light-years, not the 130 seen in projection.
  Sources: Wikipedia "Orion
  molecular cloud complex"; ESA/Hubble heic0601a (Orion Nebula page);
  Bally 2008 Orion overview; Grossschedl et al. 2018-2019 (3D shape,
  young stellar objects).
- **Carina Nebula (NGC 3372)** — one of the largest star-forming
  regions in our sky, about 8,500 light-years (2,600 parsecs) away in
  the Carina–Sagittarius Arm, some 230 light-years in radius. It hosts
  the volatile hypergiant Eta Carinae (100–150 Suns of mass), the dark
  Keyhole Nebula, the photoevaporating Defiant Finger globule, and the
  young clusters Trumpler 14 (half a million years old, ~2,000 stars)
  and Trumpler 16. The Mystic Mountain pillar stands 3 light-years
  tall with jet-driving protostars on its peaks; the northwest edge
  holds NGC 3324 and its Cosmic Cliffs cavity wall 7,600 light-years
  away, where Webb's 2022 first-image NIRCam frame caught pillars,
  bubbles, blow-outs, protostellar gold jets and hundreds of hidden
  stars plus background galaxies. A deep dive into that frame later
  found dozens of energetic jets and outflows previously buried by dust.
  The pictured ESO wide field covers about 72 light-years.
  Sources: Wikipedia "Carina Nebula"; ESO eso0905a (object table and
  caption); ESA/Webb weic2205a (Cosmic Cliffs); Hubble 20th-anniversary
  Mystic Mountain.
- **Cloud lifetimes** — assembled and destroyed on roughly 10–30
  million-year timescales; star formation converts about 1-2 percent of
  the mass before feedback clears the rest. Growth alone runs about 20
  million years under 100,000 Suns and up to 100 million years for the
  heaviest clouds, fastest in centers (about 16 million years). Free-fall
  5-20 million years; depletion billions of years; shear and orbital
  60-200 million years. Sources: Wikipedia
  "Molecular cloud"; Wikipedia "H II region" (few-Myr H II lives, under
  10 percent efficiency); Bazzi et al. 2026 via Phys.org.

### Cloud rules and clouds in other galaxies

Bigger clouds stir faster. On average the internal velocity spread
grows roughly as the square root of cloud size, a sign of supersonic
turbulence inside, and most clouds sit near virial balance, where
gravity pulling in roughly matches internal motion pushing out. Mean
density falls roughly as one over size, so mass grows roughly as size
squared — that is, clouds have roughly similar surface densities,
with plenty of scatter from place to place. In words: line width scales
with size, clouds are roughly virialized, and column density is roughly
constant. The relations hold with real scatter because environment,
feedback and magnetic support shift individual clouds off the mean.
Source: Larson (1981)
scaling relations as summarized in the RAA review.

The same patterns show up beyond the Milky Way. ALMA's PHANGS survey
mapped cold carbon-monoxide gas at about 100-parsec resolution in
some 90 nearby star-forming galaxies, cataloguing on the order of
100,000 giant clouds. The 2021 census (Leroy et al., 90 galaxies) found
stellar nurseries are not all alike: clouds in dense galaxy centers run
more massive, denser and more turbulent than clouds in quiet outskirts,
and the neighborhood — bar, arm, inter-arm, center — measurably shifts
where and how many stars are born. The 2026 follow-up (Bazzi et al.,
108,466 clouds in 66 galaxies, JWST plus ALMA with the giant-cloud mass
function model) timed growth directly: clouds under 100,000 Suns form in
about 20 million years, heavier clouds in up to 100 million years;
centers grow fastest at about 16 million years, 5-10 million years
faster than arms, inter-arms or disks; free-fall runs 5-20 million
years against 60-200-million-year shear and orbital times, so clouds
live and die faster than the galaxy turns beneath them. The cloud mass
spectrum shifts with environment, and only about one percent of a
cloud's gas becomes stars over its life. Sources: ALMA Observatory
PHANGS census press release (2021, Leroy et al.); Phys.org summary of
the 2026
108,466-cloud PHANGS lifetime study (Bazzi et al., arXiv:2604.24759).

### What JWST changed since 2022

Three Webb views rewrote the close-up. In the Eagle, NIRCam's
semi-transparent pillars exposed hundreds of hidden stars and red
molecular-hydrogen jet shocks only a few hundred thousand years old,
sharpening star counts and gas-and-dust budgets for feedback models. In
Carina's Cosmic Cliffs (NGC 3324 edge), NIRCam resolved the cavity wall
into pillars, bubbles, a top-center blow-out, gold protostellar jets
and background galaxies through the veil, with early star formation
lasting only 50,000-100,000 years per star. In Orion's Bar, the PDRs4All
team caught the first methyl cation (CH3+) in the d203-506 disk,
validating 1970s carbon-chemistry predictions: ultraviolet light that
was assumed purely destructive actually powers CH3+ formation and
rewrites disk chemistry (no water detected there, unlike shielded
disks). Together they show feedback sculpting, triggering and chemically
resetting nurseries at the same time. Sources: NASA Webb Pillars NIRCam
2022; ESA/Webb weic2205a (Cosmic Cliffs); ESA/Webb weic2315 (Orion Bar
CH3+, PDRs4All, Nature 2023).
