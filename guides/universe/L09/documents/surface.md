# The surface — the face of the star

The surface is the
face of L9: the photosphere with its granulation, sunspots,
faculae, limb darkening, and spin. This page covers how it looks,
how it changes over time, how it was created, and how it
interacts with the rest of the star and its planets. The engine
below is in [interior.md](./interior.md); the crown above is in
[atmosphere.md](./atmosphere.md); the eruptions it launches are in
[activity.md](./activity.md).

## How it looks

The Sun has no solid surface — it is a ball of plasma — and the
part we call its surface is the photosphere, the layer that emits
most of the visible light at about 10,000 degrees F (5,500 degrees
C). It is a thin skin by solar standards: NASA's facts page quotes
about 250 miles thick, while the Marshall solar-physics pages quote
about 100 km thick. Read both figures as the same order of
magnitude — a few hundred kilometres against a 700,000 km radius —
not a surveyed boundary, since the exact depth depends on which
optical-depth definition of "surface" is used.

It is tiled by granules: small cellular features about 1,000 km
across covering the whole disk except where spots sit. Granules
are the tops of convection cells where hot plasma rises in the
bright centers, spreads across the surface, cools, and sinks back
along the dark intergranular lanes. An individual granule lives
only about 20 minutes before it is pushed aside by newly emerging
ones, so the whole tiling boils continuously. Flows inside
granules can top 7 km/s — supersonic, fast enough to generate
acoustic noise that rings the Sun as surface oscillations — while
the dark lanes host tiny bright points where bundled magnetic field
collects. At coarser Doppler resolution a larger tiling shows
through: supergranules about 35,000 km across, lasting a day or
two with roughly 0.5 km/s flows that sweep field bundles to cell
edges and build the chromospheric network above.

Sunspots are the dark marks: cooler regions where concentrated
magnetic field lines, thousands of times stronger than Earth's
field, hold back the heat. Temperatures in the dark centers drop
to about 3,700 K against about 5,700 K for the surrounding
photosphere (around 6,000 degrees F against 10,000), so spots
glow dimmer rather than truly black. A mature spot shows a dark
central umbra, where the field is strongest and most vertical, and
a lighter filamentary penumbra, where it is weaker and more
horizontal. Spots usually arrive in bipolar groups — one set with
north polarity, the companion with south — last days to months
(large ones for weeks), and range from about 1,000 to 100,000
miles across. Viewed near the limb the depressed umbral floor is
seen partly edge-on (the Wilson effect), a direct hint that the
cooler, more transparent spot atmosphere sits lower than its
surroundings.

Near the edge of the disk the view slants through only the upper,
cooler, dimmer gas — that is limb darkening, why the Sun's rim
looks darker. Looking at disk center peers straight down into
hotter, brighter depths; looking at the limb peers diagonally
through the thin layer and samples only its higher, cooler
reaches. Bright faculae obey the opposite geometry: bundles of
field much narrower than a spot's, brightest when seen near the
limb where their hot walls face the observer. Spots darken the
Sun, faculae brighten it — and over a sunspot cycle the faculae
win: the Sun measures about 0.1 percent brighter at sunspot
maximum than at minimum.

## How it changes over time

The face turns, but not as one piece. At the equator the Sun
rotates once in about 25 Earth days, at the poles once in about
36 — the polar zones take more than 30 days while the equatorial
belt takes about 24. Seen from moving Earth this appears as about
27 days, which is why older texts quote 27: sidereal spin versus
synodic appearance. The spin was first found by watching spots
drift across the disk after the telescope reached the Sun in 1610,
and continuous daily spot counts from Zurich (1849 onward,
extended back to 1610) remain the longest consistent solar record.
Modern Doppler timing from the GONG network and from SOHO's
Michelson Doppler Imager, now continued by SDO's HMI, separates
rotation from the weaker superposed motions: convective and
oscillatory signals near 300 m/s against the roughly 2,000 m/s
equatorial rotation, plus a feeble roughly 20 m/s meridional drift
from equator toward the poles. The spin axis itself tilts about
7.15 degrees to the ecliptic, so September shows more of the north
pole and March more of the south. Sharpness keeps improving from the
ground too. The Inouye Solar Telescope's first light (January 2020,
at 789 nm) resolved the surface at 30 km — Texas-size boiling
cells with bright magnetic specks glittering in the dark lanes,
the finest solar image ever taken at the time, covering about
36,500 km square. Its first sunspot image followed the same month
(taken January 28 with the wavefront-correction context viewer at
530 nm, released December 2020): streaky hot and cool gas
spidering from the dark center, penumbral grains and umbral dots
evolving over about ninety seconds, at about 2.5 times the
previous best resolution — magnetic structure down to about 20 km.
Even the dark lanes of that spot exceed 7,500 degrees F; the field
holds heat back but never switches it off. Hinode's Solar Optical
Telescope remains the complement in orbit: the first spaceborne
instrument to map the photosphere's vector magnetic field.

Watchers keep the long record: SOHO marked 25 years in
orbit on December 2, 2020 — designed for two, still going, having
observed full versions of two whole cycles. The Inouye program
then entered survey science: eight Cycle-1 images released in May
2023 (Visible Broadband Imager) sampled sunspots and quiet Sun
together, including pores that never grew penumbrae and
chromospheric fibrils rooted in photospheric field, some frames
coordinated with Parker Solar Probe and Solar Orbiter. In
September 2024 the same telescope produced its first coronal
magnetic-field maps with the Cryo-NIRSP instrument via the Zeeman
effect — the crown's field, kept on [atmosphere.md](./atmosphere.md).
In April 2025 its final first-generation instrument, the Visible
Tunable Filter built by Freiburg's KIS institute, saw first light
on a sunspot cluster at 10 km per pixel in the sodium 588.9 nm
line, promising full velocity-plus-field maps once commissioning
completes; and in June 2025 the sharpest surface view yet resolved
ultra-fine striations only about 20 km wide — Manhattan-scale
bright and dark stripes rippling across granule walls like curtains
in wind, where light crossing sheet-like field brightens or dims
with local field strength, matching state-of-the-art
magnetoconvection simulations in the G-band.

Every 11
years or so the spot count runs its cycle from spotless minimum to
freckled maximum, and spots migrate as it runs: bands first form at
mid-latitudes, widen, then drift toward the equator — the butterfly
pattern traced from Greenwich data since 1874. Numbering began with
Cycle 1 in 1755; Cycle 24 ran December 2008 to December 2019 with
peak in April 2014, and Cycle 25 opened at the December 2019
minimum. The 2019 panel predicted a weak-to-average climb peaking
near 115 smoothed spots in mid-2025, but the Sun ran hotter than
forecast from 2021 onward: NASA and NOAA announced the maximum
phase on October 15, 2024, with elevated activity expected into
2026 — and the most violent outbursts often land on the long
shoulder after peak, as the flare log in [activity.md](./activity.md)
shows. Historical minima run deeper still: from about 1645 to 1715
almost no spots appeared at all, the Maunder minimum overlapping
the Little Ice Age. On the longest clock the face is middle-aged:
our star is a 4.5-to-4.6-billion-year-old G2 V yellow dwarf a
little less than halfway through its roughly ten-billion-year main
sequence. The dated version runs through [lifetime.md](./lifetime.md).

## How it is created

The face is the top of the boiling zone. The interior's regions
run core, radiative zone, convection zone — the full engine is in
[interior.md](./interior.md) — and moving outward, the visible
photosphere is next. At the convection zone's base, near 2,000,000
degrees C, heavy ions hold electrons and trap radiation until the
fluid boils; rising bubbles of hot plasma carry the energy the
last stretch to a 5,700 K surface at a density of only about
2×10⁻⁷ g/cm³, where the gas finally turns transparent enough for
most of the Sun's radiation to escape outward. That escaping light
reaches Earth as sunlight about eight minutes after it leaves. The
whole stack dates to the same birth: the solar nebula collapse 4.6
billion years ago that put 99.8 percent of the system's mass into
the Sun, told in full in [lifetime.md](./lifetime.md).

Sources: NASA Sun in-depth page; NASA Sun facts page; NASA solar
interior page.

## How it interacts with the others

Light is the first interaction: the photosphere's energy is why
life as we know it could exist on Earth, and its distance sets the
habitable zone — the range where liquid water can exist — with
Earth sitting comfortably inside. Its brightness is not perfectly
steady: dark spots subtract light while bright faculae add it, and
the faculae win by about 0.1 percent at maximum, so total
irradiance ticks upward with the cycle. Spots are the second
interaction: sunspots mark active regions, areas of intense tangled
field where solar eruptions like flares and coronal mass ejections
typically burst, and the resulting space weather can interfere with
satellites, GPS, and radio, cripple power grids, and corrode
pipelines — the storm scales and historical hits are logged in
[activity.md](./activity.md). The face also feeds upward: what boils
here shapes the chromosphere and corona ([atmosphere.md](./atmosphere.md))
and drives the wind and weather ([activity.md](./activity.md)) —
supergranular flows gather the field into the network that roots
loops, streamers, and fast-wind lanes.

Sources: NASA Sun facts page; NASA habitable zone page; NASA
sunspots page; NASA photospheric-features page.

## Size and mass

- Type: G2 V yellow-dwarf main-sequence star; age 4.5–4.6 billion years, a little less than halfway through a roughly ten-billion-year main sequence.
- Diameter about 865,000 miles (1.4 million km); radius about 435,000 miles (700,000 km); mass over 330,000 Earths; volume 1.3 million Earths; about 100 times wider than Earth.
- Photosphere a few hundred kilometres thick (about 100 km on the Marshall scale-height definition, about 250 miles on the facts-page visible-layer definition) at 5,500 degrees C; granules about 1,000 km across with about 20-minute lifetimes; supergranules about 35,000 km across lasting a day or two; spots 1,600 to 160,900 km across lasting days to months.
- Spin: about 25 days at the equator, about 36 near the poles (about 27 days seen from Earth); equatorial surface speed about 2,000 m/s; spin-axis tilt about 7.15 degrees.
- Sharpness ladder: 30 km Inouye first light (2020), about 20 km first sunspot and 2025 striations, 10 km per pixel VTF sampling.
- Distance to Earth: 93 million miles (150 million km), exactly 1 AU; about 8 light-minutes away.

Sources: NASA Sun facts page; NASA Sun by-the-numbers page; NASA
photospheric-features and surface-flows pages; NSO Inouye releases
(2020, 2023, 2025).

## Example objects

Example features: a large sunspot group (cooler magnetic patch with
dark umbra and filamentary penumbra, HMI view); photospheric
granulation (convection tops, 20-minute Texas-size cells with
magnetic bright points in the lanes); supergranulation (Doppler-only
tiling feeding the chromospheric network); limb faculae (bright
magnetic bundles, hot-wall brightening); the darkened limb itself
(slant view into cooler gas); 2020 Inouye granulation and sunspot
fields; 2025 striations (20 km magnetic curtains on granule walls).
Example numbers: one 11-year cycle (Cycle 25 maximum phase October
2024, active into 2026); one 25-day equatorial spin; one 0.1-percent
brightness swing won by faculae.
