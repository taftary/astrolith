# The interior — the engine of the star

The interior is the
engine of L9: the core, the radiative zone, and the convection zone
where the Sun's light is born and begins its long climb out. This
page covers how it looks in cutaway, how it changes over time, how
it was created, and how it interacts with the rest of the star. The
bridge behind us is in `previous-l8.md`; the dive that brings you
here is in `entry.md`.

## How it looks

Cut the Sun open and four regions show (three bulk layers plus
the thin interface between the outer two). At the center sits the
core, the innermost 25 percent, where the temperature is about 15
million degrees C (27 million degrees F) and the density is about
150 g/cm³ — roughly ten times the density of gold or lead —
nuclear burning is almost completely shut off beyond
its outer edge, about 175,000 km from the center, where the
temperature has halved and the density has fallen to about
20 g/cm³. Around it lies the
radiative zone, from 25 percent of the way to the surface out
to 70 percent, where the temperature falls from 7,000,000 degrees C
to about 2,000,000 degrees C and the density drops from about
20 g/cm³ (near gold) to only 0.2 g/cm³ (less than water),
and energy is carried by light itself:
photons bouncing from particle to particle — so many scatterings
in the dense plasma that one photon needs on the order of a
million years to cross this zone (NASA's facts page quotes about
170,000 years for the core-to-surface-top leg; read both figures
as the same long random-walk delay, not a single timed path).
Outside that runs the
convection zone, the outermost 30 percent, from a depth of about
200,000 km right up to the visible surface — at its base the
2,000,000-degree plasma is cool enough for heavy ions (carbon,
nitrogen, oxygen, calcium, iron) to hold some electrons, which
traps radiation, makes the fluid unstable, and starts the boil:
rising bubbles of
hot plasma carrying the energy the last stretch to a 5,700 K
surface at a density of only 0.0000002 g/cm³. Between the two
sits a thin interface layer, the tachocline, where the Sun's
magnetic field is thought to be generated — shear flows across it
stretch field lines stronger, and chemical composition appears to
change suddenly across it.

## How it changes over time

The engine burns steadily but not forever. Nuclear reactions
consume hydrogen to form helium through the proton-proton chain —
in three steps: two protons make deuterium plus a positron and a
neutrino; a proton plus deuterium makes helium-3 plus a gamma ray;
two helium-3 nuclei make helium-4 plus two protons — and the Sun
fuses about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen every
second, of which about four million tonnes vanish into pure
energy (luminosity 3.8 × 10^26 W) — and has done so since it was born, around 4.6 billion
years ago. A second furnace burns beside the first: the
carbon-nitrogen-oxygen (CNO) cycle, hydrogen fusion catalyzed by
carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen, contributes only about 1 percent of
the Sun's energy — but dominates in stars heavier than about
1.3 solar masses. Its neutrinos went unseen until Borexino's
Gran Sasso detector, thermally stabilized 2015–2019 to pin down
bismuth-210 background, caught the few-counts-per-day excess
(Nature, November 2020, better than 5 sigma) — the first direct
proof of the Bethe–von Weizsäcker cycle. The full Phase-III
data set (PRL, December 2022, about 7 sigma; rate 6.7 counts per
day per 100 tonnes, flux 6.6 × 10^8 cm^-2 s^-1) turned the
signal into a metallicity meter: with the beryllium-7 and
boron-8 fluxes as a thermometer, the C+N abundance disfavors the
low-metallicity solar model at 3.1 sigma in favor of the
high-metallicity one, and a 2023 Cherenkov-directionality
reanalysis of all Borexino data (2007–2021) confirmed it without
background assumptions. The climb of each photon outward takes on the order of
hundreds of thousands of years (NASA's interior page gives about a
million years across the radiative zone; the facts page gives
170,000 years core-to-top-of-convection — read both as the same
long random-walk delay, not a single number). On the longest clock the fuel
runs down: the Sun is a little less than halfway through its life,
with about 5 billion years left before it swells into a red giant
and ends as a white dwarf. The dated version of that story runs
through `lifetime.md`.

Sources: NASA Sun facts page; ESA Sun page; NASA solar interior page; NASA Cosmicopia Ask-Us (600 Mt/s fused, ~4 Mt/s to energy); Borexino CNO evidence (Nature 2020) and Phase-III update (PRL 2022) plus CID reanalysis (PRD 2023).

## How it is created

The interior was built the day the Sun lit. About 4.6 billion
years ago a giant spinning cloud of gas and dust — the solar
nebula — collapsed under its own gravity; most of the material was
pulled toward the center to form the Sun, which accounts for 99.8
percent of the solar system's mass, while the leftovers spread
into a disk of gas and dust that made the planets. When the center
grew hot and dense enough, fusion ignited — and the layered engine
above has run ever since.

Sources: NASA Sun facts page.

## How it interacts with the others

Everything above is fed from below. Energy from the core is
carried outward by radiation, then by boiling convection, and the
nuclear reactions where hydrogen fuses into helium power the Sun's
heat and light — the light the surface (`surface.md`) shines with
and the habitable zone lives on. The same layered shear makes the
weather: the dynamo that builds the Sun's field lives in the
tachocline, winding poloidal field into toroidal belts as
differential rotation drags the equator around the poles (the
omega effect — a north-south line wraps once around in about
8 months), then twisting rising flux tubes back toward the poles
(the alpha effect, the source of Joy's tilt and the 22-year Hale
reversal). Buoyant flux that would escape a convective dynamo too
fast is parked in the stable layer instead — the interface-dynamo
idea — while the slow meridional return flow (about 1–2 m/s
inside, equatorward over roughly 11 years) paces the sunspot
bands. Two subtler exports escape: the
ghost particles called neutrinos, made in the same reactions,
pass right through the overlying layers — about 66 billion cross
each square centimeter of Earth every second — and reach us in
about 8 minutes with news of the core's present state, while the
photon heat is still on its hundred-thousand-year climb. Yet for
decades only about a
third of the expected electron neutrinos showed up — the solar
neutrino problem, open since Davis's Homestake chlorine tank
(1968, about one-third of prediction) and confirmed by Kamiokande
(1988, first directional proof they come from the Sun, about half
the expected flux) plus the GALLEX and SAGE gallium experiments
at low energies. The solvers
have names: Sudbury's 1,000 tons of heavy water (SNO, announced
June 18, 2001) saw one-third in electron flavor
while Super-Kamiokande saw about half (about 45 percent at high
accuracy, June 2000) with partial sensitivity to the other
flavors, and the difference was the
smoking gun — the total across all three flavors matched the Sun's
model, so the missing
ones had changed identity in flight (neutrino oscillation:
vacuum oscillation at low energies, matter-enhanced MSW
conversion at high energies; Nobel 2002 to Davis and Koshiba,
Nobel 2015 to McDonald and Kajita; KamLAND's reactor
antineutrinos closed the loop independently). Super-Kamiokande even images the Sun
in neutrinos, straight from the core (1996–2018 sky map). Watchers probe all of it
without going inside: helioseismology reads the interior the way
geologists read the Earth with seismic waves — patches of
photosphere bob up and down on a 5-minute period (found 1962,
explained 1970 as trapped sound, confirmed 1975), millions of
pressure-driven p-modes ringing at once, some diving through the
core and some bending back shallow, so timing them maps
temperature, density, composition, and flow versus depth. Six
identical GONG
telescopes ringing the globe for round-the-clock Sun sound, joined
by SOHO's Michelson Doppler Imager (plus GOLF and VIRGO) and now SDO's HMI watching the
full disk at 6173 Å — rotation inversions from these data show
the surface shear running inward and vanishing at the convection
base (the dashed line in Thompson's classic plot). Below that the
radiative zone turns as a near-solid body at about 430 nHz down
to at least 0.2 solar radii; p-modes barely reach deeper, so the
core spin is still open. A 2017 claim from 20 years of SOHO/GOLF
data (Fossat et al.) of a core spinning 3.8 times faster
(one-week period) via g-mode imprints on p-modes did not survive
independent reanalysis — read it as contested, not as fact.
Cleaner new ears opened in 2021: global inertial (toroidal)
modes, restored by the Coriolis force on rotation-period
timescales — high-latitude, critical-latitude, and equatorial
Rossby families — peak near the convection-zone base and already
cap deep superadiabaticity and turbulent viscosity an order of
magnitude below mixing-length lore, implying a barely unstable
lower convection zone where a flux-transport dynamo can park
field. Gravity modes themselves remain unconfirmed
(quarry since the 1980s South-Pole campaigns), though a 2025
simulation predicts a mixed f/g mode ringing hourly with
30 times the core-rotation sensitivity of any observed p-mode —
the most promising future probe. The composition the waves move
through is itself disputed: 3D-spectroscopy metallicities split
low (AAG21, Z/X about 0.0187) versus high (MB22, Z/X about
0.0225, back near the 1990s values), envelope inversions (Buldgen
et al., 2024) favoring low while Borexino's CNO neutrinos favor
high — the solar abundance problem, honestly open, with opacity
and transport degeneracies shared between the camps.

Sources: NASA Sun facts page; NASA solar interior, dynamo, and
helioseismology pages; Super-Kamiokande solar-neutrino pages;
Nobel Prize missing-neutrino review (Bahcall).

## Size and mass

- Whole star: diameter about 865,000 miles (1.4 million km); radius about 435,000 miles (700,000 km); mass over 330,000 Earths; volume 1.3 million Earths.
- Core: innermost 25 percent, about 175,000 km from center to edge (NASA's facts page alternatively quotes about 138,000 km thick — read both as the inner quarter-to-fifth, not a surveyed boundary); 15 million degrees C, halving to about 7,000,000 at the edge; density about 150 g/cm³, falling to about 20 g/cm³ at the edge.
- Radiative zone: 25 to 70 percent of the way out; 7,000,000 down to 2,000,000 degrees C.
- Convection zone: outermost 30 percent, from about 200,000 km deep to the surface; about 2,000,000 degrees C at its base.

Sources: NASA Sun facts page; ESA Sun page; NASA solar interior page; Gizon et al. inertial modes (A&A 2021); Buldgen et al. envelope metallicity (A&A 2024).

## Example objects

Example layers: the fusing core (pp-chain furnace plus a
1-percent CNO side-fire); the radiative
zone (photon random-walk); the tachocline (dynamo seat); the
boiling convection zone (granulation roots, inertial-mode
resonator). Example numbers: one
600-million-tonnes-fused, 4-million-tonnes-to-energy burn rate; one million-year photon
climb against one 8-minute neutrino dash; one red-giant fate.
