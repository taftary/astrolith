# The companions — the company of the star

Companions are the
company of L9: the same planets as nearby points, the large moons,
the dust and sungrazers, and the rare stellar companions — with
the preview toward L10. This page covers how the company looks,
how it changes over time, how it was created, and how it interacts
with the star. The voice that shapes it is in `activity.md`.

## How it looks

From star-zoom the system shrinks to company. The eight planets
stand as nearby points at 0.39 to 30.1 AU: Mercury, Venus, Earth
with its Moon — 384,400 km out, 3,474 km across, tidally locked so
the same face always points home (the L10 dive) — Mars, then
Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune — the
inner four roughly to scale with each other, the outer four
roughly to scale with each other. Around the giants ride swarms of
moons like miniature systems of their own — Mercury and Venus
alone have none — with Ganymede larger than the planet Mercury and
Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto the great four. Between the points
hangs the zodiacal glow: sunlight reflected by a cloud of tiny
dust particles orbiting the Sun. Through the glare dive the
sungrazers: comets passing very close to the Sun like small solar
probes through its atmosphere — plunging through it they trace
its structure the way probes would. On March 25, 2024 Sungrazer
volunteer Hanjie Tan, a doctoral student in Prague who has found
more than 200 comets since age 13, spotted the 5,000th comet in
SOHO/LASCO data: a short-period Marsden-group body related to
96P/Machholz, one of only about 75 Marsden members among the 5,000
(most SOHO finds overall are Kreutz sungrazers). Never designed as
a comet hunter, SOHO is now the most prolific finder in history,
with more than half of all known comets found in its watch, most of
them by volunteers with no formal training. And beyond them all,
rare stellar company: more than half of all stars in the sky have
one or more partners — and company is not always one star plus
planets, as Kepler-16b proves: a Saturn-size circumbinary world
orbiting two suns on a 229-day year, a real Tatooine minus the
desert. Our own Sun travels alone, its nearest
neighbor the triple Alpha Centauri system, with Proxima Centauri
4.25 light-years out hosting at least one planet, the
1.27-Earth-mass Proxima b on an 11-day year inside its star's
habitable zone — found in 2016 by the Pale Red Dot HARPS wobble
watch (11.2-day signal, 7 million km out, 5 percent of an AU),
though frequent red-dwarf flares may have stripped
any atmosphere it once held. Forty light-years out, the
TRAPPIST-1 ultra-cool dwarf holds seven Earth-size rocky worlds,
lettered b through h, three of them in its tight habitable zone —
the most thoroughly characterized exoplanets, weighed against each
other with Spitzer's gravitational tugs. Webb is now reading their
air: TRAPPIST-1 b runs about 500 kelvins (450 F) with likely no
significant atmosphere, c about 380 kelvins (225 F) with an
extremely thin veil at best, and preliminary 2025 data show d
likewise apparently airless — with the outer, more temperate
planets still to come, so the habitability verdict stays open.

## How it changes over time

The company keeps time by Kepler's law: the square of a period
grows with the cube of its distance. Mercury laps the Sun in 88
days, Earth in 365, Saturn in 10,747 — and Neptune needs 59,800
days, about 164 years. A close planet has a short year, a far one
a long one; that single rule spaces the whole company. Where
stellar companions exist they waltz slower still: Alpha Centauri A
and B take 80 years per orbit, while Proxima needs more than half
a million years to circle the pair. Beyond the Sun the count keeps
climbing: thirty years after 51 Pegasi b (October 1995), NASA's
confirmed-exoplanet tally passed 6,000 in September 2025 and then 6,300, with
more than 8,000 further candidates awaiting confirmation in the
NASA Exoplanet Archive — only three years after the 5,000 mark.
Rocky worlds look
more common across the galaxy than our evenly split solar system
suggests, alongside hot Jupiters inside Mercury's distance,
circumbinaries, rogue starless worlds, pulsar planets around dead
stars, lava worlds, Styrofoam-light giants, and gemstone-cloud
skies — and fewer than 100 seen directly, the rest caught by
transits and other indirect winks, with Webb already reading more
than 100 atmospheres. Roman, launched August 30, 2026, should add
thousands more by microlensing plus a coronagraph try at Jupiter
analogues, pathfinding toward a Habitable Worlds Observatory for
true Earth twins. On the longest clock the
company thins: when the Sun swells red the inner points fall
silent — the dated endgame is in `lifetime.md`.

Sources: NASA orbits-and-Kepler page; NASA years-on-other-planets
page; NASA nearest-star page; NASA 6,000-exoplanets milestone;
NASA exoplanets overview.

## How it is created

The company is leftover Sun-stuff. Even though the Sun gobbled up
more than 99 percent of the disk, material remained — and some of
it grew big enough to become spheres: planets and dwarf planets.
That is why the same 4.6-billion-year birthday runs through every
point of company. Stellar companions form their own ways — disk
fragmentation, core fragmentation, dynamical capture — and capture
happens small too: Triton is thought to be a Kuiper Belt object
caught by Neptune's gravity, the exception that proves the
co-formed rule. Between planets and stars sit the brown dwarfs,
13 to 80 Jupiter masses: too heavy to be planets, too light for
sustained hydrogen fusion, glowing almost only in the infrared.
Some collapse like stars from gas clumps yet never ignite; others
may assemble like planets in disks around stars — which is why the
same object can read as either a failed star or a giant companion,
hunted in the infrared and by volunteers in Backyard Worlds.
Most moons and all eight planets grew up in the
disk; Triton was adopted.

page; NASA Triton page; NASA star-types page (brown and red dwarfs).

## How it interacts with the others

Gravity is the first interaction: it holds every planet in orbit
around the Sun. Wind is the second: Jupiter's magnetosphere, like
Earth's, deflects the solar wind but gets pushed around by its
gusts — stretched into a windsock tail reaching Saturn's orbit —
and shock waves from solar outbursts brighten Jupiter's polar
auroras, while Saturn's auroral storms answer mainly to solar-wind
pressure. Light is the third: the habitable zone it sets — the distance
where liquid water could pool on a surface, Goldilocks not too hot
nor too cold — picks out Earth, sitting comfortably inside. Every
star has one, wider for hot stars and tight for dim red dwarfs, the
most common stars at about 75 percent of the sky; their close-in
planets take hundreds of thousands of times Earth's X-ray and UV
dose, and young red-dwarf flares can sterilize a toehold biosphere.
Some researchers favor orange K dwarfs as the true Goldilocks
stars, steady for tens of billions of years. And the company points
onward:
at least one planet, possibly more, orbits Proxima — and the next
dive after this company overview is Earth with its Moon (L10), the
only planet-and-moon pair with humans.

## Size and mass

- Planets: Mercury 0.39 AU to Neptune 30.1 AU; five officially named dwarf planets (Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, Eris); hundreds of moons and thousands of asteroids and comets.
- Years: Mercury 88 days; Earth 365 days; Saturn 10,747 days; Neptune 59,800 days (about 164 years).
- Moons: Ganymede larger than Mercury; Galilean four Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto; latest NSSDC tally Jupiter 95, Saturn 274, Uranus 28, Neptune 16; Pluto has five moons including Charon, so large it makes Pluto wobble.
- Stellar company: more than half of all stars have partners; nearest neighbor Proxima Centauri 4.25 light-years; Alpha A+B nearest Sun-like pair at 4.3 light-years, 80-year orbit.

Sources: NASA NSSDC planetary fact sheet; NASA Space Place pages;
NASA multiple-star page.

## Example objects

Example company: Earth with its Moon (next dive, L10); Jupiter
with the Galilean four (mini system); Ganymede (moon bigger than a
planet); Triton (the captured exception); Kepler-16b, 245
light-years out, the 2011 Saturn-size gas giant on a 229-day year
around two suns — no surface to stand on, dry-ice cold, seen from a
hypothetical moon under two shadows; TOI-1338 b, the first TESS
circumbinary (2019); Proxima b (nearest exoplanet, 11-day
super-Earth in the zone); TRAPPIST-1 b through h (seven rocky
Earth-size worlds, Webb reading their air); SOHO-5000
(volunteer-spotted Marsden sungrazer). Example numbers: one 88-day year; one 164-year year; one
4.25-light-year neighbor; one 6,000-exoplanet census (September 2025).
