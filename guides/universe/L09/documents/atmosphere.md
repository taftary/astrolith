# The atmosphere — the crown of the star

The atmosphere is
the crown of L9: the chromosphere, the transition region, and the
corona with prominences, coronal holes, and spicules. This page
covers how it looks, how it changes over time, how it is created,
and how it interacts with the heliosphere and the planets. The face
below is in `surface.md`; the wind it feeds is in `activity.md`.

## How it looks

Above the photosphere the air stacks in three shells. First the
chromosphere, a thin reddish layer (hydrogen H-alpha glow, hence
"color-sphere") between about 250 and 1,300 miles (400 to 2,100 km)
up, warming from about 4,000 K at its base (the temperature minimum)
to about 8,000 K at its top in the IRIS layer model — broader
chromospheric summaries extend the top toward about 20,000 K where
dynamic fibrils and spicules overshoot. Then the transition region,
a thin irregular skin only about 60 miles (100 km) thick where the
temperature leaps from about 8,000 K to about 500,000 K (up to about
1,000,000 degrees in hotter patches), glowing in ultraviolet only
reachable from space — the prime IRIS target. Hydrogen is fully
ionized there, so the glow comes instead from highly ionized carbon,
oxygen, and silicon (C IV, O IV, Si IV near 100,000–200,000 K),
mapped by SMM, SOHO/SUMER, TRACE, and IRIS. Then the corona:
super-heated at 500,000 K and past 1,000,000 degrees up to a few
million K, extending more than a million km out with no fixed upper
edge — million-degree heat sustained far above the cool surface,
while just 1,000 miles below
sits the 5,500-degree-C surface. Read it three ways at once:
white-light crown at eclipse (pearly streamers, plumes, and loops,
cycle-shaped); emission-line crown of highly ionized iron and
calcium in coronagraphs; and X-ray crown across the disk, where
Skylab first revealed coronal holes and bright points and
Yohkoh, SOHO, TRACE, and SDO now watch daily. The crown carries structure:
prominences as great red-glowing looped walls of plasma — charged
hydrogen and helium flowing along tangled dynamo-built field —
anchored in the photosphere and looping hundreds of thousands of
miles into the corona (seen as dark filaments against the disk;
prominence and filament are one thing viewed limb versus on-disk).
A prominence forms over about a day and a stable one can persist for
months, with quiet quiescent weeks giving way to eruption in minutes
to hours when its loops turn unstable; chromospheric plage (bright
H-alpha patches around spots) and the Ca II K network tracing
supergranule boundaries mark the same magnetic web from below.
Coronal holes
as large dark regions in ultraviolet and X-ray where thin plasma
lets fast wind escape; and spicule jets throughout the network —
typically 20–30 km/s rising threads lasting only a few minutes,
with faster surges toward about 100 km/s (about 60 miles per second)
in the tall type, thousands of miles long, up to about 10 million
present at once. Between those landmarks runs the loop architecture:
helmet streamers capping active regions with prominences at their
bases and wind-drawn pointed peaks above; polar plumes on open polar
field; and dense coronal loops tying spot to spot for days to weeks
— with flare-associated loops flashing far shorter. IRIS (2013,
20-cm UV telescope, 150-mile resolution on 1 percent of the disk at
a time) stares at exactly this interface to ask which nonthermal
energy dominates, how the chromosphere feeds mass and energy upward,
and how emerging flux triggers flares and ejections.

## How it changes over time

The crown breathes with the 11-year cycle. Coronal holes can form
at any time but grow prominent near solar minimum, when persistent
holes settle near the poles and migrate to lower latitudes; during
minimum they can outlast many rotations. Eclipses give the honest
portrait: the corona shows only when the Moon blocks the disk (or
a coronagraph does), as a pearly white crown whose shape follows
the spot cycle — and during the few minutes of totality almost
nothing changes. On the longest clock the crown has shone for 4.6
billion years and will keep shining about 5 billion more; the dated
story is in `lifetime.md`.

Sources: NASA SDO coronal-hole pages; NASA corona and
coronal-feature pages; NASA photojournal PIA17669 (March 2013 hole,
May 2013 Saturn auroras).

## How it is created

The crown is heated from below, and how exactly is still open.
Two families of ideas compete: wave heating (ion-cyclotron and
plasma waves dumping energy) and small-flare heating (nanoflares
and campfires via reconnection). Constant boiling below generates
tangled magnetic islands; when field lines snap back they expel
plasma and energy upward — the
birth of spicules and a share of coronal heat. But the full feat —
a million-degree crown over a 5,500-degree surface — remains one
of the greatest unanswered questions in astrophysics: no one can
really explain how this corona exists, and without a permanent
heating mechanism the plasma would cool within about an hour. Two
new clues arrived by spacecraft. In 2021 Parker Solar Probe became
the first craft ever to touch the Sun — flying through the corona
on April 28 at 18.8 solar radii (about 8.1 million miles), it crossed
the Alfvén critical surface where the atmosphere ends and the solar
wind begins. The surface proved wrinkled, not smooth: Parker slipped
in and out through spikes and valleys, threading a quiet
pseudostreamer eye where particles slowed and switchbacks dropped.
Closer passes traced those magnetic switchbacks — S-shaped kinks
plentiful near the Sun — back to photospheric funnels between
supergranules, a candidate source region for fast-wind plasma.
And Solar Orbiter's first images (EUI at 77 million km, May 2020)
revealed omnipresent miniature flares
dubbed campfires near the surface — over 1,500 flickering
brightenings, 400 to 4,000 km wide, living 10 to 200 seconds, a
million to a billion times smaller than Earth-seen flares — with
component reconnection at work that could help hold the corona's
heat. Modeling (Chen et al., EGU 2021) reproduces campfire
brightenings through component reconnection at very small angles —
near-parallel field lines breaking and rejoining — plus untwisting
flux ropes in some cases, with enough released energy in the model
to maintain coronal temperatures. SDO triangulation puts campfires
only a few thousand km above the photosphere, lower than first
assumed, and joint EUI–PHI–SPICE observations were tasked to test
the heating share directly. IRIS added the complementary smoking gun:
nanojets imaged in 2014 coronal rain (Nature Astronomy 2020) —
bright thin streaks across loop field, reconnection avalanches
consistent with nanoflare heating in simulation, with SDO and Hinode
confirming the full-disk context. Frequency and total-energy share
remain open, which is why Parker, Solar Orbiter, and IRIS are read
as one campaign. The moss roots sharpen the picture: Hi-C plus IRIS
(Nature Astronomy 2024) show patchy moss around spot-group centers,
straddling chromosphere and corona beneath coronal loops, heated
from 10,000 F toward 1,000,000 F by tangled-field currents in
addition to downward heat from the overlying corona — a piece, not
the whole. For Parker's later record passes (3.8 million miles,
430,000 mph from December 2024, sampling quiet through maximum into
decline), see `activity.md` and keep this page on the boundary
crossed, not the flight log. Write the puzzle as narrowing, not solved.

Sources: NASA coronal-heating research; NASA IRIS nanojets (2020)
and moss-roots (2024) pages; NASA Parker pages; ESA campfire pages;
NASA SDO and Hinode pages.

## How it interacts with the others

The corona is where the solar wind originates: it continues outward
as a supersonic stream of plasma permeating the entire solar
system — humans live well within the extended atmosphere of our
Sun. Coronal holes are the fast lanes: their open field lines send
material speeding out at about 700 km per second, and where a fast
stream piles into slower wind the squeeze can slam into planetary
magnetic bubbles — one such stream lit bright auroras at Saturn.
Erupting prominences are the loud exits: when a loop goes unstable
and bursts outward it can detach as a coronal mass ejection, the
space-weather driver covered in `activity.md`, while the steady
wind holds open the whole heliosphere met up close in
`companions.md` and far away in L8.

Sources: NASA solar-wind and Parker pages; NASA photojournal
PIA17669; `activity.md` for the wind log and storm scales.

## Size and mass

- Chromosphere: about 250 to 1,300 miles (400 to 2,100 km) above the photosphere; about 4,000 K at the base rising to about 8,000 K at the top (to about 20,000 K in dynamic overshoot).
- Transition region: about 60 miles (100 km) thick; about 8,000 K to about 500,000 K (hotter patches toward 1,000,000 degrees; C IV, O IV, Si IV near 100,000–200,000 K).
- Corona: from about 1,300 miles up, 500,000 K past 1,000,000 degrees up to a few million K, extending more than a million km (large structures about a solar radius tall; the eye sees only within about 2 solar radii at eclipse); one solar radius is about 700,000 km; Earth sits about 200 solar radii out; Alfvén critical surface near 10–20 solar radii (Parker crossed at 18.8 on April 28, 2021).

Sources: NASA layers-of-the-Sun page; NASA chromosphere, transition-region, and corona pages; NASA Parker pages.

## Example objects

Example features: an eruptive prominence (looped plasma wall, also
a filament on-disk; forms in about a day, lasts months); a polar
coronal hole (fast-wind source); a spicule swarm (10 million jets at
once); helmet streamers and polar plumes (closed versus open field);
dense coronal loops (days to weeks) and flare loops (minutes); moss
patches at loop feet (Hi-C plus IRIS); campfires (EUI miniature
flares) and nanojets (IRIS reconnection streaks); an eclipse crown
(pearly white, cycle-shaped). Example numbers: one 700-km-per-second
fast stream; one million-degree puzzle; one Saturn aurora display
(March 2013 hole, May 2013 arrival).

Sources: pages named above.
