# Lifetime — the dated life of a star like ours

This page is the
dated timeline running through L9: from molecular-cloud collapse
through the long main-sequence life to the red-giant swelling and
the white-dwarf fate. Read it with the part docs: each stage names
where it shows — `interior.md` for the engine, `surface.md` for
the face, `atmosphere.md` for the crown, `activity.md` for the
voice, `companions.md` for the company. Dates carry sources; where
no fetched page gives a date, the stage says so instead of
inventing one.

## How to read the dates

All calendar dates count from the birth of the solar system,
defined by the first solids: calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions
(CAIs) in primitive meteorites at 4,567.30 ± 0.16 million years
ago (weighted mean of four U-corrected Pb–Pb ages from Allende
and Efremovka; Connelly et al. 2012). That value was ratified in
2022 as the formal base of the Hadean eon on the geologic time
scale, and reaffirmed in the 2024 ratification paper. A newer
statistical cross-calibration of Al–Mg against Pb–Pb in quickly
cooled achondrites argues the true t-zero could be about
4568.4–4568.7 Ma — roughly 1.4 Myr older — with CAI Pb–Pb clocks
reset by later transient heating; this doc keeps the ratified
4,567.3 Ma as the anchor and notes the debate where it matters.
"Years ago" means before the present; "+X Gyr" means X billion
years from now; durations are stage lengths, not calendar dates.
A parallel debate (low- versus high-metallicity solar models,
envelope inversions versus Borexino CNO neutrinos) leaves the
deep composition honestly open — see `interior.md` — but does not
move any date below by more than the stated rounding.

This is the Sun-like track only: stars of about 8 solar masses or
less climb the red-giant branch, flash helium, pulse on the
asymptotic giant branch, shed a planetary nebula, and settle as
white dwarfs. Heavier stars burn onward through carbon, neon,
oxygen, and silicon to an iron core and a supernova — the onion
layers and day-long silicon burning belong to that other road
(CSIRO high-mass post-main-sequence page) and are named here only
to keep the fork explicit. Low-mass dwarfs under about 0.5 solar
masses never ignite helium at all and simply fade. Our Sun walks
the middle path.

## Stage 1 — Cloud collapse and protostar (about 4.6 billion years ago, tens of millions of years)

Stars form in large clouds of gas and dust called molecular
clouds — giant molecular clouds of 100,000 to 2 million solar
masses, 10–30 K, threaded with H2, CO, NH3, and methanol, studied
at millimetre CO wavelengths; dense knots collapse when a trigger
(supernova shock, spiral density wave, nearby OB-star radiation)
pushes them over the edge, fragmenting into clusters rather than
single stars. Gravity pulls clumps together until a protostar — a baby
star — grows inside, at first heated only by the collapse itself,
shining in the infrared while still shrouded in dust. A star like our Sun takes around 50 million
years to collapse (a 15-solar-mass star manages it in only a
hundred thousand; half-solar-mass stars can take twice as long as
ours). The Hayashi pre-main-sequence track for one solar mass
runs four steps: a large cool protostar ~20 Sun-diameters wide at
~100 times its future main-sequence luminosity; rapid contraction
with falling luminosity as radiated energy escapes; core reaching
10 million K so hydrogen fusion starts and slows the fall over
about 10 million years while the track edges up-left; then
hydrostatic balance and settling onto the main sequence over a
few tens of millions of years. The young Sun blew its leftovers
away with an early solar wind, leaving the disk that made the
planets. Young Sun-like stars pass a wild
T-Tauri youth — irregular, variable, shedding mass at up to
500,000 km/h (10^-7 to 10^-8 solar masses per year, up to a solar
mass over the phase) for perhaps 10 million years, lithium-rich
and fast-spinning, sitting just right of the main sequence —
though no fetched page
dates our Sun's own T-Tauri years; read them as early, not dated.
Chondrules — the melted droplets that built planetesimals —
started forming at the same time as the CAIs 4,567 Myr ago and
kept forming for about 3 Myr (disk-lifetime scale), so Stage 1
closes with the disk already cooking rock.

Sources: NASA stars page; NASA Sun in-depth page; CSIRO stellar
formation page; Connelly et al. Science 2012 (CAI 4567.30 Ma,
chondrule span ~3 Myr); ICS Hadean-base ratification (2024).

## Stage 2 — Main sequence, the long noon (4.6 billion years past, about 5 billion to go)

Our Sun is roughly midway through its main-sequence stage —
formed 4.6 billion years ago, now counted 4.5 billion years old
(NASA uses both figures on the same page; this doc uses 4.6 for the
formation date and 4.5–4.6 for today). Stars like it burn nine or
ten billion years in all — about 5 billion years still to go before
it becomes a white dwarf — brightening slowly as it ages (about
10 percent per billion years; the main-sequence band is broad
because stars climb it). The young noon was dimmer: three billion years ago
the Sun shone 70 percent as bright as today (the faint-young-Sun
puzzle; no fetched page numbers its birth brightness, so none is
claimed). Two 2026 NASA-funded answers now share the stage.
First, Airapetian's chamber-plus-model result: daily superflares
on the toddler Sun (Kepler shows young Sun-like stars erupting
almost daily) fired protons into N2–NH3–CO2–CO air and made
nitrous oxide, 300 times more potent than CO2 — even 10 percent
survival against the young UV still warms the equator to about
5 degrees C, above freezing and ideal for prebiotic chains
(Astrophysical Journal Letters 2026). Second, the SHIELD digital
twin (Opher et al., Annual Review of Astronomy and Astrophysics,
August 2026): the heliosphere was crushed inside Earth's orbit at
least three times in the last ~14 Myr — about 2–3, 6–7, and 13–14
Myr ago — by cold interstellar clouds, matching interstellar-dust
elements in seabed, Antarctic snow, and lunar cores, and possibly
driving ice-age swings. Through these billions the face (`surface.md`) spun and
spotted, the crown (`atmosphere.md`) breathed its cycle, and the
company (`companions.md`) kept Kepler time — while life on Earth
read the light. Looking forward rather than back: at ~10 percent
brighter per Gyr the habitable zone leaves 1 AU in about a
billion years (Schroeder & Smith 2008: inner edge crosses Earth;
by solar age 10 Gyr the zone sits 1.29–1.86 AU over a still-1-AU
Earth) — the end of Earth's habitability long precedes the red
giant itself.

Sources: NASA Space Place Sun-age page; NASA Sun in-depth page;
NASA faint-young-Sun research page (Airapetian N2O 2026);
NASA SHIELD heliosphere-path review (Opher et al. ARA&A 2026);
Schroeder & Smith MNRAS 2008 (HZ drift, 10 percent per Gyr).

## Stage 3 — Red giant, the swelling (about 5–7.6 billion years hence; 0.5–1.2 AU depending on model)

When the core hydrogen runs out the Sun starts to die by swelling:
hydrogen shell-burning dumps helium ash onto a contracting,
degenerate core; the envelope expands and cools while luminosity
climbs ~1,000-fold. Two sourced numbers bracket the tip, and both
are kept because they come from different model classes. The
CSIRO teaching track gives radius approximately 0.5 AU (~100
times today) at up to ~1,000 times brighter (about 2,000 times in
popular count), big enough to engulf Mercury
and Venus, and possibly Earth as well. The calibrated Schroeder
& Smith evolution model (MNRAS 2008, Schroeder–Cuntz mass loss)
gives tip-RGB at solar age 12.17 Gyr — 7.59 ± 0.05 Gyr from now —
with R = 256 solar radii = 1.2 AU, L = 2,730 Suns, Teff 2,602 K,
after losing 0.332 solar masses on the climb at up to
2.5 × 10^-7 solar masses per year. The wind turns heavy either
way: giants
shed about 10^-7 solar masses per year against today's 10^-17.
Earth's verdict in the detailed model is engulfment just before
the tip (~500,000 years early, 1.0 Myr after Venus and 3.8 Myr
after Mercury), because tidal drag and chromospheric friction
beat the orbital expansion from mass loss (1.50 AU conservative,
1.37–1.63 across calibrations); a planet starting at 1.15 AU or
more would survive. By the tip the habitable zone has fled to
49–71 AU, deep in Kuiper territory. Read the CSIRO 0.5 AU as the
classroom value and the 1.2 AU as the tested-model value; both
agree Mercury and Venus fall and Mars survives.

Sources: NASA Sun in-depth page; CSIRO post-main-sequence page;
NASA Space Place Sun-age page; Schroeder & Smith MNRAS 386 (2008)
(tip-RGB 1.2 AU at 12.17 Gyr, engulfment 7.59 Gyr from now).

## Stage 4 — Helium burning and the second giant (core 100 million K; about 100–130 million years; out to 1.5 AU)

At 100 million K the ash itself catches: helium fuses into carbon
through the triple-alpha process (two alphas make unstable
beryllium-8, a third alpha lands before it decays — carbon-12 plus
gamma; some carbon plus alpha gives oxygen-16, the main cosmic
source of both elements in our bodies), starting with a sudden helium
flash. In stars under ~2–3 solar masses the degenerate core
ignites in minutes to hours and the flash races through the whole
core; heavier stars ignite gradually with no flash. The star steadies onto the horizontal branch — helium
burning in the core, hydrogen in a shell — with enough helium fuel
for about 100 million years (130 Myr in the Schroeder & Smith
timing), luminosity roughly constant while the envelope shrinks
and heats. Then it climbs again: the asymptotic
giant swells possibly to 1.5 AU, the orbit of Mars, blazing up to
10,000 times today's luminosity, pulsing every 10,000 to 100,000
years (thermal pulses of a couple of centuries each, Mira-type
variables swinging up to 10 magnitudes with radius doubling) and shedding 10^-6 up to 10^-4 solar masses per year.
Each pulse's neutron captures run the slow s-process up to bismuth
(83 protons); deep convection dredges carbon, oxygen, and
s-process metals to the surface, so soot and silicate dust shroud
the star and it shines brighter in the infrared than in visible
light. No
fetched page dates the flash itself in calendar billions — durations
only, honestly kept — but the sequence RGB tip → ~10^8-yr
horizontal branch → AGB is the dated order.

Sources: CSIRO post-main-sequence page; Schroeder & Smith MNRAS
2008 (130-Myr He-burning phase).

## Stage 5 — Planetary nebula and white dwarf (shedding about 40 percent; nebula visible some 20,000 years)

All outer layers blow away into an expanding cloud of dust and gas
called a planetary nebula — Herschel's 18th-century "planet-disc"
misnomer for something unrelated to planets — the Helix, 650 light-years away, is a
typical example — visible only some 20,000 years before dispersing
(over 1,500 known in our galaxy; 0.1–0.2 solar masses at
Earth-vacuum densities, tens of km/s expansion; the blue-green is
doubly-ionized oxygen O III). The trigger is shell alternation:
when the helium shell runs low the hydrogen shell contracts,
ignites, dumps ash that re-ignites the helium shell in a flash —
each pulse ejecting more envelope until the hot core stands
exposed. Initially above 30,000 K and up to 100,000 times today's
luminosity, the central star ionizes the shell like an emission
nebula, then fades up to 90 percent within a century. What stays is the core: a white dwarf, a roughly Earth-sized cinder
(a half-solar-mass one spans 1.9 Earth radii; 1.0 solar mass 1.5;
1.3 solar masses 1.4 — more massive means smaller, held by
electron-degeneracy pressure, not heat) at a million times
the density of water (10^9 kg/m^3), after shedding about 40 percent of its mass
(more for heavier progenitors). Above 1.4 solar masses of remnant
the electrons lose and collapse continues to neutron star or black
hole — the Chandrasekhar limit, after Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar;
no white dwarf above it has ever been found. Only progenitors up
to ~8 solar masses end this way (a 2-solar-mass star leaves ~0.7);
white dwarfs are ~10 percent of galactic stars, the near ones
Sirius B and Procyon B in binaries.
The endpoint image below is that shed shroud, the fate every part
doc points to. Outer survivors (Mars outward, orbits widened
~1.85-fold by the halved Sun) keep circling the cinder; the
dissolution of the outer system by resonances and stellar flybys
plays out only over ~100 Gyr (Zink et al. 2020) — far beyond this
stage.

## Stage 6 — White dwarf cooling and the far future (tens to hundreds of billions of years; no black dwarfs yet)

The cinder cools: tens to hundreds of billions of years to radiate
away its heat and sink to a black, inert clump of carbon — and the
universe is not yet old enough for that to have happened, so every
white dwarf from single stars is still white. Gaia made the
cooling visible: DR2's Hertzsprung–Russell pile-up (Tremblay et
al. Nature 2019) sits where carbon–oxygen cores crystallize from
the inside out, latent heat plus carbon–oxygen phase separation
stalling cooling by ~1 Gyr — the Q branch. The stall is longer
than textbooks said. Cheng et al. (2019) showed ~6 percent of
high-mass (1.08–1.23 solar) white dwarfs linger ~8 Gyr extra,
old by velocity dispersion but young by light; Blouin's
distillation mechanism (Nature 2024, Bedard et al.: buoyant
neon-22-poor crystals float up, heavy liquid sinks, gravitational
energy released) halts cooling for billions of years — steady
luminosity past some main-sequence stars — with merger-enriched
neon as the fuel. Standard-composition delays are shorter (~0.5
Gyr past 50 percent crystallized in 40-pc-volume modeling, 2025)
but the direction is one way: white-dwarf clocks run slow, and
population ages read from them must add the stall. Meanwhile the
oldest single-star white dwarfs known — the M4 globular-cluster
faint ones near 30th magnitude, ~12 Gyr — still shine.
Past that the fetched
pages date nothing; the far future stays open on the record. What
is certain is the shape of the whole: collapse over tens of
millions of years, a ten-billion-year noon, twin giant ascents,
a twenty-thousand-year shroud, then a cooling that outlasts the
universe so far.

Sources: CSIRO low-mass-death page; Tremblay et al. Nature 2019
(Gaia crystallization pile-up); Cheng et al. ApJ 2019 (8-Gyr
Q-branch delay); Blouin–Bedard et al. Nature 2024 (neon-22
distillation); 40-pc volume population synthesis 2025 (0.5-Gyr
standard delay).

## Cross-check

Collapse 50 Myr + main sequence ~10 Gyr + horizontal branch 100
Myr + pulses every 10–100 kyr + shroud 20 kyr + cooling 10^10+ yr:
every number above names its page; undated stages say so in
place. No stage contradicts another doc's numbers. Model fork
kept explicit: CSIRO classroom tip-RGB 0.5 AU versus
Schroeder–Smith calibrated tip-RGB 1.2 AU at 12.17 Gyr with
Earth engulfed 7.59 Gyr from now — same fate for Mercury and
Venus, same survival for Mars and outward, differing only in how
far the photosphere reaches. CAI anchor fork likewise explicit:
ratified 4567.30 Ma used for all calendar dates; 4568.7 Ma
statistical alternative noted, shifting nothing by more than
~1.5 Myr.
