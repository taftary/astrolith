# Comets — traffic plus the heliosphere edge

This is the traffic of the outer solar system: short-period comets, Halley-type comets, long-period comets from the Oort cloud, Centaurs crossing between the giant planets, and three confirmed visitors from other stars.
It ends at the heliosphere boundary, where the Sun's wind gives way to interstellar space.

## How it looks

A comet far from the Sun looks like a dark, lumpy ball of ice and rock. Near the Sun it grows a glowing head (coma) and one or two long tails: a dust tail and a gas tail pushed away from the Sun.

![Comet 67P in colour from Rosetta OSIRIS, August 2014 (screensize)](../images/comet-67p-esa.jpg)

Most comets here are small and very dark. Halley's nucleus reflects only about 3% of the light that hits it. Comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko looks like a grey double-lobed rock about 4 km across in Rosetta images.

Centaurs look like a halfway stage: redder, asteroid-like bodies with faint comet activity when they come closer in. Interstellar visitors look different from each other. 1I/'Oumuamua showed no coma or tail at all and appeared as a small, elongated, reddish object about 400 m long. 2I/Borisov looked like a normal comet, with a clear coma and tail. 3I/ATLAS looks like an active comet too, with a coma and two tails unlike 'Oumuamua; Hubble observations bound its nucleus at 0.44–5.6 km (August 20 2025), and Webb spectra found carbon dioxide, water, carbon monoxide, carbonyl sulphide, and water ice in its coma. Post-perihelion Webb MIRI spectra (December 15–16 and 27 2025, at 329M and 379M km from the Sun) added the first direct methane detection on an interstellar visitor, with carbon dioxide unusually abundant versus typical solar-system comets; water vapour spreads wide because much of it leaves icy grains in the coma, while carbon dioxide and methane hug the nucleus, and water output fell fastest as the comet receded. June 2026 analysis of post-perihelion Webb spectra (published in Nature) found deuterium about 30 times enriched versus solar-system comets with only traces of carbon-13, pointing to formation 10–12 billion years ago when galactic star formation was at its height — likely the oldest comet material yet sampled.

The heliosphere itself is invisible, but NASA draws it as a bubble with layers: termination shock inside, heliosheath in the middle, heliopause at the edge, then interstellar space. NASA's IBEX mission — a small explorer mapping the boundary from Earth orbit with energetic-neutral-atom cameras — found a bright, winding ribbon of unknown origin bisecting its first all-sky map in 2009; its evolving shape over 11-plus charted years constrains the boundary models the Voyagers sample in place.

Sources: NASA comets overview (`https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/comets/`); NASA 1P/Halley (`https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/comets/1p-halley/`); NASA 67P (`https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/comets/67p-churyumov-gerasimenko/`); ESA colour image of comet (`https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2014/12/Colour_image_of_comet`).

## How it changes over time

A comet brightens near the Sun and fades far away. Ice turns to gas, dust is released, the coma and tails grow, then shrink again on the way out. Each trip wears the nucleus down by metres.

Halley-type comets like 1P/Halley loop every few decades on steep, often retrograde paths. Halley averages about 76 years (74–79 years between returns because of planetary tugs), last seen in 1986, next expected in 2061. Its orbit is tilted 18 degrees and runs backward around the Sun, stretching past Neptune to about 35 AU at aphelion. With each return a Halley-size nucleus loses an estimated 1–3 m of surface material, so an average periodic comet survives roughly 1,000 trips; Halley has held its present orbit for at least 16,000 years with no obvious fading yet. Jupiter-family comets like 67P loop faster, steered by Jupiter. 67P takes 6.45 years, and its perihelion has stood near 1.3 AU only since a 1959 kick from Jupiter; before 1840 it never came closer than about 4.0 AU and grew no visible tail.

Centaurs change orbits quickly on cosmic timescales. Pulled by the giant planets, a Centaur can become a Jupiter-family comet, be thrown outward, or be ejected.

Interstellar visitors pass through once and never return. 'Oumuamua was discovered in October 2017 already leaving. Borisov was discovered in August 2019, reached perihelion in December 2019 at about 2 AU from the Sun, and then left. 3I/ATLAS was found on July 1 2025 already inbound by the ATLAS survey in Chile, about 670M km out inside Jupiter's orbit; it passed near Mars on October 3 2025, reached perihelion on October 29–30 2025 at about 1.4 AU, came closest to Earth on December 19 2025 at about 1.8 AU (about 270M km, no hazard), passed near Jupiter in March 2026, and then left on a hyperbolic path.

The heliosphere boundary also moves. The termination shock breathes in and out with solar activity. Voyager 1 crossed it in December 2004 at 94 AU; Voyager 2 crossed it in August 2007 at 84 AU. Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause in August 2012 at 122 AU; Voyager 2 crossed it in November 2018 at 119 AU. Both Voyagers remain active in extended mission, escaping in different directions at more than 3 AU per year; power-saving work in 2026 (an instrument shutoff on Voyager 1 in April, freed power on Voyager 2 in August) keeps the interstellar plasma, field, and cosmic-ray record going.

Sources: NASA 1P/Halley, 67P, Voyager pages; ESA Rosetta timeline pages.

## How it is created

Comets are leftover ice from planet formation. Short-period comets (periods under 200 years) mostly come from the Kuiper Belt and scattered disc:

- Jupiter-family comets loop in 20 years or less after leaking through the Centaur crossing zone.
- Halley-type comets take 20–200 years on steep, often retrograde paths.
- Long-period comets come from the Oort cloud and can take up to about 30 million years per orbit.
- Centaurs are Kuiper Belt objects caught in transit between the giant planets.

There are likely billions of comets in the Kuiper Belt and more still in the far Oort shell.
Naming follows the discoverer or survey code: `67P` marks the 67th periodic comet found, `1P` the first, `C/` a non-periodic comet, and `I` an interstellar visitor (`1I`, `2I`, `3I`); 3I/ATLAS is also catalogued as C/2025 N1.

Heat creates the tails. Within a few AU of the Sun, water ice and other ices turn to gas and drag dust with them. Sunlight pushes the dust; the solar wind pushes the charged gas into a straight ion tail. On 67P, Rosetta showed the double-lobed shape controls this activity: the tilted spin keeps the north in a long 5.5-year summer while the south endures a long dark winter, then takes a brief hot summer just before perihelion that drives dust showers metres thick onto northern plains. The escaping gas also carves a magnetic-field-free cavity far larger than predicted (seen at 170 km, against 50–100 km models) and sings in plasma waves. Later analysis found a far-ultraviolet aurora, a chameleon colour cycle that fades near the Sun and reddens again outbound, cliff collapses, bouncing boulders, and a skull-ridge imprint where Philae's second touchdown pressed into ice softer than cappuccino froth.

Interstellar visitors were made around other stars and ejected. 'Oumuamua, Borisov, and 3I/ATLAS were thrown out of their home systems and happened to pass through ours; 3I/ATLAS approached from the direction of Sagittarius. They were not made by our Sun.

The heliosphere is created by the solar wind. Fast charged particles stream out from the Sun, slow suddenly at the termination shock, pile up in the heliosheath, and stop where their pressure balances interstellar pressure at the heliopause.

Sources: NASA solar-system formation, comets, and Kuiper Belt pages; ESA Rosetta backgrounders.

## How it interacts with the others

Comets connect the reservoirs. Oort-cloud nudges send long-period comets inward — some take up to 30 million years per orbit from about 100,000 AU out (see `oort.md`). Kuiper Belt leaks feed Centaurs, and Centaurs feed Jupiter-family comets. Jupiter is the main traffic cop: it captures, redirects, and sometimes ejects them. Earlier flybys set up Rosetta: NASA's Deep Space 1 passed Borrelly in 2001, Stardust flew by Wild 2 in 2004 and returned dust in 2006, Deep Impact excavated Tempel 1 in 2005, EPOXI met Hartley 2 in 2010, and Stardust-NExT revisited Tempel 1 in 2011.

Centaurs cross the orbits of the giant planets, so they interact gravitationally with Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. That is why their orbits are unstable over millions of years.

Comets also deliver material. Dust from Halley feeds two weak meteor showers each year: the Eta Aquariids in May and the Orionids in October. Rosetta showed 67P releasing water, carbon dioxide, and dust that enrich the inner solar system; its water vapour carries a significantly different deuterium signature from Earth's oceans, and the mission detected molecular nitrogen, molecular oxygen, exposed water ice, plus the amino acid glycine and phosphorus. Rosetta launched in March 2004, rendezvoused in August 2014, set Philae down on November 12 2014, escorted the comet through perihelion in August 2015, and ended with a controlled impact on September 30 2016.

The Voyagers interact with the boundary itself. Their plasma, magnetic-field, and cosmic-ray instruments measured the shock and the heliopause from the inside, then kept measuring interstellar plasma outside.

Beyond this level: the Voyagers are now in interstellar space, the preview toward L8. They still report back on interstellar magnetic field and cosmic rays.

Sources: NASA comets, 1P/Halley, and Voyager pages; ESA Rosetta results pages.

## Size and mass

Comet nuclei are kilometres or smaller. Halley is about 15 km by 8 km, often rounded to 11 km mean diameter. 67P is about 4 km across. 'Oumuamua was about 400 m long — much smaller than a typical comet nucleus. The 3I/ATLAS nucleus is bounded at 0.44–5.6 km — comet-scale, far larger than 'Oumuamua's ~400 m.

Centaurs are bigger, typically tens to ~200 km across. They bridge comet nuclei and small dwarf planets.

Masses are tiny compared to planets. A kilometre-scale comet masses roughly 10^13–10^14 kg. Even Halley masses only about 2 x 10^14 kg. Comas and tails are huge in size (coma up to hundreds of thousands of km; tails millions of km, with one dust tail pushed by sunlight and one straight ion tail pushed by the solar wind) but almost nothing in mass. 67P's porous nucleus has a density well below water and sheds about twice as much dust as gas.

The heliosphere is huge in size and almost empty in mass. Termination shock at ~84–94 AU, heliopause at ~119–122 AU from the Voyager crossings, with the nose closer and the tail stretching hundreds of AU behind.

## Example objects

- 1P/Halley — the famous retrograde visitor.
  - Period about 76 years (74–79 years between returns); retrograde orbit tilted 18 degrees, reaching past Neptune to about 35 AU.
  - Nucleus about 15 km by 8 km (about 11 km mean); albedo 0.03.
  - Last perihelion February 9 1986 at 0.59 AU; next 2061.
  - Met in 1986 by an international fleet including ESA's Giotto, which imaged the nucleus, plus Soviet Vega 1 and 2, Japanese Suisei and Sakigake, and NASA-supported ICE.
  - Its dust feeds the Eta Aquariids (May) and Orionids (October).
- 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko — the Rosetta comet.
  - Jupiter-family, period 6.45 years; discovered 1969 by Klim Churyumov and Svetlana Gerasimenko.
  - Visited by ESA's Rosetta orbiter with Philae lander in 2014; ended September 30 2016 with a controlled impact.
  - Double-lobed nucleus about 4 km across, porous with density well below water, shedding about twice as much dust as gas.
  - Water vapour differs from Earth's oceans; first-at-comet detections of molecular nitrogen and molecular oxygen, exposed water ice, plus repeated glycine detections tied to dust (October 2014, March 2015, pre-perihelion outbursts) and phosphorus.
- 1I/'Oumuamua — first confirmed interstellar visitor.
  - Discovered October 19 2017 by Pan-STARRS1; about 400 m long and about ten times as long as wide.
  - Spinning every 7.3 hours with tenfold brightness swings; reddish, inert, with no coma seen; perihelion September 9 2017, already leaving when found.
  - Slight non-gravitational acceleration hints at comet-like outgassing despite the missing dust.
- 2I/Borisov — first confirmed interstellar comet.
  - Discovered August 30 2019 by Gennady Borisov; about 975 m across.
  - Active coma and tail at about 177,000 km/h; perihelion December 2019 at ~2 AU.
  - Hubble watched a fragment break off in March 2020.
- 3I/ATLAS — third confirmed interstellar object, first active interstellar comet after Borisov.
  - Discovered July 1 2025 by the ATLAS survey in Chile at about 670M km out, inside Jupiter's orbit; hyperbolic path from the direction of Sagittarius.
  - Active coma and two tails; nucleus bounded at 0.44–5.6 km (Hubble, August 20 2025).
  - Speed rose from about 221,000 km/h at discovery to about 246,000 km/h at perihelion — the fastest visitor on record.
  - Perihelion October 29–30 2025 at ~1.4 AU, just inside Mars' orbit; passed about 29M km from Mars on October 3 2025; closest to Earth December 19 2025 at about 1.8 AU (about 270M km, no hazard); passed near Jupiter in March 2026, then left.
  - Chemistry unlike most solar-system comets: carbon dioxide unusually abundant; first direct methane on an interstellar visitor (Webb MIRI, December 15–16 and 27 2025); deuterium about 30 times enriched with only traces of carbon-13 (Nature, June 2026), pointing to formation 10–12 billion years ago.
  - Watched by a fleet: Hubble, Webb, SPHEREx, TESS, Swift, STEREO, SOHO, Parker Solar Probe, PUNCH, Lucy, Psyche, Europa Clipper, Mars orbiters and rovers (MRO, MAVEN, Mars Express, ExoMars TGO, Perseverance), plus XRISM and XMM-Newton in X-rays — the first interstellar comet seen in X-ray light.
  - ESA's JUICE craft imaged it with NavCam/JANUS from November 2025 to February 2026 (see `giants.md`); April 2026 first results measured about 2,000 kg of water vapour per second on November 2 2025, days after perihelion (high but not exceptional: 67P gave about 300 kg/s, Halley about 20,000 kg/s), mostly sunward and much of it from icy grains rather than the nucleus directly, with gas and dust reaching 5M+ km — behaving like a typical solar-system comet despite its origin.
  - NavCam tracking from a different angle than Earth also sharpened the trajectory for planetary-defence methods.
  - ESA's Comet Interceptor, now in development after Rosetta, may one day meet a pristine long-period or even interstellar comet — but 3I/ATLAS itself passed too fast and too soon for any visit.
- Centaurs — the crossing population.
  - Icy bodies orbiting between the giant planets, typically tens to ~200 km across.
  - Unstable over millions of years; future Jupiter-family comets, ejections, or Sun/planet impacts.
- Heliosphere boundary — the edge crossed in place.
  - Termination shock: Voyager 1 December 2004 at 94 AU, Voyager 2 August 2007 at 84 AU.
  - Heliopause: Voyager 1 August 2012 at 122 AU, Voyager 2 November 2018 at 119 AU.
  - Voyagers launched 1977; grand tour 1979–1989; now escaping at more than 3 AU per year on the interstellar mission outward — past the planets, away from L8's realm, into interstellar space.

Sources: NASA 1P/Halley, 67P, 'Oumuamua, Borisov, and Voyager pages; ESA Rosetta pages.
