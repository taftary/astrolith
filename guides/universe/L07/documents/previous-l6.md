# L6 behind us — Stellar neighborhood as previous level

This page is the bridge
from L6 (Stellar neighborhood) into L7 (Outer solar system): what
the previous level looks like, what carries over when you zoom in,
and what changes at L7 scale. The part docs from the loop's first
pass now exist — follow the cross-links below. Entry itself —
the visual transition and its effects — lives in [entry.md](./entry.md). The
dated timeline runs through [lifetime.md](./lifetime.md) in Round 2.

## What L6 is and how it looks

L6 is the neighbor-suns scale at 10^16–10^17 m: individual stars
with colors — blue-white Sirius-like sparks, yellow Sun-like ones,
swarms of faint red M dwarfs — plus bound doubles and triples,
cooling brown dwarfs and fading white dwarfs, drifting through thin
local gas. Its anchor is the Alpha Centauri distance, 4.37
light-years (4.13 x 10^16 m): the nearest star system, a triple —
Alpha Centauri A (Sun-like G star), B (orange K star), and Proxima
Centauri (a faint red M dwarf with the planet Proxima b, about 1.06
Earth masses on an 11.2-day orbit at 0.0485 AU, found 2016).
The bright landmark is Sirius
at 8.6 light-years: the brightest star in our night sky, a
blue-white A star with a tiny white-dwarf companion, Sirius B,
only about 7,500 miles across yet holding 98% of the Sun's mass. Our own star
sits here too: the Sun, a G2V yellow dwarf. The census rule is that
most neighbors are dim: 284 of the 378 stars within 10 parsecs
(75%) are red M dwarfs (RECONS April 2018; the 2026 Gaia-DR3
definitive census refines the neighborhood total to 424 stellar
and substellar objects — see L06 `stars.md` and `multiples.md`). The air between them is near-vacuum: the Local
Bubble cavity about 1,000 light-years across holding the Sun and
all nearby young stars — blown by about 15 supernovae starting
some 14 million years ago, entered by the Sun about 5 million
years back — and the wisp of the Local Interstellar
Cloud the Sun's heliosphere pushes into.

## What carries over into L7

Everything L6 counts at neighbor scale is still there, only far
away. The triple L6 frames as the anchor becomes the background
reference: Alpha Centauri A and B with Proxima beside them, and
Sirius with its ember companion, now context dots, not
destinations. Our own star becomes the center: the Sun, the G2V
dwarf that L6 counts as one neighbor among hundreds, is the one star of
L7. The L7 anchor is the Oort cloud outer edge, 100,000
astronomical units (1.50 x 10^16 m): the distant shell of icy
bodies around the Sun, its inner edge estimated at 2,000–5,000 AU
(NASA facts page). In log terms the Oort edge is about a third
of the way to Alpha Centauri — that compression is the science, not
a bug. The thin local medium L6 maps as Bubble and Fluff becomes
the outside: L7 sits inside the heliosphere's shelter, with the
Voyager crossings (termination shock December 2004 at ~94 AU and
August 2007 at ~84 AU; heliopause near 120 AU — August 2012 at
~122 AU and November 2018 at ~119 AU) marking the edge toward
interstellar space. Sources: ladder R5 anchors; NASA Oort cloud
facts page; NASA Voyager fast-facts page; NASA Alpha Centauri and Proxima b pages; ESA Sirius
pages; RECONS census summaries.

## What changes at this zoom

**From neighbors to one system.** At L6 the view counts suns
light-years apart. At L7 each L6 cell opens into usually one
system: a single star with its shells — giant planets, belts,
scattered bodies, the Oort sphere (see [giants.md](./giants.md),
[kuiper.md](./kuiper.md), [scattered.md](./scattered.md),
[oort.md](./oort.md)).

**From many portals to a sparse step.** The L6 cell opens into L7
through at most 6 markers per cell, usually one system portal, at
a true size ratio of 3.55e-1 (`../../../../docs/universes/ladder.md`, R6
table) — and the short L6–L7 span takes no zoom gap at all.
Populations (shown points) never open; only portals do. Inside L7
the next step is dense again: L7 to L8 takes two zoom gaps at ratio
1.20e-3 with 32 markers (one star plus shell populations).
Sources: ladder R6 amendment and gap note; ADR 0010 (portal vs
population).

**From counting hearths to mapping shells.** L6 shows finished
hearths — steady suns, companions circling, embers cooling, and
the thin medium they move through. L7 shows what one of those
hearths holds: the giant-planets zone, the Kuiper Belt doughnut,
the scattered disc and detached objects, the spherical Oort
reservoir, and the traffic through them — comets, Centaurs, and
interstellar visitors — with the heliosphere boundary and the
Voyager craft at its edge (see [comets.md](./comets.md)). The neighbor
stars are still there, light-years away — but here the story is
the shells of home, not the census.

## How the parts fit together

One system runs through the part docs. The giant-planets
zone ([giants.md](./giants.md)) sets the inner frame — Jupiter to Neptune with
rings and large moons as distant points; the Kuiper Belt
([kuiper.md](./kuiper.md)) holds the doughnut past Neptune with Pluto and
Arrokoth; the scattered disc and detached objects
([scattered.md](./scattered.md)) hold Eris, Sedna, and the Planet Nine search
area; the Oort cloud shells ([oort.md](./oort.md)) hold the spherical
long-period reservoir out to 100,000 AU; and the comets with
Centaurs and interstellar visitors ([comets.md](./comets.md)) are the traffic
plus the heliosphere boundary with the Voyager crossings and the
preview toward L8. The dated version of this story runs through
[lifetime.md](./lifetime.md), and the dive that brings you here is in
[entry.md](./entry.md).

## Fresh context (2024–2026)

The anchors above keep tightening: Gaia DR3 parallaxes now pin
the neighborhood distances behind every L6 number, and the 2026
definitive 10-parsec census (424 objects) is the current
cross-check (see L06 `stars.md`, `multiples.md`). The boundary
ahead is under new observation: NASA's IMAP probe, launched
September 2025 and stationed at Sun–Earth L1, maps the
heliosphere edge at about thirty times the IBEX resolution
(see L06 `ism.md`). Around the anchor triple itself, a
Saturn-mass planet candidate around Alpha Centauri A (imaged by
VLT in 2019, re-seen by JWST in 2024) still awaits confirmation
(see L06 `multiples.md`). None of this moves the L6-to-L7
mechanics above; it is why the bridge numbers can be trusted as
measured structure.

## Key numbers

- L6 span: 10^16–10^17 m; anchor Alpha Centauri distance 4.37 ly (4.13 x 10^16 m).
- L7 span: 10^15–10^16 m; anchor Oort cloud outer edge 100,000 AU (1.50 x 10^16 m).
- Alpha Centauri: triple at ~4.37 ly; A is Sun-like, B orange, Proxima a red dwarf with planet b (~1.06 Earth masses, 11.2 days at 0.0485 AU, found 2016).
- Sirius: 8.6 ly away; A is the brightest star in our sky; B is an Earth-sized white dwarf at 98% of the Sun's mass, 10,000x fainter than A, 50-year orbit.
- Neighborhood census: 284 of 378 stars within 10 parsecs (75%) are red M dwarfs (RECONS 2018.3; 2026 Gaia-DR3 census: 424 objects).
- Sun: G2V yellow dwarf at the center of L7.
- Local Bubble: a low-density cavity about 1,000 light-years across holding the Sun and all nearby young stars, blown by ~15 supernovae from ~14 Myr ago (Sun inside ~5 Myr).
- L6 to L7 ratio: 3.55e-1, at most 6 markers per L6 cell (usually one system portal), no zoom gap on this short span; L7 to L8 ratio 1.20e-3, 32 markers, two zoom gaps.
