# Lifetime — dated stages of the outer solar system

A short dated story of L7, from dust to Voyagers to the far future. Each stage gives one time, what changed, and where to read the full part doc. Times are in Ga (billion years ago), Myr (million years), kyr (thousand years), AU (Earth–Sun distances), and pc (parsecs; 1 pc is about 206,265 AU). Uncertainties are stated where the literature disagrees, especially for migration timing and the bombardment record.

This document is the dated spine for L7. Canonical detail lives in the part docs: `giants.md` (planets zone), `kuiper.md` (belt), `scattered.md` (scattered and detached), `oort.md` (far shell), `comets.md` (traffic and heliosphere edge). Related views: `entry.md` (arrival) and `previous-l6.md` (bridge).

## 4.6 Ga — solar nebula collapses, giants start to form

About 4.6 billion years ago a nearby cloud collapse forms the Sun and a disc of gas and dust. Radiometric dating of calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions puts the first solids at about 4.567–4.568 Ga; the Sun ignites shortly after while the protoplanetary disc still surrounds it. Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune grow from that disc over the next few million years: rock and ice cores first (about 10 Earth masses to start runaway gas capture), then gas capture for Jupiter and Saturn beyond the frost line, where water ice is abundant. Jupiter forms first, in under about 3 Myr, and takes most of the leftover mass; Saturn follows with less gas available; Uranus and Neptune accrete mostly ices (water, methane, ammonia) with little hydrogen and helium, which is why they are ice giants rather than gas giants. The gas disc disperses by about 10 Myr, ending rapid giant growth (see `giants.md` for the zone layout; `kuiper.md` for the leftover belt material).

What this sets up for L7: a crowded primordial Kuiper disc (originally some 7–10 Earth masses), a population to be scattered outward into the scattered disc, and a cloud of icy leftovers that galactic tides will round into the Oort cloud (see `oort.md`). The birth environment matters too: Kuiper-belt structure and anomalous materials suggest the Sun formed in a cluster of roughly 1,000–10,000 stars, whose early stellar passages helped shape detached orbits (see `scattered.md`).

Sources: NASA solar-system formation overviews; NASA Our Solar System formation pages; Planetary Society solar-system history (Jupiter under ~3 Myr, ice-giant sequence).

## 4.5–4.4 Ga — giants migrate, belt sculpted, Oort emplaced

The giant planets do not stay where they formed. Jupiter moves slightly inward while Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune move outward, scattering icy bodies everywhere. The canonical framework is the Nice model (named for Nice, France, 2005): the giants start compact (roughly 5.5–14 AU, Jupiter and Saturn near mutual resonance) with a massive planetesimal disc beyond (inner edge near 16 AU, outer edge near 30 AU, some 35–40 Earth masses). Planetesimal encounters migrate Jupiter inward and the others outward; when Jupiter and Saturn cross their mutual 1:2 (later work: escape from a 3:2 resonant chain) mean-motion resonance, eccentricities spike and the system goes briefly unstable. Uranus and Neptune plough outward through the disc, and Jupiter ejects most of the disc mass out of the system or into the far shell.

Three things happen at once: the Kuiper Belt is sculpted into resonant, classical, and scattered populations (see `kuiper.md`) — Neptune's outward drift traps Pluto and other plutinos in 3:2 resonance while the cold classicals stay nearly unmoved; millions of icy bodies are thrown outward and the galaxy's pull rounds them into the spherical Oort cloud (see `oort.md`) — Jupiter does most of the throwing, the galactic tide does most of the rounding, with the inner Hills cloud (about 2,000–20,000 AU) staying flatter and the outer sphere (about 20,000–100,000 AU) becoming isotropic; Neptune migrates outward through the disc, trapping Pluto and others in resonances.

Timing is debated, which is why this section spans 4.5–4.4 Ga rather than naming a single year. The original Nice model placed the instability late (about 700 Myr after formation, near 3.9 Ga, to explain a bombardment spike); the Nice-2 revision starts the giants in a multi-resonant chain from gas-driven migration and triggers the instability when the disc edge is far enough out. Meteorite-plus-simulation work (notably Mojzsis and collaborators, 2019) argues for a very early migration near 4.48 Ga, consistent with no meteorite impact-reset ages younger than about 4.5 Ga except reset rims; slow dust-driven escape variants allow instability within the first ~10 Myr after gas dispersal. Variants include the jumping-Jupiter scenario (an ice giant encounters Saturn then Jupiter, stepping their orbits apart quickly to protect terrestrial eccentricities and the asteroid belt) and a possible fifth ejected ice giant. For L7 the consequence is the same across variants: migration within the first ~100 Myr sculpts the belt and emplaces the cloud.

Sources: Nice-model literature via NASA and ESA formation explainers; NASA Kuiper Belt and Oort Cloud pages; Mojzsis et al. migration-timing work (4.48 Ga early-instability case).

## 4.1–3.8 Ga — bombardment and comet delivery (debated spike)

Around 4 billion years ago the inner planets record heavy cratering — but whether that record is a sharp spike, a sawtooth uptick, or a smooth tail is still debated, and this document does not claim more than the part docs. The classic Late Heavy Bombardment (LHB) picture reads Apollo impact-melt ages clustering near 3.9 Ga as a 20–200 Myr cataclysm when migrating giants threw asteroids and comets inward. Three live alternatives now share the literature: a terminal cataclysm (sharp ~3.9 Ga spike, tied to a late Nice instability); a sawtooth (slow decline from leftover planetesimals plus a modest ~4.1 Ga uptick when migration starts, 5–10 times the background); and a tail-end decline (monotonic decay since accretion, with the 3.9 Ga clustering produced by Imbrium-ejecta sampling bias plus argon-age resetting). Numerical melt-mixing models compared against Apollo and lunar-meteorite ages currently favour the tail-end shape, with the sawtooth not ruled out and the sharp cataclysm least favoured; Apollo-melt modelling (Blevins and colleagues, 2025) instead supports a rapid post-Imbrium decline consistent with the Robbins chronology for the late Imbrian. NASA's 2025 explainer states the position plainly: the bombardment is not a done deal, and samples from the South Pole–Aitken basin could decide it.

For L7 this is the Oort cloud and Kuiper Belt paying their debt inward: long-period comets and impactors released by the same migration that built them. Details in `comets.md`. The outer-system contribution is real under every hypothesis — migration must deliver some inward flux — but its size and date move with the hypothesis: a late instability gives a late comet-heavy pulse; an early instability (near 4.48 Ga) folds delivery into the first 100 Myr and leaves the ~3.9 Ga record mostly asteroidal or illusory. Spherule beds on Earth extend a long-lived tail of large impacts down to about 2.0–2.5 Ga in hybrid models. Chang'e-5 and Chang'e-6 crater densities (nearly identical 200 m–1 km densities despite a ~0.8 Gyr age gap) further suggest non-steady flux with a 2.0–2.8 Ga lull and a 2.8–3.4 Ga enhancement from asteroid-family breakups — a reminder that impact flux varies episodically long after migration ends.

Sources: NASA lunar-cratering and LHB explainers (~3.8–4.1 Ga debate); NASA What-is-the-LHB page (Apr 2025); Morbidelli et al. sawtooth model; Blevins et al. 2025 (rapid Imbrian decline); Chang'e-5/6 lull-and-spike result (2026).

## 3.8 Ga–present — slow grinding, leakage, and steady traffic

After the bombardment window the outer system settles into the long quiescence we still see. The Kuiper Belt grinds itself down by collisions: big bodies chip into fragments, fragments become dust that the solar wind blows away, and a trickle of escapees drifts sunward. Neptune's nudges feed the scattered disc; the scattered disc feeds the Centaurs crossing between the giants (lifetimes only tens of Myr before ejection, Sun or planet impact, or handoff to Jupiter); the Centaurs feed the Jupiter-family comets looping in 20 years or less (see `kuiper.md`, `scattered.md`, `comets.md`). Cold classicals barely move over billions of years — Arrokoth's soft-merged lobes are a preserved ~4.5 Ga planetesimal — while Pluto's nitrogen atmosphere breathes in and out with its 248-year orbit (see `kuiper.md`).

The Oort cloud loses a few comets per year to the galactic tide, passing stars, and giant molecular clouds, and gains little back; ejections and star passages thin it over the age of the system (see `oort.md`). Halley-type comets (20–200 years, steep and often retrograde) and long-period comets (up to ~30 Myr per orbit from ~100,000 AU) keep arriving on their own slow schedules, brightening near the Sun and fading outward, each perihelion costing metres of nucleus (see `comets.md`). Nothing in this stage needs a cataclysm: it is the background rate against which showers and visits stand out below.

Sources: NASA Kuiper Belt and Oort Cloud facts (populations, leakage, few-per-year long-period flux); NASA comet facts (period classes, activity).

## 80 kyr ago to 1.3 Myr future — showers, passages, slow erosion

The Oort cloud keeps losing and gaining comets. About 70,000–80,000 years ago Scholz's star — a dim M9.5 plus T5 brown-dwarf binary of only ~0.15 solar masses — passed ~0.25 pc (~52,000 AU, ~0.8 light-years) from the Sun, through the outer Oort cloud (Mamajek et al. 2015: 70 +15/−10 kyr, 98% probability of outer-cloud passage, ~10^-4 for the inner Hills cloud under 20,000 AU). Low mass plus high speed (~83 km/s at passage) made the encounter dynamically weak: negligible effect on the long-period flux, and far too faint (about magnitude 10 even at closest) for the eye except possibly during rare flares. Refined astrometry (Dupuy et al. 2019: 68.7 ± 2.0 kAU at 80.5 ± 0.7 kyr) keeps the same picture. Encounters this close happen roughly once per ~9 Myr; passages through the Sun's tidal radius (~1.35 pc) happen a few times per Myr, so Scholz's star will likely lose its closest-known-passer title as Gaia finds more.

About 1.29 million years from now Gliese 710 (a ~0.6 solar-mass K7 dwarf, now ~19 pc away in Serpens Cauda) will pass ~0.052 pc (~10,600 AU) from the Sun and stir fresh comets loose — deep inside the outer Oort cloud and near the Hills-cloud outskirts. Gaia DR3 integrations give 1.29 ± 0.02 Myr and 0.052 ± 0.002 pc (90% within 0.048–0.056 pc); a 2026 re-analysis finds 0.0621 ± 0.0023 pc at 1.3446 ± 0.0022 Myr, same order. At closest it will shine near magnitude −2.7 with proper motion near an arcminute per year — the brightest, fastest visitor on the sky — and simulations predict an observable shower near ten comets per year lasting 3–4 Myr. Effects inside ~40 AU (planets, classical belt, Pluto–Charon) stay negligible because the planets' mutual coupling absorbs the perturbation. Day to day, the Sun steadily strips the outer Oort edge while interstellar comets pass through (see `oort.md` for the shell, `comets.md` for the visitors).

Sources: Mamajek et al. 2015 (Scholz's star 70 kyr, 52 kAU, dynamically weak); Dupuy et al. 2019 refinement; de la Fuente Marcos Gaia DR3 integrations (Gliese 710 1.29 Myr, 0.052 pc); ESA Gaia pages on Gliese 710; 2026 encounter-rate study (one GJ-710-class event per ~50 Myr).

## 1977–2018 — Voyager era: launches, shock, heliopause

Humanity touches L7 in place. 1977: Voyager 2 launches 20 August, Voyager 1 launches 5 September, on trajectories that use a rare outer-planet alignment for gravity assists. 1979–1989: grand tour — Jupiter (1979, both craft), Saturn (1980 Voyager 1, 1981 Voyager 2), Uranus (Voyager 2, January 1986 — still the only visit), Neptune (Voyager 2, August 1989 — still the only visit), imaging 48 moons and all four ring and field systems along the way. 1990 bookend: Voyager 1's Pale Blue Dot (14 February 1990, ~40 AU / 6 billion km out) looks back at Earth as a mote in a sunbeam (see image above). December 2004: Voyager 1 crosses the termination shock at 94 AU, where the solar wind slows suddenly. August 2007: Voyager 2 crosses the termination shock at 84 AU — the asymmetry (94 vs 84 AU) shows the bubble breathes with solar activity and is dented on Voyager 2's side. August 2012: Voyager 1 crosses the heliopause at 122 AU into interstellar space. November 2018: Voyager 2 crosses the heliopause at 119 AU with a working plasma instrument, confirming the edge from field, plasma, and cosmic-ray data together. Both craft keep escaping at over 3 AU per year in different directions; at that speed they will not enter the Oort cloud for ~300 years and will need ~30,000 years to cross it. Power-saving work in 2026 (an instrument shutoff on Voyager 1 in April, freed power on Voyager 2 in August) keeps the interstellar plasma, field, and cosmic-ray record going (see `comets.md` for the boundary layers, `giants.md` for the tour order).

Sources: NASA Voyager mission and Interstellar Mission pages; NASA Pale Blue Dot page; NASA IBEX boundary context; 2026 Voyager power-management blogs.

## 2015–2026 — surveyed in place: New Horizons, visitors, new moons

The last decade put instruments inside L7 rather than inferring it from afar. 14 July 2015: New Horizons flies past Pluto at ~12,500 km after a 2006 launch and 2007 Jupiter assist, imaging Sputnik Planitia's nitrogen-ice convection cells; 1 January 2019: it flies past Arrokoth at 3,538 km, the most distant flyby ever, confirming a soft-merged cold-classical contact binary. 1 October 2024: it passes 60 AU, twice Pluto's flyby distance, its Student Dust Counter recording higher-than-expected dust over 45–55 AU (February 2024 Astrophysical Journal Letters) alongside Subaru finds beyond the old ~50 AU edge — the belt may reach ~80 AU or a second belt may lie beyond. 5 August 2026 analysis of 2015 images reports the first evidence of recently flowing liquid on Pluto: dark wetted features between Sputnik Planitia cells best explained by liquid nitrogen welling up from basal melting, not rain (Planetary Science Journal, 31 July 2026). Power and propellant should last into the 2040s past 100 AU (see `kuiper.md`).

Interstellar traffic arrives in the same window. 19 October 2017: 1I/'Oumuamua found already leaving (~400 m, no coma, non-gravitational acceleration). 30 August 2019: 2I/Borisov found inbound (active coma, perihelion December 2019 at ~2 AU). 1 July 2025: 3I/ATLAS found inbound inside Jupiter's orbit; perihelion 29–30 October 2025 at ~1.4 AU; Earth approach 19 December 2025 at ~1.8 AU (no hazard); Jupiter passage March 2026, then out on a hyperbolic path. Post-perihelion Webb MIRI spectra (December 2025) give the first direct methane on an interstellar visitor with unusually abundant carbon dioxide; June 2026 analysis finds deuterium ~30 times enriched versus solar-system comets with only traces of carbon-13, pointing to formation 10–12 Gyr ago — likely the oldest comet material yet sampled. ESA's JUICE (Jupiter arrival July 2031) caught 3I/ATLAS days after perihelion: ~2,000 kg/s of water vapour on 2 November 2025, mostly sunward from icy grains — an interstellar comet behaving like a normal one (see `comets.md`, `giants.md`).

Surveys reset the small-body census. Saturn reaches 293 confirmed moons (August 2026); Uranus gains S/2025 U1 (Webb, August 2025) for 29; Neptune's S/2002 N5 and S/2021 N1 await names. Hubble tracking published September 2026 finds a giant decagonal (ten-sided) wave around Saturn's south pole to pair with the northern hexagon. The Rubin Observatory's 10-year LSST begins 30 June 2026 and adds 380+ trans-Neptunian objects in early operations — the dataset that will test the Planet Nine clustering claim against the OSSOS uniform-distribution result (see `scattered.md`).

Sources: NASA New Horizons mission and dusty-belt result (Feb 2024); NASA Pluto-liquid result (Aug 2026); NASA 3I/ATLAS overview and Webb methane/ancient-origin releases (Jun 2026); ESA JUICE 3I/ATLAS results (Apr 2026); NASA Saturn/Uranus/Neptune moon counts; Rubin LSST begins (Jun 2026).

## 5 Gyr future — red giant Sun, then white dwarf; cloud stripped

About 5 billion years from now the Sun exhausts core hydrogen and becomes a red giant. The envelope expands past Mercury and Venus and possibly Earth; luminosity rises hundreds of times while the surface cools. The outer system heats up: ices melt farther out, Kuiper Belt and Oort objects are stirred, and frozen worlds far from today's habitable zone briefly thaw. As the red giant sheds about half its mass in winds and episodic bursts, its gravitational hold weakens and the survivors' orbits expand outward — Neptune-class orbits roughly doubling. The red-giant phase lasts about a billion years. Afterward the exposed core becomes a white dwarf: Earth-sized, ferociously hot, cooling over billions of years toward a black dwarf, surrounded briefly by an expanding planetary nebula of the shed envelope.

For L7 the fate is stripping. The weakened gravity plus the galactic tide and the ~12,000 Oort-cloud stellar passages expected over the system's life eject much of the cloud into interstellar space; models with compact Oort configurations find cumulative encounters within 1 pc dominate over the tide in eroding the cloud. What remains is a thinned, expanded shell around a stellar cinder — the slow fate of the shells. The word fate belongs here: this is the far-future fate of the shells. The Voyagers, silent for billions of years by then, will still be coasting outward among them.

Sources: NASA Sun evolution and exoplanet life-and-death pages (~5 billion years to red giant, ~1 Gyr red-giant phase, white dwarf after); 2026 stellar-encounter-rate study (GJ-710-class rarity, cumulative erosion).

## Assumptions and open questions

- Migration timing follows the early-instability reading (within the first ~100 Myr, possibly near 4.48 Ga) without asserting a single year; a late ~3.9 Ga instability is retained as the minority case tied to the cataclysm hypothesis.
- Bombardment is presented as an open three-way debate (cataclysm, sawtooth, tail-end) per NASA's 2025 explainer; no L7 claim depends on the spike being real.
- Oort distances use NASA facts framing (inner edge 2,000–5,000 AU, outer edge 10,000–100,000 AU) with the Hills/outer split from `oort.md`; estimates vary by author and are stated as ranges.
- Gliese 710 numbers follow Gaia DR3 integrations (0.052 ± 0.002 pc at 1.29 ± 0.02 Myr) with the 2026 slightly wider result noted; shower rates (~10 comets/yr for 3–4 Myr) are model predictions, not observations.
- 2026 results (Pluto basal melting, Saturn decagon, 3I/ATLAS deuterium enrichment, Chang'e lull-and-spike) are reported as published findings from the cited releases; isotopic and chronology interpretations may revise with further samples.
