# Giants — the planets zone

## How it looks

From far away the four giants are just bright points in order out from the Sun: Jupiter at 5.2 AU, Saturn near 9.5 AU, Uranus near 19 AU, and Neptune near 30 AU. Up close they split into two pairs: the gas giants Jupiter and Saturn (mostly hydrogen and helium, banded clouds, big storms) and the ice giants Uranus and Neptune (smaller, blue-green-blue, mostly water, methane, and ammonia fluid over a small rocky core).

![Neptune full disk with dark storm and bright streaks, Voyager 2 1989 (screensize)](../images/neptune-full-disk-voyager.jpg)

What each one shows:

- Jupiter: tan, orange, brown, and white bands. Dark belts and light zones flow opposite ways, with jet streams and long-lived storms. The Great Red Spot is a storm that was twice Earth's width when Voyager flew by in 1979 and about 1.3 times Earth's width now, still lasting hundreds of years; three smaller ovals merged into the half-size Little Red Spot beside it. Jupiter holds 115 IAU-recognized moons plus thousands of smaller objects: the four Galileans dominate, with volcanic Io, ocean-world Europa, giant Ganymede (larger than Mercury), and cratered Callisto.
- Saturn: soft gold, yellow, brown, and gray with fainter stripes than Jupiter, plus a six-sided jet stream (hexagon) at the north pole — about 20,000 miles (30,000 km) across with ~200 mph winds and a central storm — and, since Hubble tracking published in September 2026, a giant ten-sided (decagonal) wave around the south pole, the first large regular polygon seen there. Saturn holds 293 confirmed moons as of August 2026, from planet-sized Titan down to arena-sized rocks; smoggy Titan and ocean-spraying Enceladus are the priority targets for future missions.
- Uranus: pale blue-green from methane gas, mostly smooth in Voyager 2 views but with changing bright clouds near equinox. It spins on its side (tilt 97.77 degrees, retrograde like Venus), so its faint rings look like an oval around the ball. Its 13 rings run Zeta, 6, 5, 4, Alpha, Beta, Eta, Gamma, Delta, Lambda, Epsilon, Nu, Mu outward — nine narrow dark-grey inner rings plus a reddish inner-outer and a blue outer ring. Its five big moons (Titania, Oberon, Umbriel, Ariel, Miranda, found 1787–1948) are half water ice and half rock; the rest are likely captured asteroids with literary names from Shakespeare and Pope. Uranus holds the coldest planetary-air record at 49 K (-224.2 C), and its winds reach 560 mph (900 km/h), retrograde at the equator and prograde near the poles.
- Neptune: deeper blue with bands, white streaks, and dark storms. Voyager 2 saw the Earth-sized Great Dark Spot in 1989; that storm later faded and new ones formed. Reprocessed Voyager images published in 2024 show the two ice giants look much more alike than the old enhanced views suggested. Neptune is the windiest world known: methane clouds race at over 1,200 mph (2,000 km/h), three times Jupiter's winds and nine times Earth's. Its five main rings run Galle, Leverrier, Lassell, Arago, Adams outward, with four dust arcs (Liberte, Egalite, Fraternite, Courage) clumped in the Adams ring and held in place by the shepherd moon Galatea; the whole system looks young and short-lived.

All four planets have rings, but only Saturn's are bright and wide. Jupiter's rings are small and dark (found by Voyager 1 in 1979), likely dust kicked off its small inner moons by meteoroids. Uranus has 13 faint narrow rings, and Neptune has five main rings plus clumped arcs in the outer Adams ring. Saturn's run D, C, B, Cassini Division, A, F, G, E outward — with the Cassini Division a 2,920-mile (4,700 km) gap between the A and B mains — plus the far faint Phoebe ring (found 2009 by Spitzer); each ring orbits at its own speed, and the main sheets are ice-and-rock chunks from dust grains to house- and mountain-sized blocks.

The large moons stay star-like points at L7 range: Jupiter's four Galilean moons (Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto), Saturn's Titan, and Neptune's Triton are the easy ones to name. The full counts run 115 at Jupiter (IAU-recognized), 293 at Saturn (August 2026), 29 at Uranus (with S/2025 U1), and 16 at Neptune with two more (S/2002 N5, S/2021 N1) announced but awaiting names; all but two of Neptune's 16 are named for lesser sea gods and nymphs.

Sources: NASA Science Jupiter facts (`https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-facts/`); Saturn facts (`https://science.nasa.gov/saturn/facts/`); Uranus facts (`https://science.nasa.gov/uranus/facts/`); Neptune facts (`https://science.nasa.gov/neptune/neptune-facts/`); Neptune photojournal full-disk view (`https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/neptune-full-disk-view`).

Winds at a glance (equatorial peaks unless noted):

| Planet | Top winds | Storms and jets |
|---|---|---|
| Jupiter | ~335 mph (539 km/h) | dozen-plus prevailing winds; Great Red Spot (~1.3 Earths) + Little Red Spot |
| Saturn | ~1,600 ft/s (500 m/s) | hexagon jet ~200 mph north; decagonal wave south (2026) |
| Uranus | ~560 mph (900 km/h) | retrograde equator, prograde poles; bright equinox clouds |
| Neptune | 1,200+ mph (2,000 km/h) | windiest world; Great Dark Spot gone, new storms tracked |

Jupiter's colours sit on three stacked cloud decks spanning ~44 miles (71 km): ammonia-ice cirrus on top, ammonium hydrosulfide crystals in the middle, water ice and vapour deepest; plumes of sulfur- and phosphorus-bearing gas from the warm interior paint the belts, and the 10-hour spin shears the decks into the opposing belt-zone jets.

## How it changes over time

The giants move slowly because they are far out. One year lasts about 12 Earth years on Jupiter, 29.4 on Saturn, 84 on Uranus, and 165 on Neptune; Neptune finished its first full orbit since discovery (1846) only in 2011. Their days are short: about 9.9 hours (Jupiter), 10.7 hours (Saturn), 17 hours (Uranus), and 16 hours (Neptune).

Seasons come from tilt, not distance. Jupiter barely tilts (3 degrees) so it has little seasonal change; Saturn tilts 26.73 degrees like Earth so it has real seasons; Uranus lies almost sideways (97.77 degrees) so each pole gets about 21 years of light then 21 years of dark; Neptune tilts 28 degrees so each of its four seasons lasts over 40 years.

NASA's Juno orbiter (arrived July 4, 2016; extended mission through September 2025) rewrote the Jupiter-system record from pole to pole: eight cyclones ring the north pole in an octagon and five-to-six the south pole in a pentagon, holding station for years; the belt-zone jet streams reach ~1,900 miles (3,000 km) deep with the belts flipping contrast 40 miles (65 km) down like oceans; the Great Red Spot's roots run 200 miles (320 km) deep with a gravity signature to ~300 miles (500 km); lightning falls as polar ammonia-water hail; and the magnetic field tops 30 times Earth's in places around a drifting equatorial Great Blue Spot. Close flybys weighed the moons too: Ganymede (June 7, 2021, within 645 miles/1,038 km), Europa (September 29, 2022, sizing its ice shell over a candidate ocean), and Io (December 2023 and February 2024, mapping fresh lava fields and taking its temperature). Europa Clipper (launched October 14, 2024) now flies to follow up on Europa's habitability.

Storms and clouds change fastest. Jupiter's Great Red Spot has lasted 300+ years but shrinks and grows (recent Hubble tracking shows continued narrowing with shape cycling); Neptune's Great Dark Spot seen by Voyager 2 in 1989 is gone and replaced by newer storms tracked from the ground and Hubble; Uranus grows more active with bright clouds as it nears equinox; Saturn's poles show evolving polygon waves. ESA's JUICE craft (launched April 14, 2023 on Ariane 5; Lunar-Earth flyby August 2024; Venus flyby August 31, 2025 at 5,088 km; Earth flyby September 28, 2026 within 8,640 km; final Earth flyby January 2029; Jupiter arrival July 2031; Ganymede orbit December 2034) will extend the Jupiter-system record with 35 moon flybys and the first orbit of an ocean world. En route it caught interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS days after perihelion: MAJIS measured 2,000 kg of water vapour per second on November 2, 2025 (high but not exceptional — 67P gave ~300 kg/s, Halley ~20,000 kg/s), SWI saw most of it escaping sunward from icy grains rather than the nucleus directly, UVS traced gas and dust past 5 million km, JANUS resolved the coma and two tails from ~60 million km, and NavCam tracking from off-Earth angles sharpened the trajectory for planetary-defence methods — an interstellar visitor behaving like a normal solar-system comet (see `comets.md`).

The dated story, from nebula collapse to the far-future Sun, lives in `lifetime.md`; the dive that arrives here is described in `entry.md`.

Who has visited, in order:

- Pioneer 10 (1973) and 11 (1974–1979 flybys) — first Jupiter and Saturn crossings; still escaping.
- Voyager 1 and 2 (1979–1989) — the grand tour of all four giants plus 48 moons; the only visits Uranus and Neptune ever got.
- Galileo (1995–2003) — Jupiter orbiter; dropped an entry probe in 1995 that found a strangely dry hotspot.
- Cassini–Huygens (2004–2017) — Saturn orbiter from July 1, 2004; Huygens landed on Titan on January 14, 2005; ended with a planned plunge into Saturn on September 15, 2017, sampling the atmosphere to the last second.
- New Horizons (2007 Jupiter gravity assist) — en route to Pluto and Arrokoth.
- Juno (2016–2025) — polar Jupiter orbiter; fuzzy core, deep jets, polar cyclones, Galilean flybys.
- JUICE (2031–2035) — ESA Jupiter orbiter, then the first orbiter of an ocean world (Ganymede).
- Europa Clipper (launched 2024, arrival 2030) — repeated Europa flybys to test its ocean's habitability.

No mission has yet orbited Uranus or Neptune: both ice giants remain Voyager-only worlds.

## How it is created

All four giants formed with the rest of the solar system about 4.5–4.6 billion years ago, when gravity pulled swirling gas and dust together. Jupiter took most of the leftover mass after the Sun formed — more than twice the combined material of everything else — but never grew hot enough to ignite like a star.

Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants: mostly hydrogen and helium, with deep liquid-hydrogen layers and, on Jupiter, a large dilute ("fuzzy") core found by NASA's Juno gravity data rather than a small hard ball. Uranus and Neptune are ice giants: 80% or more of their mass is a hot dense fluid of water, methane, and ammonia above a small rocky core.

About 4 billion years ago the giants settled into their current order — Jupiter fifth, Saturn sixth, Uranus seventh, Neptune eighth — with Uranus and Neptune likely forming closer to the Sun and moving outward. That outward push scattered the leftover icy bodies into the belt, the scattered disk, and the Oort Cloud.

Gravity science now reads the interiors the formation left behind: Jupiter's dilute core has no sharp boundary with the metallic-hydrogen envelope (a giant-impact or early-mixing relic still debated), while Saturn keeps a compact dense core of iron, nickel, rock, and ices wrapped in a smaller metallic-hydrogen layer — consistent with the solar system's lowest density, below water.

Sources: NASA Science Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune facts pages; Kuiper Belt facts formation section (`https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/kuiper-belt/facts/`).

## How it interacts with the others

Neptune is the gatekeeper of the outer system: its gravity sets the inner edge of the Kuiper Belt at 30 AU, holds resonant objects like Pluto in a 3:2 rhythm (Pluto orbits twice per three Neptune orbits), and scatters disk objects outward, while detached bodies like Sedna never come near it. The belt's slow erosion and Neptune's nudges feed the Centaurs that cross between the giants and become short-period Jupiter-family comets looping in 20 years or less.

Jupiter finishes the job: its gravity corrals those comets into short loops or ejects them, and together with Saturn it long ago slingshotted most of the original 7–10 Earth masses of belt material out to the Oort Cloud or out of the system entirely. The comet traffic itself lives in `comets.md`; the belt it comes from in `kuiper.md`; the scattered and detached strays in `scattered.md`; the far shell in `oort.md`; the timeline in `lifetime.md`; the arrival view in `entry.md`.

The moons feed back too. Five giant-system moons hide likely global oceans under ice, which is why JUICE and Europa Clipper fly there:

- Europa — salty ocean under a thin shifting shell; Juno microwave data now bounds the shell thickness; plumes and chaos terrain hint at surface–ocean exchange.
- Ganymede — the largest moon, with its own magnetic field and stacked ocean layers; JUICE will orbit it from December 2034.
- Callisto — ancient cratered crust over a deep ocean; JUICE's inclined-phase target.
- Titan — methane lakes and rain under orange smog, plus a buried water ocean; Huygens landed there in 2005.
- Enceladus — south-polar jets spray its ocean into Saturn's E ring; a top life-search target.

Inside each system the planets rule their moons and rings: Jupiter's magnetosphere — 16 to 54 times Earth's strength, ballooning 1–3 million km sunward with a tail past Saturn's orbit — bathes the Galileans in radiation and drives the strongest auroras in the system (up to 400,000 electron volts per particle, versus a few thousand in Earth's best); Saturn's field (578 times Earth's) holds its rings and moons including Titan, with auroras fed by moon-ejected particles as well as the solar wind; Uranus drags a corkscrew magnetic tail from its sideways spin, its magnetic axis tipped 60 degrees off the spin axis and offset a third of the planet's radius, so its auroras sit far from the poles; Neptune's field (27 times Earth's, tipped 47 degrees) wobbles wildly each 16-hour rotation. Neptune's moon Triton — likely a captured Kuiper object on a backward orbit, found October 10, 1846, just 17 days after Neptune itself — still erupts nitrogen geysers 5 miles (8 km) high through a -391 F (-235 C) crust under a thin warming atmosphere. Voyager 1 (launched September 5, 1977) and Voyager 2 (launched August 20, 1977) together explored all four giants, 48 of their moons, and their ring and field systems: Jupiter and Saturn flybys 1979–1981, Uranus 1986, Neptune 1989 — the only visits Uranus and Neptune have ever had — before crossing the termination shock (94 AU in 2004, 84 AU in 2007) and the heliopause (122 AU in 2012, 119 AU in 2018) at over 3 AU per year.

Sources: NASA Kuiper Belt facts (`https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/kuiper-belt/facts/`); NASA Voyager mission overview (`https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/mission-overview/`); NASA Voyager fast facts (`https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/fast-facts`).

## Size and mass

Order holds size and distance together: Jupiter (5.2 AU), Saturn (9.5 AU), Uranus (~19 AU), Neptune (30 AU). Typical numbers:

- Jupiter: ~88,846 miles (142,984 km) wide, ~11 times Earth; mass ~318 Earths; 1,000 Earths could fit inside.
- Saturn: ~74,898 miles (120,536 km) wide, ~9 times Earth; mass ~95 Earths; the only planet less dense than water.
- Uranus: ~31,763 miles (51,118 km) wide, ~4 times Earth; mass ~14.5 Earths; slightly wider than Neptune but lighter.
- Neptune: ~30,775 miles (49,528 km) wide, ~4 times Earth; mass ~17 Earths; densest of the giants.

The whole zone (5–30 AU) is tiny inside L7: the L7 anchor is the Oort edge at 100,000 AU (1.50 x 10^16 m), so the giants span only the inner three ten-thousandths of this level's radius.

## Example objects

- Jupiter — the king at 5.2 AU. Diameter ~142,984 km (radius 43,440.7 miles / 69,911 km, 11 Earths wide), mass ~318 Earths, day 9.9 hours, year ~12 Earth years (4,333 days), tilt 3 degrees. 115 recognized moons: the Galileans (Io the volcanic world, Europa the ocean world, Ganymede the largest moon in the system, Callisto the ancient surface) found by Galileo in 1610; faint dark rings; Great Red Spot shrinking from twice to ~1.3 Earth widths. Studied by Juno (2016–2025) and next by JUICE (2031) and Europa Clipper.
- Saturn — the ringed giant at 9.5 AU. Diameter ~120,536 km, mass ~95 Earths, day 10.7 hours, year ~29.4 Earth years. Rings spanning up to 175,000 miles (282,000 km) yet only ~30 feet (10 m) thick, ordered D-C-B-Cassini-A-F-G-E plus Phoebe; 293 confirmed moons including Titan (larger than Mercury, methane lakes) and Enceladus (spraying ocean jets). Visited by Cassini (2004–2017); hexagon north, decagon south.
- Uranus — the sideways ice giant at ~19 AU. Diameter ~51,118 km, mass ~14.5 Earths, day ~17 hours, year ~84 Earth years. Tilt 97.77 degrees, 13 faint named rings, 29 known moons with S/2025 U1 still provisional; five big icy-rock moons (Titania, Oberon, Umbriel, Ariel, Miranda); coldest air 49 K; corkscrew magnetotail. Visited once, by Voyager 2 in 1986.
- Neptune — the windy edge at 30 AU. Diameter ~49,528 km, mass ~17 Earths, day ~16 hours, year ~165 Earth years. Five named rings (Galle to Adams) plus four Galatea-shepherded arcs; 16 recognized moons plus two awaiting names; Triton, the large backward-orbiting captured moon with 8-km nitrogen geysers. Visited once, by Voyager 2 in 1989; first full orbit since discovery closed in 2011.
