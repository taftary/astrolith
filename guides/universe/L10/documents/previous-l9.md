# L9 behind us — Star close-up as previous level

This page is the bridge from L9 (Stars) into L10 (Planets and
moons): what the previous level looks like, what carries over when
you zoom in, and what changes at L10 scale. The part docs from the
loop's first pass now exist — follow the cross-links below.
Entry itself — the visual transition and its effects — lives in
[entry.md](./entry.md). The dated timeline runs through
[lifetime.md](./lifetime.md).

## What L9 is and how it looks

L9 is the star close-up scale at about 10^9 m: one star filling
the whole view. Its anchor is the Sun's width, 1.39 x 10^9 m
(about 1.4 million km, or about 865,000 miles across), the
IAU 2015 nominal solar radius of 695,700 km, doubled to a
diameter. Our Sun is the example star: a 4.6-billion-year-old
yellow dwarf (type G2V, now about halfway through its
ten-billion-year main-sequence life) holding 99.8 percent
of the solar system's mass, sitting about 93 million miles
(150 million km) from Earth — about 8.35 light-minutes away.

Inside the view are the layers. Deep down is the interior: the
core where fusion makes the heat and light (about 15 million
degrees C, 27 million degrees F), wrapped in the radiative and
convection zones that carry that energy outward. Then comes the
face we actually see, the photosphere — the bright "surface"
layer, only about 250 miles (400 km) thick, glowing at about
5,500 degrees C (10,000 degrees F), marked by dark sunspots
(1,600–160,900 km across, cooler than their surroundings) and
bright patches. Above that stretches the atmosphere: the thin
pinkish chromosphere, the narrow transition region, and the huge
hot crown, the corona, reaching up to about 2 million degrees C
(3.5 million degrees F) — hotter with height, one of the Sun's
biggest puzzles — with arches, streamers, dark coronal holes,
and red tongues of gas (prominences) lifting off the edge.

Around all of that is the action: flares — sudden bright flashes
as powerful as billions of hydrogen bombs — great clouds of
charged gas thrown outward (coronal mass ejections) at over a
million miles per hour, and the steady solar wind streaming past
the planets at hundreds of km/s. Far off, tiny and faint, sit the
companions: the eight planets as nearby points going around the
Sun (Mercury at 0.39 AU out to Neptune at 30.1 AU), plus dust
rings tracing planetary orbits, sungrazing comets diving through
the glare, and — around more than half of all stars, though not
our Sun — a rare second star nearby.

## What carries over into L10

Everything L9 shows at star scale is still there, only closer.
The blinding disk L9 frames as the whole becomes the surroundings:
the Sun, the G2V dwarf about 100 times wider than Earth and about
10 times wider than Jupiter, still shining from about 93 million
miles away — its light still taking about 8.35 minutes to reach
us. The wind L9 watches leaving the crown becomes the weather up
close: the same stream of charged gas that brushes past Earth,
stretches Jupiter's magnetosphere into a windsock tail, and paints
the northern and southern lights. The warmth L9 counts as
the habitable zone's gift becomes the ground under our feet —
liquid water covering about 71 percent of Earth's face, air to
breathe (78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen), and seasons from
Earth's 23.4-degree tilt. The companion points L9 keeps at the
edge become the subject: one of those dots — Earth, the third
planet from the Sun, the biggest rocky world and fifth largest
planet overall — now fills the view, with its own Moon going
around it at about 239,000 miles (384,400 km) away, some 30
Earths in a row. Sources: ladder R5 anchors;
NASA Sun facts page; NASA Earth facts page; NASA Moon facts page.

## What changes at this zoom

**From one star to one planet with moons.** At L9 the view holds
one star close-up at about 10^9 m. At L10 each L9 cell opens
into one planet with its moons at 10^6–10^8 m: a single round
world with its lands, waters, air, and night lights, plus the
moons going around it — the inside, the face, the air, the moons,
and the magnetic shield with its space weather (see
[interior.md](./interior.md), [surface.md](./surface.md),
[atmosphere.md](./atmosphere.md), [moons.md](./moons.md),
[magnetosphere.md](./magnetosphere.md)). Recent context: Solar
Cycle 25 (begun December 2019) reached its maximum phase around
2024–2025 and stays active into 2026, so the L9 activity markers —
spots, flares, coronal mass ejections, wind streams — are near
their most dramatic just as this bridge looks back at them.

**From a dense step to a sparse one.** The L9 cell opens into L10
at a true size ratio of 9.33e-3 (`../../../../docs/universes/ladder.md`, R6 table) —
the Earth (1.28 x 10^7 m across) against the Sun (1.39 x 10^9 m) —
with about 12 markers per cell (the planets plus rare companions).
The L9–L10 span takes two invisible magnification milestones, the
same quiet stepping the ladder's gap note describes for most long
spans. Populations (shown points) never open; only portals do.
Sources: ladder R6 amendment and gap note; ADR 0010 (portal vs
population).

**From reading a star to standing on a world.** L9 shows a burning
ball — layers, face, crown, outbursts, and distant companion
points. L10 shows what one of those companion points holds up
close: the rocky inside with inner core, outer core, mantle, and
crust; the surface of oceans (averaging about 3.6 km deep),
land, ice, and life; the air of nitrogen and oxygen
that shields and warms; the Moon — about 3,474 km across
(radius about 1,740 km), some 30 Earths away in a line,
tidally locked so the same face always looks home — going around
every 27.3 days (29.5 days phase to phase) and pulling
the tides while steadying Earth's wobble; and the magnetic shield
that bends the solar wind into the auroras. The star is still
there, 93 million miles off — but here the story is the planet,
not the star. Sources: NASA Sun facts page; NASA Earth facts
page; NASA Moon facts page.

## How the parts fit together

One planet runs through the part docs. The interior
([interior.md](./interior.md)) sets the engine — inner and outer
core, mantle, crust, differentiation and the dynamo seat; the
surface ([surface.md](./surface.md)) sets the face — oceans, land,
ice, tectonics, life; the atmosphere ([atmosphere.md](./atmosphere.md))
sets the blanket — air, clouds, weather, climate, ozone; the moons
([moons.md](./moons.md)) set the company — our Moon's shape,
phases, tides, and water-ice story; and the shield
([magnetosphere.md](./magnetosphere.md) with the activity) sets the
voice — the magnetic field, the solar wind bending around it,
auroras, and space weather. The dated version of this story runs
through [lifetime.md](./lifetime.md), and the dive that brings you
here is in [entry.md](./entry.md). Sources: L09 part docs
as the pattern; NASA Earth, Moon, and Sun facts pages.

## Key numbers

- L9 span: about 10^9 m; anchor Sun diameter 1.39 x 10^9 m (IAU 2015 nominal radius 695,700 km).
- L10 span: 10^6–10^8 m; anchor Earth equatorial diameter 1.28 x 10^7 m (7,926 miles / 12,756 km).
- Sun: G2V yellow dwarf, about 4.6 billion years old (about halfway through a ten-billion-year main sequence), 99.8 percent of system mass, diameter about 1.4 million km (865,000 miles); face (photosphere) about 5,500 degrees C (10,000 degrees F), about 250 miles (400 km) thick; core about 15 million degrees C (27 million degrees F); crown (corona) up to about 2 million degrees C (3.5 million degrees F); 93 million miles (150 million km) from Earth, about 8.35 light-minutes.
- Earth: third planet from the Sun, biggest rocky world and fifth largest planet; day 23.9 hours, year 365.25 days, tilt 23.4 degrees; oceans cover about 71 percent, averaging about 3.6 km deep; air 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen.
- Earth–Moon: Moon diameter about 3,474 km (radius about 1,740 km); average distance 384,400 km (238,855 miles, about 30 Earths in a row); Moon circles Earth every 27.3 days (29.5 days phase to phase), tidally locked; Moon's pull steadies Earth's wobble and raises the tides; Moon drifts about an inch farther away each year.
- L9 to L10 ratio: 9.33e-3, about 12 markers per L9 cell; L9–L10 takes two zoom gaps.
