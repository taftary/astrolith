# Hydrosphere — the water around the world

This page is the L10 part doc for water: the global ocean,
the ice, the groundwater, and the cycle that moves between
them at whole-planet scale. The face this water covers lives
in `surface.md`; the air it trades with lives in
`atmosphere.md` and `clouds-weather.md`; the fire that
breathed much of it out lives in `volcanism.md`; the
companion that pulls its tides lives in `moons.md`; the dated
story runs through `lifetime.md`. Rivers, lakes, and deltas
at 100–1,000 km will live in the planned L11 pages; the
close-up of coasts, glaciers, and groundwater belongs at L12.

Earth is a watery place wearing its water thin. The oceans
average only about 3.6 km deep on a planet 12,756 km across
— a film a thousand times shallower than the world is wide,
yet holding nearly all the water there is. Almost everything
wet you will ever see is that film; the fresh water of every
river, lake, and aquifer is a rounding error beside it, and
the ice that could drown the coasts sits locked at the poles
waiting for warmth.

## The inventory — where the water sits

Of about 332.5 million cubic miles of water on Earth, the
oceans and seas hold about 321 million — roughly 96.5
percent. Only 2.5 percent is fresh, and of that fresh
sliver, about 68.7 percent is locked in ice caps, glaciers,
and permanent snow, and about 30.1 percent hides underground
as groundwater. Everything else — lakes, rivers, wetlands,
soil moisture, the air itself, and all living things —
shares the remaining 1.2 percent of fresh water. Rivers, the
source of most water people use, hold about 509 cubic miles:
0.006 percent of fresh water, one ten-thousandth of one
percent of all water.

The air carries almost nothing at any moment — about 0.001
percent of all water — yet it moves the cycle, because each
molecule stays aloft only 9–10 days before falling again.
Groundwater rests about 300 years on average; ice waits over
a thousand. The ocean turns over in thousands of years,
breathing 114 cm of evaporation off its skin every year —
about 90 percent of all vapor the cycle ever lifts.

Sources: USGS How Much Water Is There; USGS Water Science
School; NOAA water-cycle pages.

The ocean is one world, not five. Basins have names, but the
water connects around every cape and under every ice edge,
one salty body averaging 3.6 km deep and plunging to about
11 km in the Mariana Trench (see `surface.md`). Salt is the
memory of everything washed off land: rivers carry dissolved
rock to the sea, evaporation leaves the salt behind, and the
concentration steadies where sea ice, rain, and currents
balance. The Gulf Stream alone moves 100 times the water of
all rivers on Earth combined, carrying tropical warmth to
the North Atlantic at 97 km per day — the currents are the
climate's second circulation after the winds (see
`clouds-weather.md`).

Sources: USGS Oceans and the Water Cycle; NOAA ocean facts
pages.

## The cycle — how water moves

The loop never closes because the Sun never stops paying for
it. Ocean water evaporates, most in winter hemispheres;
vapor rises, cools, and condenses into the cloud decks of
`clouds-weather.md`; clouds drop rain and snow; rivers gather
it back to the sea, or it sinks into soil and aquifers, or
it locks into ice for a thousand years. Plants return
groundwater to the sky directly — evapotranspiration — so
forests make their own rain downwind.

Over land the simple loop tangles. Some rain infiltrates to
springs and wells; some runs off in floods; frozen ground
holds melt until spring, then releases it all at once. The
highest evaporation from oceans falls in winter; the
heaviest rains fall where air is forced up — equator,
monsoon coasts, mountain walls — and the driest lands sit
where air sinks or the Moon-like cold freezes vapor out
(Atacama, McMurdo Dry Valleys; see `surface.md`).

Warming rewrites the schedule without changing the loop.
Hotter air holds more vapor, so wet places flood harder;
ice-stored water moves to the ocean and the seas rise;
snowmelt that villages timed for centuries arrives weeks
early. The cycle keeps its shape while its extremes grow
teeth — the same sentence written about the winds in
`clouds-weather.md`, because it is one machine.

Sources: USGS Water Cycle pages; NOAA JetStream water-cycle
pages.

## The ice — water in storage

Ice is the cycle paused. Antarctica holds about 26.5–30
million cubic km over 14 million square km — near 58 m of
sea level; Greenland holds about 2.9 million cubic km over
1.7 million square km — about 7.4 m; together with mountain
glaciers they store over 68 percent of all fresh water and
more than 99 percent of land ice (see `surface.md`). The
Arctic's floating sea ice, watched every year since 1978,
keeps setting young thin records — the 2026 minimum tied
tenth-lowest at 4.6 million square km.

Ice is also the record keeper. Each year's snow buries the
last with its trapped air, so ice cores read atmospheres
hundreds of thousands of years old — the receipts behind
every climate claim in `atmosphere.md` and `lifetime.md`.
And ice is the sculptor: sheets that spread in ice ages
ground valleys, pushed moraines, and drowned coasts when
they melted, the carving credited in `surface.md` and the
close-up reserved for the planned L11 ice and L12 glacier
pages.

Sources: NSIDC ice-sheet pages; NASA Earth Observatory ice
pages; NOAA sea-ice pages.

## Where the water came from

Earth was not born wet. Some water arrived locked inside the
rocky building blocks; more rode in on icy asteroids and
comets; and as the surface cooled, steam rained out of the
thick early air the volcanoes were breathing — the same
outgassing that built the air itself (see `volcanism.md`
and `atmosphere.md`). Oceans then soaked up carbon dioxide
and left nitrogen behind, which is why the air and the seas
are one story told twice.

The oldest continental hearts carry zircon time capsules
near 4.0 billion years old (see `surface.md`), and water
was already cycling by then — oceans may be nearly as old
as the crust. What followed is the long negotiation between
oceans, ice, air, and life: oxidation rusted the seas,
plants colonized land, ice ages locked and released the
water in pulses, and the timeline in `lifetime.md` carries
the dates.

Sources: NASA solar-system formation pages; USGS oldest-rock
pages.

## How it interacts with the others

The air and the sea are one trade (see `atmosphere.md` and
`clouds-weather.md`). Oceans evaporate the moisture every
cloud is made of and take rain back; ice reflects sunlight
and cools the air; plants breathe vapor upward; Saharan dust
fertilizes the Amazon across the Atlantic. The meeting point
is visible from orbit: cloud swirls over dark water.

The Moon pulls the water twice daily (see `moons.md`). Tides
rise in every ocean — and minutely in the solid Earth —
while the Moon drifts 4 cm farther each year and Earth's
spin slows. Shores, estuaries, and tidal flats march to
this rhythm; the planned L11 and L12 coast pages hold the
close-up.

The fire feeds and threatens the water (see `volcanism.md`
and `tectonics.md`). Ridges and volcanoes breathed the
oceans out and still top up gases; sinking plates drag water
back down into the mantle, closing the loop; eruptions can
cool the planet for years or, like Hunga Tonga, throw vapor
skyward instead. Tsunamis are the sea answering a slipped
fault (see `tectonics.md`).

The shield guards the whole reservoir (see
`magnetosphere.md`). Without the magnetic field bending the
solar wind around the planet, air and then oceans would leak
to space far faster — Mars is the demonstration (see
`atmospheres.md`).

## Other worlds' water

**Mars — the dried sea.** Ancient valleys, deltas, and lake
beds (Jezero's delta held a lake) record rivers under a
thicker blanket; today water hides as polar ice, ground ice,
and rare brines, with no oceans and no rain (see
`surface.md` and `atmospheres.md`).

**Europa — the hidden ocean.** Jupiter's ice moon wraps a
bright cracked shell over a salty ocean that may hold twice
Earth's water, warmed by the same tidal kneading that melts
Io — Hubble has caught plume candidates rising 100 km.
Europa Clipper is on its way to taste it.

**Enceladus — the sampled ocean.** Saturn's 500-km moon
vents its subsurface sea through south-pole tiger stripes at
400 m/s; Cassini flew through and found water with carbon
dioxide, methane, ammonia, hydrogen, silica grains from
above-90°C vents, and phosphorus — hydrothermal chemistry
like Earth's white smokers. Its spray feeds Saturn's E ring.

**Titan — the methane mirror.** Polar seas of liquid methane
and ethane where water would be, rained from orange smog
(see `atmospheres.md`) — the only standing liquid besides
Earth's, and not water at all.

**Ganymede, Callisto, Triton** likely hide oceans too; the
count of ocean worlds grows with every mission. Earth
remains the only one with its ocean on the outside.

Sources: NASA Mars facts; NASA Europa and ocean-worlds
pages; NASA Cassini Enceladus science; NASA Titan facts.

## Key numbers

- Total: ~332.5M cubic miles; oceans ~96.5%; fresh 2.5%.
- Fresh split: ice ~68.7%, groundwater ~30.1%, surface and
other ~1.2%; rivers 0.006% of fresh; air 0.001% of all.
- Stays: air 9–10 days; rivers 12–20 days; groundwater
~300 years; ice 1,000+ years; ocean thousands.
- Ocean: 71% of the face, avg 3.6 km, deepest ~11 km;
Gulf Stream 100x all rivers at 97 km/day.
- Ice sheets: Antarctica 26.5–30M cubic km (~58 m seas);
Greenland 2.9M cubic km (~7.4 m).
- Other oceans: Europa maybe 2x Earth's water; Enceladus
plumes 400 m/s, vents above 90°C.
- Anchor world: Earth 12,756 km across; the whole
hydrosphere a film a thousand times thinner than the world
is wide.

Sources: USGS and NOAA pages named above; NASA ocean-worlds
pages.

## Example objects

Earth is the anchor: the only world with its ocean outside
— blue film, white caps, cloud swirls, twice-daily tides.

Mars is the memory: dry valleys and deltas with no sea,
ice at the poles and under the dust, waiting for a blanket
that left.

Europa is the vault: twice our water under bright ice,
cracked and lined, possibly venting — the best place to
look for life beyond Earth.

Enceladus is the sample: an ocean we have already flown
through, tasted, and found fed by hot vents.

Titan is the mirror: seas and rain with no water in them,
methane playing every role water plays here.

Sources: NASA Mars, Europa, Enceladus, Titan, and Earth
facts pages.
