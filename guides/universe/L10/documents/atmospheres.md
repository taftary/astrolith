# Atmospheres — the air on every world

This page is the L10 comparison doc for air: what each
world's atmosphere is made of, how heavy it presses, what its
clouds are made of, and where its edge glows. Earth's own
layers live in `atmosphere.md` — this page measures everyone
else against that standard. The winds that stir these airs
live in `clouds-weather.md`; the fire that breathed them out
lives in `volcanism.md`; the shield that guards them lives
in `magnetosphere.md`; the dated story runs through
`lifetime.md`.

The recipe varies more than the planets do. One world wears
nitrogen and oxygen at a comfortable pressure; its twin next
door wears crushing carbon dioxide under acid cloud; a moon
farther out wears thicker nitrogen air than Earth with methane
for weather; the giants wear no solid ground at all, only
hydrogen deepening forever into liquid. Same physics —
gravity holding gas, sunlight warming it, wind stripping it —
wildly different airs.

## The standard: Earth

Earth's air near the ground is about 78 percent nitrogen, 21
percent oxygen, 1 percent everything else — mostly argon plus
carbon dioxide, neon, and more — pressing 1013 hPa at sea
level, about 10 tonnes on every square metre. Its clouds are
water in three decks plus storm towers (see
`clouds-weather.md`); its layers run troposphere to exosphere
(see `atmosphere.md`). Everything below is measured against
this: the only nitrogen–oxygen air, the only open oceans, the
only ozone shield — the living standard.

Sources: NASA Earth facts page; NOAA JetStream pages.

## Venus — the warning

Slightly smaller than Earth, Venus kept its carbon dioxide
instead of burying it in oceans and rocks, and the result is
a runaway blanket. The air is more than 96 percent carbon
dioxide with about 3.5 percent nitrogen and traces of sulfur
dioxide, carbon monoxide, and water vapor. At the surface it
presses about 93 times Earth's sea-level pressure — the same
as a kilometer deep in our oceans — under 467°C heat that
melts lead. The few landers sent there died within two
hours.

Above the furnace sits the veil: thick clouds at 30–60 km
made of sulfuric-acid droplets condensing on polysulfur
haze, with sulfur dioxide feeding them from below — a sign
of volcanic activity now or recently, since that gas is
quickly eaten by the surface. Acid rains down but evaporates
before reaching the ground; lightning fires inside the acid
clouds instead of water ones. The cloud tops race around the
planet in about four Earth days at up to 360 km/h while the
ground below creeps through a 243-day backward rotation —
super-rotation whose engine is still debated. Small meteors
never reach the surface: craters under ~1.5–2 km do not
exist, burned up in the dense air.

Sources: NASA Venus facts pages; NASA Venus lithograph;
JGR Venus climate-evolution review.

## Mars — the thin leftover

Mars wears mostly carbon dioxide too, but almost none of it:
under 1 percent of Earth's surface pressure, so heat escapes
straight to space and liquid water cannot rest on the
surface. The sky is dusty pink from floating dust, and the
dust is the weather: regional storms can grow every few
years until they blot the Sun from the whole disk for months
(see `clouds-weather.md` and `atmosphere.md`).

Thin air is a fossil of a lost shield. Mars's global magnetic
field faded billions of years ago (see `magnetosphere.md`
and `interior.md`), and the solar wind has been stripping
the top of the air ever since — the story Earth avoided by
keeping its dynamo. What remains still sculpts: dust devils
wander the plains, morning frosts whiten the ground, and
ancient river valleys and lake beds record an era when the
blanket was thick enough for rivers (see `surface.md`).

Sources: NASA Mars facts page; NASA MAVEN escape pages.

## Titan — the strange cousin

Saturn's largest moon wears the only thick moon-air known:
about 94 percent nitrogen and 5–6 percent methane at 1.5 bar
— half again Earth's surface pressure, four times our
sea-level air density in the cold — with hydrogen, argon,
and a zoo of organics. Sunlight and Saturn-magnetosphere
particles split methane and nitrogen high up; the fragments
recombine into ethane, hydrogen cyanide, and complex
carbon-nitrogen smog that paints the world orange and hides
the surface. Methane plays water's role: it evaporates from
polar lakes and seas, condenses into clouds — Webb caught
convection over the northern lakes in 2025 — and falls as
chilly rain onto water-ice bedrock. Sunlight destroys
methane continuously, so something below must resupply it;
the source is still unknown. Beneath it all likely sits a
salty water-ammonia ocean.

Titan's ionosphere out-complicates Earth's: a main charged
layer near 1,200 km plus a lower one near 63 km, fed by
sunlight, Saturn's magnetosphere, and cosmic rays, dividing
the air into two radio-resonating regions.

Sources: NASA Titan facts pages; NASA Cassini Titan
science; NASA Webb Titan forecast (2025); Huygens GCMS
results (Nature, 2005).

## The giants — air all the way down

Jupiter and Saturn wear hydrogen and helium with no surface
beneath: ammonia clouds deck the visible tops in belts and
zones, water clouds hide deeper, and Juno's gravity readings
trace the jet streams 3,200 km down into a deepening fluid
that never meets ground (see `clouds-weather.md`). Uranus
and Neptune add methane that drinks red light and leaves
ice-giant blue; Neptune's winds are the fastest measured
anywhere. Lightning on the giants is a polar phenomenon
dropping ammonia-water hailstones the size of softballs —
upside down from Earth's equatorial storms.

With no ground, the concepts this ladder takes for granted —
surface pressure, sea level, a Kármán line — simply do not
apply. A giant's "surface" is wherever the clouds turn
opaque; below it the air thickens by degrees into an ocean
of hydrogen and, deeper still, exotic ices and rock. The
close-up portraits of these bottomless airs live at L07 and
L08 (`giants.md` at both levels); the optional L10
giant-planets page would hold their interiors.

Sources: NASA Jupiter and Saturn facts pages; NASA Juno
mission pages.

## The wisps — worlds barely wearing air

Not every world qualifies. Our Moon wears nothing at all —
sunrise shadows are pitch sharp, and the sky is black at
noon. Mercury wears only a trace exosphere of atoms knocked
off its rocks, constantly lost and renewed. Io — the most
volcanic world known — breathes sulfur dioxide from its
endless eruptions, but Jupiter's magnetic field strips it
almost as fast as it forms, so the air never builds (see
`volcanism.md` and `moons.md`). Thin air is the default
where gravity is weak, the wind is fierce, or no volcano
replenishes the loss.

## The glowing edge — ionosphere and airglow

Above the weather, sunlight rips electrons off atoms and the
upper air becomes a plasma: the ionosphere, roughly the
thermosphere up, where radio waves bounce and aurorae paint
(see `magnetosphere.md`). Earth's ionosphere thickens by day
and thins by night, breathes with the 11-year solar cycle,
and carries the currents that make compasses twitch in
storms.

Separate from aurorae is airglow: the whole sky faintly
shining all the time as atoms recombine and release light —
green oxygen bands near 100 km, red higher, sodium and
hydroxyl veils between. From orbit it rims the night planet
in green-gold; from the ground it sets the floor of natural
sky brightness no telescope escapes. Every world with air
glows a little; Venus, Mars, and Titan each wear their own
airglow chemistry, and Titan's double ionosphere makes its
edge the most structured after Earth's.

Sources: NASA aurora and ionosphere pages; NOAA space
weather pages.

## How airs are made, kept, and lost

Three ledgers decide every atmosphere. **Outgassing** fills:
ridges and volcanoes breathe steam, carbon dioxide, and
nitrogen from the interior (see `volcanism.md`); Titan's
methane source, whatever it is, must still be filling today
or the smog would already be gone. **Delivery** tops up:
icy impactors brought part of Earth's water, and Titan's
nitrogen isotopes point to comet-like Oort material rather
than Saturn's own. **Escape** drains: the young Sun's wind
stripped Earth's first hydrogen-helium envelope; Mars lost
its blanket after its shield died; light hydrogen liberated
from Titan's methane escapes irreversibly, raining ethane
down as the receipt.

Pressure is the running balance. Earth buried its carbon in
oceans and rocks and kept nitrogen; Venus kept everything
and cooked; Mars lost almost everything and froze; Titan
trades methane for smog in a steady state we still cannot
fully balance. The same three entries, four different
fortunes — and giants, where escape never wins because
gravity never loses.

Sources: NASA Earth, Mars, and Titan facts pages; NASA
MAVEN pages; JGR Venus review.

## Key numbers

| World | Main gases | Surface pressure | Surface temp | Cloud chemistry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Earth | 78% N2, 21% O2 | 1013 hPa | ~17°C avg | Water, 3 decks + towers |
| Venus | >96% CO2, 3.5% N2 | ~93 bar | 467°C | Sulfuric acid, 30–60 km |
| Mars | ~95% CO2 | <1% of Earth's | ~−63°C avg | Water-ice wisps + dust |
| Titan | 94% N2, ~5.6% CH4 | 1.5 bar | −179°C | Methane + organic smog |
| Jupiter | H2 + He | no surface | −110°C cloud tops | Ammonia belts and zones |
| Io | SO2 trace | near vacuum | −143°C avg | Volcanic plumes, no deck |

- Venus spin: 243 days backward; cloud tops circle in ~4
days at 360 km/h. Smallest surviving crater: ~1.5–2 km.
- Titan air: 50% denser surface pressure than Earth, 4x
sea-level density; main ionosphere ~1,200 km, lower layer
~63 km; subsurface ocean likely.
- Jupiter jets: 3,200 km deep; Great Red Spot roots ~500 km
(see `clouds-weather.md`).
- Anchor scale: the whole comparison spans one L10 frame,
10^6–10^8 m; every air here is thinner than its world is
wide by a thousand times or more.

Sources: NASA planet and moon facts pages; sources named
per section above.

## Example objects

Earth is the anchor: the only nitrogen–oxygen air with
oceans and an ozone shield — the standard every row of the
table is measured against.

Venus is the hottest air known: a 93-bar carbon-dioxide
ocean of gas under acid cloud, the fate of a world that
kept everything.

Mars is the lost air: a thin pink remainder still
sculpting dunes and devils, haunted by valleys its rivers
cut when the blanket was thick.

Titan is the second nitrogen air: thicker than Earth's,
methane-rained and smog-veiled, with lakes no water ever
touches and an ocean underneath.

Jupiter is the bottomless air: belts with no ground, jets
kilometers deep, storms wider than Earth that outlive
centuries.

Io is the air that never accumulates: breathed from
volcanoes and stripped by Jupiter as fast as it forms.

Sources: NASA Venus, Mars, Titan, Jupiter, and Io facts
pages.
