# Impacts — the scars of falling sky

This page is the L10 part doc for impacts: craters and basins
at whole-planet scale, the bombardment record, and how
impacts date every surface. The Moon's close-up lives in
`moons.md`; the dinosaur-ending crash sits in its dated
context in `lifetime.md`; the air an impact can wreck lives
in `atmosphere.md` and `atmospheres.md`. Craters 10–300 km
across will live in the planned L11 craters page; single
small craters belong at L12; the living threat — near-Earth
objects and planetary defense — will live in the planned L08
page.

From far away, impacts read as circles. A fresh crater is a
bright ring with rays splashing hundreds of kilometers; an
old basin is a dark-floored plain wider than continents; a
planet that kept every hit wears overlapping rings pole to
pole, while a planet that erases them shows only the latest
few. Earth hides its scars under water, air, and moving
plates — the Moon, with no eraser at all, remembers
everything.

## How craters work

Everything falls at cosmic speed — 15–20 km/s for rocks
crossing Earth's path — so even a stony kilometer-wide body
hits with the energy of millions of atomic bombs. The impact
vaporizes rock, digs a bowl, melts a sheet, and throws a
blanket of debris outward in seconds. Then gravity rebuilds
the hole, and the final shape depends on size:

**Simple craters (under ~15–20 km on Earth) — the bowls.**
Clean depressions with raised rims and breccia floors, like
a splash frozen mid-crown. Most small lunar craters keep
this shape forever.

**Complex craters — the ringed mountains.** Above the
threshold, the floor rebounds into a central peak or peak
ring while the walls slump in terraces. Tycho and Copernicus
— the bright-rayed pair any backyard telescope finds — are
the type examples, and their full portraits wait at L11.

**Multi-ring basins — the bullseyes.** The largest impacts
send ripples through the whole crust that freeze as
concentric mountain rings hundreds to thousands of
kilometers wide. The Moon's Orientale, with rings like a
frozen splash, is the textbook case, read from Lunar
Reconnaissance Orbiter gravity maps.

**The three great basins.** The South Pole–Aitken basin on
the Moon's far side spans about 2,500 km — nearly a quarter
of the Moon — and plunges 6–8 km deep, the largest, oldest,
and deepest basin recognized on the Moon; Chang'e-6 samples
returned in 2024 date it to about 4.25 billion years, and
its floor may expose the lower crust nowhere else visible.
Mars answers with Hellas, a similar giant about 2,300 km
across whose floor lies among the lowest ground on the
planet, where the thicker air once let Viking-era landers
down gently. Earth's eraser never lets scars this size
survive — but one almost did (see Vredefort below).

Sources: NASA SPA basin pages; NASA LRO pages; NASA Mars
science pages.

## Earth's erased record

Earth is hit as often as the Moon and shows almost nothing.
Oceans cover 71 percent of the target, weather grinds rims
down, and plate tectonics recycles the whole ocean floor
every ~180 million years (see `tectonics.md` and
`surface.md`). What survives does so on the old continental
cores — and the survivors are giants:

**Vredefort, South Africa — the largest known.** Formed
2.02 billion years ago by a 5–15 km rock at ~15–20 km/s,
originally 250–280 km across; two billion years of erosion
have planned it to the 90–120 km Vredefort Dome visible
today, ringed by younger rock. A NASA Earth Observatory
frame from July 2025 calls it the world's oldest and
largest known impact structure.

**Sudbury, Canada — the miner's basin.** About 130–250 km
across (estimates vary with erosion), 1.85 billion years
old, filled with nickel and copper ores from the melted
crust — the impact paid for a mining district.

**Chicxulub, Mexico — the famous one.** About 180 km wide,
buried under Yucatan limestone and half the Gulf, drilled
but never seen: 66 million years ago a ~10 km carbonaceous
asteroid struck, threw sulfur and dust worldwide, and ended
three quarters of species including the non-avian dinosaurs
(see `lifetime.md`). Shocked quartz, a gravity anomaly, and
the worldwide iridium layer convicted it; oil prospectors
found the ring in the late 1970s. Some estimates stretch the
original to 260 km.

Smaller fresh scars survive where the desert guards them —
the 1.2-km Meteor Crater in Arizona is the L12 portrait —
but the rule stands: Earth remembers only its biggest and
its youngest hits, and dates the rest by association.

Sources: NASA Earth Observatory Vredefort (2025); JGR
Vredefort revision (2022); Britannica Chicxulub; USGS
impact pages.

## Dating by counting — how impacts tell time

With no eraser, the Moon is a clock. Old ground wears many
craters; young lava seas wear few — count the craters per
area, calibrate against Apollo samples of known age, and any
surface in the solar system gets a date. The lunar maria
flooded 4.2–1.2 billion years ago (see `moons.md`); the
lightly cratered plains of Mars are young; Mercury's
saturated highlands are ancient. Every timeline in these
guides — including `lifetime.md` — leans on crater counts
somewhere.

The early clock ran fast. The Late Heavy Bombardment —
whatever its true shape, debated between a spike and a long
decline — resurfaced the inner worlds around 4 billion
years ago (see `lifetime.md:69-71`), digging SPA and Hellas
among hundreds of basins since erased on Earth and
preserved on the Moon. Today's flux is gentle by comparison:
a ~20 km impactor falls about once every 300 million to a
billion years; city-killers every thousands; car-sized rocks
burn up as fireballs somewhere yearly, most over ocean or
wilderness. Chelyabinsk 2013 — a ~20 m rock bursting over
Russia with 400 kilotons of energy — is the modern reminder
that the rain never stopped; the watch against the next one
will live in the planned L08 near-Earth-objects page.

Sources: USGS impact-rate studies; NASA fireball and
near-Earth-object pages.

## How it interacts with the others

Impacts dig what the interior hides (see `interior.md`).
SPA's depth may expose the Moon's lower crust; Vredefort's
dome brings deep rock to the surface; Chicxulub's drill
cores read target limestone turned to breccia. Before
seismology, impacts were the only deep probes — and on
worlds without quakes, they still are.

Impacts reset the face (see `surface.md`). Lava seas fill
basins, rays brighten highlands for hundreds of millions of
years, and each giant impact melts and differentiates the
target anew — the Moon-forming crash in `lifetime.md` is
this page's logic at planet scale.

Impacts wreck the blanket (see `atmosphere.md` and
`atmospheres.md`). Dust and sulfur from Chicxulub blocked
the Sun for years; Toba did the same from below (see
`volcanism.md`); the smallest air — Mars's — cannot even
slow small meteors, while Venus's blanket burns up
everything under ~1.5–2 km. Air thickness decides which
rocks reach the ground.

The companion keeps the cleanest record (see `moons.md`).
The Moon's highlands are saturated, its maria counted, its
rays — Tycho's splash reaches halfway across the near side —
the freshest paint. Mercury, geologically quiet for
billions of years, runs second; Earth last.

Sources: NASA SPA and LRO pages; NASA Earth Observatory;
NASA Moon facts page.

## Key numbers

- SPA: ~2,500 km wide, 6–8 km deep, ~4.25 billion years;
nearly a quarter of the Moon.
- Hellas: ~2,300 km across; among the lowest ground on Mars.
- Vredefort: 250–280 km original, 2.02 Ga; largest known on
Earth; dome remnant 90–120 km.
- Chicxulub: ~180 km, 66 Ma, ~10 km impactor; ended ~75%
of species.
- Sudbury: ~130–250 km, 1.85 Ga; nickel basin.
- Flux: ~20 km impactor every 300M–1B years; ocean floor
recycled every ~180M years, so no Earth crater is older.
- Anchor scale: basins to 2,500 km fill an L10 frame
(10^6–10^8 m); single craters belong at L11–L12.

Sources: NASA, USGS, and JGR pages named above.

## Example objects

The Moon is the anchor record: saturated highlands, counted
maria, bright rays — the clock every other surface is read
against.

Mars keeps its giants: Hellas and its kin, softened by dust
and wind but unsubducted, readable for billions of years.

Earth erases: oceans, weather, and plates destroy all but
Vredefort, Sudbury, and Chicxulub — the three survivors that
prove the rain falls here too.

Mercury is the quiet archive: cratered grey plains,
unrecycled since its engine cooled (see L08
`terrestrials.md`).

Vesta is the small witness: a 525-km asteroid whose south
pole wears two overlapping basins nearly its own width —
impacts at the scale of the world itself (see L08
`belts.md`).

Sources: NASA Moon, Mars, and Mercury facts pages; NASA
Dawn Vesta pages.
