# Tectonics — the cracked planet

This page is the L10 part doc for the cracks: plates, faults,
rifts, earthquakes, and tsunamis seen at whole-planet scale.
The engine below lives in `interior.md`; the face it builds
lives in `surface.md`; the air it breathes lives in
`atmosphere.md`; the dated story runs through `lifetime.md`.
The regional view — named ranges, rift valleys, and fault
zones at 100–1,000 km — will live in the planned L11 pages;
single faults and small ruptures belong at L12.

Earth is the only known world whose whole skin is cracked into
moving pieces. From far away you cannot see the cracks
themselves, only what they build: a globe-girdling underwater
mountain range where new skin is born, deep trenches where old
skin dives back down, folded mountain belts where pieces
collide, and chains of volcanoes and earthquake dots tracing
every boundary.

## How it looks

The skin is broken into about seven or eight great plates and
many smaller ones. The great ones carry whole oceans and
continents: Pacific, North American, South American, African,
Eurasian, Australian-Indian, and Antarctic, plus midsized pieces
such as Nazca, Cocos, Caribbean, Arabian, Philippine, Juan de
Fuca, and Scotia. A single plate can hold both ocean and
continent — North America rides with half the northern
Atlantic, and the Pacific plate is almost all ocean except for
slivers of California west of the San Andreas Fault.

Sources: USGS This Dynamic Earth plates pages; USGS plate
motions FAQ.

There are three kinds of boundary, and each looks different:

**Where plates pull apart — ridges and rifts.** New crust
rises as hot rock wells up and the pieces separate. Under the
oceans this builds the mid-ocean ridge system, the longest
mountain range on Earth, winding through every ocean basin.
The best-known stretch splits the Atlantic from the Arctic
past Africa into the southern ocean; it spreads about 2.5 cm
per year — about as fast as fingernails grow — and its central
rift valley is shaken by constant small quakes. Where a
continent itself is splitting, the crack shows on land: long
straight valleys with steep walls, shallow quakes, and young
volcanoes. The East African Rift and its Afar triangle are the
living example — a continent visibly unzipping.

**Where plates collide — trenches, arcs, and folded ranges.**
Old ocean floor dives back into the mantle beneath its
neighbor, bending the seafloor into a deep trench. The diving
slab melts as it sinks and feeds a curved chain of volcanoes
behind the trench — island arcs such as Japan and the
Aleutians, or continental arcs such as the Andes. Where two
continents meet, neither sinks easily, so the land crumples
upward instead: the Himalayas are still rising as India pushes
into Asia. The deepest seafloor on Earth — the Mariana Trench
at about 11 km down — is a collision scar of this kind (see
`surface.md`).

**Where plates grind past each other — transform faults.**
Neither born nor buried, the skin simply slides sideways along
a near-vertical crack. The San Andreas Fault is the type
example: about 1,300 km long, slicing through two thirds of
California, with the Pacific side grinding past North America
for about 10 million years at roughly 5 cm per year on
average. Motion along such faults is mostly horizontal, and
the quakes they make are shallow and sharp.

Sources: USGS Understanding Plate Motions; USGS Mid-Atlantic
Ridge pages; USGS San Andreas pages.

Motion is slow by human standards and relentless by geologic
ones. The fastest plates (Pacific, Australian, Nazca) top
10 cm per year in places; the slowest (North American) creeps
near 1 cm per year in the south to almost 4 cm in the north.
Plates move as rigid caps rotating over the globe, so one
plate can move at different rates in different places. The
San Andreas itself slips about 35 mm per year near Parkfield
and only about 18 mm per year in the south — the missing
motion is shared with sister faults such as San Jacinto at
about 14 mm per year.

Sources: USGS plate-motion FAQ; LibreTexts geosciences
compilation of Rice University plate data; USGS southern
California fault pages.

## Earthquakes — the cracks talking

A quake is a crack slipping all at once after years of stuck,
silent strain. Almost all large quakes sit on plate
boundaries, which is why quake dots trace the plates better
than any drawn map: the mid-ocean ridges hum with small
shallow events, transform faults snap in sharp shallow
shocks, and subduction zones host the true giants.

The giants happen on megathrusts — the stuck contact where
one plate dives beneath another. When centuries of locked
strain let go, the seafloor jumps meters in seconds over
hundreds of kilometers. The ten largest ever recorded are all
megathrust events:

| Rank | Size | Year | Place |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | M9.5 | 1960 | Valdivia, Chile |
| 2 | M9.2 | 1964 | Alaska, Prince William Sound |
| 3 | M9.1 | 2004 | Sumatra-Andaman |
| 4 | M9.1 | 2011 | Tohoku, Japan |
| 5 | M9.0 | 1952 | Kamchatka, Russia |
| 6 | M8.8 | 2025 | Kamchatka, Russia |
| 7 | M8.8 | 2010 | Maule, Chile |
| 8 | M8.8 | 1906 | Ecuador-Colombia |
| 9 | M8.7 | 1965 | Rat Islands, Alaska |
| 10 | M8.6 | 1950 | Assam-Tibet |

The 1960 Valdivia quake remains the largest ever measured:
about 1,655 dead and 2 million homeless. The 1964 Alaska
quake — the largest in US history — shook for four and a
half minutes, killed 131 people, and cost about 3.1 billion
dollars in 2024 terms; most deaths came not from shaking but
from tsunamis and landslide waves. The 2004 Sumatra quake
killed more than 280,000 around the Indian Ocean; the 2011
Tohoku quake killed more than 15,000 and displaced 130,000.

Sources: USGS 10-largest list (updated 2025); USGS Alaska
1964 pages; USGS Tohoku and Sumatra event pages.

Not every dangerous quake is a giant. Shallow crustal faults
near cities can kill at magnitude 6–7 because the shaking
starts almost underneath: the 1933 Long Beach quake (M6.4)
on the Newport-Inglewood Fault and the 1918 San Jacinto quake
(M6.7) are southern California warnings of exactly this
kind. The San Andreas itself can make about M8.2; its last
great southern rupture was the M7.9 Fort Tejon quake of 1857.

Quakes are also our X-ray of the planet. Side-to-side waves
that vanish mark the liquid outer core; waves that bend and
slow map the mantle, the D-double-prime floor, and the
growing solid center (see `interior.md`). The deepest hole
ever dug reaches only about 12 km — everything deeper is
read from quake waves, lab-squeezed rocks, and gravity.

Sources: USGS Inside the Earth pages; USGS southern
California fault pages.

## Tsunamis — the sea answers the slip

A tsunami is born when the seafloor itself moves. In a great
megathrust quake, one side of the fault lurches up and the
other drops, shoving the whole water column above it. That
hump of water then races across the open ocean at jet speed —
harmless in deep water at under a meter high — and rears up
only when it shoals near shore, arriving as a fast flood, not
a curling surf wave. Underwater landslides set off by the
shaking can make their own local waves the same way; in
Alaska in 1964, landslide waves caused most of the deaths.

Two modern disasters wrote the rulebook. In 2004 a M9.1
rupture off Sumatra lifted the seafloor along more than a
thousand kilometers of trench and sent walls of water across
the Indian Ocean, killing across South Asia and East Africa.
In 2011 a M9.1 rupture off Japan did the same across the
Pacific, with waves overtopping seawalls built for smaller
events. Both struck subduction zones where an oceanic plate
dives beneath its neighbor — the same setting as Chile,
Alaska, Kamchatka, and Cascadia.

Sources: USGS Subduction Zone Science tsunami pages; USGS
Sumatra 2004 and Tohoku 2011 event pages; NOAA tsunami
program pages.

## How it changes over time

The plates never stop, so the map is always expiring. Ocean
floor born at a ridge rides its slow conveyor for up to about
180 million years, then dives back down — which is why no
ocean floor is older than that while continents carry rocks
near 4 billion years old. The Atlantic widens year by year
because its new floor is not being swallowed as fast as it
grows; the Pacific shrinks for the opposite reason, ringed by
trenches that devour its edges.

Over hundreds of millions of years the pieces gather and
part. Continents merge into supercontinents, then split
again; the last full gathering broke up over the past 200
million years into the continents of `surface.md`, and
Australia only parted from Antarctica about 50 million years
ago. India has raced north since, colliding with Asia to
raise the Himalayas. Fifty million years hence the map will
be unrecognizable, but the engine — heat escaping from
`interior.md` — will still be driving it.

Sources: USGS historical-perspective pages; USGS plate-motion
FAQ.

Quake strain builds on human timescales and releases in
seconds. A fault locked for a century at 3 cm per year owes
about 3 meters of slip; when it pays, the ground jumps. That
is why the same boundary can sit quiet for generations and
then produce the decade's largest disaster — and why the
record in `lifetime.md` reads as long calm punctuated by
catastrophe.

## How it was created

The cracks are younger than the planet. Earth sorted into
core, mantle, and crust about 4.56 billion years ago while
largely melted, and the Moon-forming crash remelted much of
it again (see `interior.md` and `lifetime.md`). Continents
began as small granite-like scraps stacked onto ancient
cores — zircon time capsules in Canada read near 4.0 billion
years — while heavy ocean floor recycled back down from the
start.

When exactly the modern global crack network switched on is
still debated; the dated spine in `lifetime.md` carries the
current reading. What is certain is the mechanism: a hot
mantle that can creep over millions of years drags the stiff
outer shell, opens oceans at ridges, closes them at
trenches, and keeps the face forever young in patches. Small
worlds lose this trick as they cool — Mars and the Moon are
the still faces that result.

Sources: USGS oldest-rock pages; Natural Resources Canada
Acasta pages; NASA solar-system formation pages.

## How it interacts with the others

Heat from below drives every crack (see `interior.md`). The
slow mantle flow drags plates centimeters per year, opens
ridges, feeds hotspots such as Hawaii, Iceland, and
Yellowstone from deep plumes, and clusters quakes along
every boundary. Without that heat the face would freeze
still.

The cracks build the face (see `surface.md`). Ridges grow
the longest mountain range — hidden underwater, four times
the Andes, Rockies, and Himalayas combined. Trenches dig the
deepest scars. Collisions raise Everest and its kin while
wind, water, and ice wear them back down.

The cracks breathe into the blanket (see `atmosphere.md`).
Ridges and volcanoes exhale steam, carbon dioxide, and other
gases that built the first air and still top it up; sinking
plates drag water and carbon back down, closing a slow loop
between inside and outside. Big eruptions can cool the
planet for a year or two; the 2022 Hunga Tonga blast instead
threw record water vapor into the stratosphere and trapped a
little extra heat for a few years.

The Moon tugs at the cracked skin too (see `moons.md`). Its
pull raises ocean tides twice a day and tiny solid-Earth
tides as well, slowly braking Earth's spin while the Moon
drifts about 4 cm farther each year. Tides do not drive the
plates, but they load and unload faults on a strict twice-
daily schedule.

The shield guards the whole machine (see
`magnetosphere.md`). Made by the same churning iron that the
mantle draws heat from, the magnetic field bends the solar
wind around the planet so air and oceans are not stripped
away — the precondition for oceans, and therefore for wet,
slippery plates that keep moving instead of seizing up.

Sources: USGS plate-tectonics and hotspot pages; NASA Earth
facts page; NASA Moon facts page; NASA Hunga Tonga studies.

## Key numbers

- Plates: 7–8 great plates plus midsized pieces (Nazca,
Cocos, Caribbean, Arabian, Philippine, Juan de Fuca,
Scotia).
- Speeds: fastest plates over 10 cm/year; slowest near
1 cm/year; Mid-Atlantic Ridge about 2.5 cm/year; San Andreas
about 5 cm/year averaged over 10 million years, 18–35 mm/year
by segment today.
- San Andreas: about 1,300 km long, capable to about M8.2,
last great southern rupture M7.9 in 1857.
- Largest quake: M9.5 Valdivia, Chile, 1960; largest US
quake M9.2 Alaska, 1964 (4.5 minutes of shaking).
- Deadliest recent: Sumatra-Andaman 2004 M9.1 (over
280,000 dead); Tohoku 2011 M9.1 (over 15,000 dead,
130,000 displaced).
- Oldest ocean floor: about 180 million years; oldest
continental grains: about 4.0 billion years.
- Anchor world: Earth 12,756 km across; cracks span the
whole disk at 10^6–10^8 m.

Sources: USGS pages named above; USGS 10-largest list
(2025); NASA Earth facts page.

## Example objects

Earth is the anchor: the only world with a globe-spanning
crack network — ridges birthing skin, trenches swallowing
it, continents colliding, and quake dots tracing every
seam.

Mars is the cooled contrast. Half Earth's width, its shield
faded, its air thinned, and its plates — if it ever had
them — stopped. Olympus Mons, about 22 km high, grew over
one unmoving hotspot instead of a drifting chain, and the
Valles Marineris canyon stretch is a crack that never became
a plate boundary.

Venus is the seized contrast. Slightly smaller than Earth,
it resurfaces itself in planet-wide volcanic episodes
instead of steady plate motion; whether its highlands hide
folded ranges or its rifts are starting cracks is still open.

The Moon is the dead contrast. Layered long ago but too
small to stay hot, it has no plates, no ridges, no trenches
— only ancient scars and the wrinkle ridges of a cooling,
shrinking skin.

Io is the squeezed contrast. About Moon-sized but kneaded
without pause by Jupiter's pull, its skin is constantly
repaved by volcanoes — plate tectonics replaced by tidal
violence.

Sources: NASA Mars, Venus, Moon, and Io facts pages; NASA
and USGS Olympus Mons pages.
