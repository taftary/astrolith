# Volcanism — the planet on fire

This page is the L10 part doc for fire: volcanoes, eruptions,
lava fields, and ice volcanoes at whole-planet scale. The
cracks they rise through live in `tectonics.md`; the engine
that melts the rock lives in `interior.md`; the face they
build lives in `surface.md`; the air they feed and sometimes
cool lives in `atmosphere.md`; the dated story runs through
`lifetime.md`. The regional view — named volcanic fields,
arcs, and calderas at 100–1,000 km — will live in the planned
L11 pages; single cones and small craters belong at L12.

From far away, volcanism reads as geography, not flame. No
eruption is visible from across a planet — what you see is
the record: a lone white-capped cone higher than everything
around it, a chain of islands trailing behind a moving plate,
dark plains of frozen lava filling low ground, a 600-km-wide
bulge on a rust-red disk. Earth wears thousands of these
scars; Io, a moon barely wider than ours, is being resurfaced
by them right now.

## How it looks

Where the magma comes from decides the shape. Thin runny
basalt builds low, broad shields; thick gassy magma builds
steep cones that blow themselves apart; collapsing chambers
leave holes wider than mountains. Geologists group volcanoes
into a few main kinds:

**Shield volcanoes — the broad warriors.** Built almost
entirely of fluid lava flows that travel far and cool as thin
sheets, piling thousands deep into a dome shaped like an
inverted warrior shield, with slopes under about 10 degrees.
Hawaii's Mauna Loa is the largest volcano on Earth: about
120 km across and 9 km tall measured from its seafloor base
— taller than Everest base-to-summit, most of it underwater
(see `surface.md`). Eruptions are usually gentle fountains
and rivers of lava from summit vents and flank rift zones.

**Stratovolcanoes — the grand cones.** Also called composite
volcanoes: steep, symmetrical mountains of alternating lava,
ash, cinders, and bombs, rising as much as about 2,400 m
(8,000 feet) above their bases. Fuji, Rainier, and St. Helens
are the type. Their sticky andesite and dacite magmas trap
gas until it bursts, so these are the mountains that
explode.

**Cinder cones — the small sharp ones.** The simplest kind:
gas-charged lava blown from a single vent, cooling as it
flies and piling as cinders around the hole, rarely more than
about 300 m high, usually with a bowl crater on top.
Paricutin in Mexico grew one on a farm in 1943 and stopped a
decade later; Sunset Crater in Arizona is its preserved
American cousin. Thousands dot western North America, often
on the flanks of bigger volcanoes. Most erupt only once.

**Lava domes — the squeezed toothpaste.** Very sticky
rhyolite magma that barely flows, piling into rounded domes
of blocky rock over the vent. They grow quietly, then fail
loudly: the 1980 St. Helens blast and the 1990s Soufriere
Hills collapses were dome failures sending hot ash clouds
racing downhill.

**Calderas — the holes bigger than mountains.** When a giant
chamber empties, the roof falls in, leaving depressions from
about 1.5 km across to monsters 100 km long. Crater Lake in
Oregon fills a 10-km caldera around its Wizard Island cinder
cone. Yellowstone's most recent of three giant collapses was
about 600,000 years ago (see `interior.md`). Toba in Sumatra
blew about 74,000 years ago in an eruption 40 times a
hydrogen bomb and a thousand times Krakatau, dropping global
temperatures 5–9 degrees for a decade — close enough to a
bottleneck that some read it in human genes.

**Fissures and flood basalts — fire without a mountain.**
Basalt sometimes pours from long cracks instead of a central
vent, flooding whole countrysides flow on flow into broad
plateaus: Iceland's Laki flow, the Columbia Plateau of the
Pacific Northwest, India's Deccan, Siberia's Traps. The dark
lunar maria our Moon shows (see `moons.md`) are flood
basalts on another world — ancient lava seas, now still.

**Maars and tuff rings — the water explosions.** Where
rising magma meets groundwater, the flash to steam blasts a
wide low crater, often holding a lake. They erupt once and
stay as circles of water in green fields.

Sources: USGS Principal Types of Volcanoes; USGS About
Volcanoes; NPS Types of Volcanoes; Smithsonian on Toba;
USGS Yellowstone pages.

On a whole planet, volcanoes sit in three addresses. Most
rise over subduction zones in curved island and continental
arcs — Japan, the Aleutians, the Andes — fed by diving slabs
(see `tectonics.md`). Some split continents and oceans along
rifts and ridges, mostly hidden underwater. And some stand
alone far from any boundary over deep hot columns: Hawaii
grows as the Pacific plate slides over one, leaving Kauai's
5.5-million-year rocks behind the Big Island's under-0.7-
million ones; Iceland and Yellowstone are fed the same way,
though scientists still debate how deep and how fixed such
hotspots are.

Sources: USGS hotspot pages; USGS This Dynamic Earth.

## Eruptions — how the mountain talks

Eruptions sort by what the magma lets escape. Runny basalt
lets gas out and flows: lava fountains tens of meters high,
rivers that outrun a person, plumes that reach the
troposphere at most. Sticky magma holds gas in and bursts:
ash columns tens of kilometers high, pyroclastic flows —
ground-hugging clouds of hot gas and rock — that flatten
everything downhill, and caldera collapse when the chamber
empties.

The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) grades blasts 0–8, each
step about ten times the ejected volume. Anything VEI 6 and
up writes itself into climate and history:

| Eruption | Year | VEI | What it did |
|---|---|---|---|
| Toba, Sumatra | ~74,000 ago | 8 | Global volcanic winter, 5–9 degrees cooling for a decade |
| Tambora, Sumbawa | 1815 | 7 | Largest in recorded history; 1816 the Year Without a Summer; over 70,000 dead with famines |
| Krakatau, Indonesia | 1883 | 6 | Island destroyed, tsunamis, sunsets painted worldwide for years |
| Pinatubo, Philippines | 1991 | 6 | About half a degree of global cooling for two years; the modern calibration event |
| St. Helens, USA | 1980 | 5 | North flank collapsed; lateral blast leveled 600 square km of forest |
| Hunga Tonga | 2022 | 5–6 | Record water vapor thrown into the stratosphere; small extra heat for a few years (see `atmosphere.md`) |

Sources: Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program (Tambora,
Toba); USGS Tambora pages; NOAA Tambora pages; NASA Hunga
Tonga studies.

Big blasts cool the planet and small gases warm the record.
Sulfur thrown into the stratosphere becomes a veil of
droplets that reflects sunlight — Tambora took a full degree
Fahrenheit off the northern summer and starved continents.
The same veil is why Pinatubo is the yardstick every climate
model is checked against. Between catastrophes, the steady
breathing of ridges and small eruptions tops up the air with
carbon dioxide and water, building the blanket grain by
grain.

## How it was created

Volcanoes built the world twice: first the air and seas,
then the land. The newborn Earth's first hydrogen-helium
gases were stripped by the young Sun's wind; the air we have
was breathed out of the planet itself — steam, carbon
dioxide, and nitrogen vented for hundreds of millions of
years — while icy impactors delivered part of the water that
became the oceans (see `atmosphere.md`). Every breath we
take is recycled outgassing.

Flood basalts mark the violent chapters. The Siberian Traps
poured about 252 million years ago alongside the worst mass
extinction on record; the Deccan poured as the dinosaurs'
era ended. Whether the lava or the contemporaneous Chicxulub
impact deserves more blame is still argued — both halves of
the story sit in `lifetime.md`.

Hotspot chains write plate motion in stone. Because the deep
column stays roughly put while the plate slides over it,
islands age along the chain: Kauai at 5.5 million years,
Oahu younger, the Big Island still growing today, and a new
seamount — Loihi — already rising southeast of Hawaii. Read
backward, the Emperor seamounts bend where the Pacific plate
changed direction tens of millions of years ago.

Sources: NASA Earth formation pages; USGS hotspot pages;
NASA astrobiology extinction pages.

## How it interacts with the others

Heat from below is the source (see `interior.md`). Mantle
flow drags plates, opens ridges where most of Earth's lava
erupts unseen underwater, and feeds deep plumes to Hawaii,
Iceland, and Yellowstone. The D-double-prime floor where
plumes are born and sunken slabs rest is the deepest half of
this page's story.

The cracks are the plumbing (see `tectonics.md`). Arcs trace
subduction, rifts trace splitting, transforms mostly sit
quiet — quake dots and volcano dots draw the same map in
different ink. Olympus Mons grew to 22 km precisely because
Mars has no moving plates: one hotspot, one unmoving crust,
one pile growing for billions of years.

The blanket is fed and veiled (see `atmosphere.md`). Ridges
and volcanoes exhale the gases that built the first air and
still top it up; sinking plates drag water and carbon back
down. Great blasts cool the planet for years; Hunga Tonga's
water-vapor blast warmed a little instead — the exception
that tests the rule.

The squeezed worlds erupt without plates at all. Io —
Jupiter's innermost great moon, barely wider than ours — is
kneaded 100 m up and down each orbit by Jupiter with Europa
and Ganymede keeping time, and the friction melts its
inside: hundreds of volcanoes, lava fountains dozens of
kilometers high, plumes reaching 500 km, calderas 200 km
across, lava lakes of molten silicate. It repaves itself so
fast it has almost no impact craters. Where water replaces
rock, the same physics makes ice volcanoes: Enceladus vents
plumes of vapor and ice grains from its tiger-stripe faults
at the south pole, fed by a hidden ocean; Triton heats
nitrogen geysers the same tidal way.

Sources: NASA Io facts pages; USGS Volcano Watch on Io;
NASA JPL Enceladus shear-heating study; USGS Olympus Mons
topography.

## Key numbers

- Kinds: shields (slopes under ~10 degrees), stratovolcanoes
(to ~2,400 m above base), cinder cones (under ~300 m, often
one eruption), domes, calderas (1.5–100 km), fissures and
flood basalts, maars.
- Largest on Earth: Mauna Loa, ~120 km across, 9 km from
seafloor base; largest known: Olympus Mons, over 600 km
across, 22 km high, summit caldera ~65 by 85 km.
- Largest in recorded history: Tambora 1815, VEI 7, Year
Without a Summer; largest in human experience: Toba
~74,000 years ago, VEI 8, 5–9 degrees of cooling.
- Io: hundreds of volcanoes, fountains dozens of km high,
plumes to 500 km, surface tides of 100 m in solid rock.
- Yellowstone: three giant collapses, most recent ~600,000
years ago; Hawaii chain: Kauai 5.5 Ma to Big Island under
0.7 Ma.
- Anchor world: Earth 12,756 km across; most of its lava
erupts unseen on underwater ridges.

Sources: USGS, NPS, and Smithsonian pages named above; USGS
Olympus Mons topography; NASA JPL Olympus Mons mosaic; NASA
Io facts; NASA Earth facts.

## Example objects

Earth is the anchor: the only world with all three volcanic
addresses at once — arcs, rifts and ridges, and hotspots —
plus oceans that hide most of the eruptions and an air
blanket the volcanoes built.

Mars is the piled contrast. Half Earth's width with no
moving plates, it stacked Olympus Mons — 22 km high, over
600 km wide, five times the largest Earth shield — over one
unmoving hotspot, with the whole Tharsis rise behind it and
lava flows possibly younger than a million years.

Venus is the smothered contrast. Slightly smaller than
Earth, it resurfaced itself in planet-wide volcanic episodes
within the last few hundred million years instead of steady
plate-driven eruption; its highlands may hide folded ranges,
its rifts may be starting cracks.

The Moon is the frozen contrast. Its dark maria are flood
basalts from over 3 billion years ago — lava seas as wide as
continents, now utterly still on a world too small to stay
hot.

Io is the tidal contrast. About Moon-sized, kneaded by
Jupiter, it erupts more than every other solid world
combined — silicate lava lakes and 500-km plumes on a world
with no plates at all.

Enceladus is the ice contrast. A moon 500 km across venting
ocean spray through south-pole faults — volcanism where the
rock is ice and the lava is water.

Sources: NASA Mars, Venus, Moon, Io, and Enceladus facts
pages; USGS Olympus Mons topography; Smithsonian GVP.
