# Clouds and weather — the winds around the world

This page is the L10 part doc for winds, clouds, and storms at
whole-planet scale: the cloud families, the global wind belts,
and the storm systems seen across the full disk. The layered
air they move through lives in `atmosphere.md`; the face they
rain on lives in `surface.md`; the dated story runs through
`lifetime.md`. The regional view — hurricanes, monsoons, and
fronts at 100–1,000 km — will live in the planned L11 weather
page; single storm cells and city skies belong at L12.

From far away, weather is the planet thinking out loud.
White swirls curl off the equator and trail across the middle
latitudes; long bands line up in rows; deserts sit clear
under sinking air while the equator stays cloudy almost every
day. Earth wears its winds where everyone can see them — and
so does every world with air, each in its own style: Jupiter
in permanent stripes, Venus under one unbroken veil, Mars in
dust that sometimes swallows the whole disk.

## How it looks

Clouds are water made visible — vapor cooling until it
condenses on dust into droplets or ice crystals. Seen from
above they sort by height into three decks plus the towers
that punch through them:

**High clouds (above ~6 km) — the ice veils.** Thin, wispy
cirrus of pure ice crystals, and their sheet form,
cirrostratus, which rings the Sun and Moon with halos. They
drift with the upper winds and change slowly.

**Middle clouds (2–6 km) — the grey sheets and rolls.**
Altostratus spreads flat and dim; altocumulus lines up in
rippled rows. A lowering, thickening middle deck is the
classic sign that rain is on its way.

**Low clouds (below ~2 km) — the cotton and the blanket.**
Cumulus heaps with flat bottoms and cauliflower tops on fair
days; stratus spreads as one unbroken grey sheet; fog is
stratus touching the ground. Nimbostratus is the rain sheet
that soaks for hours.

**Towering clouds — the storms themselves.** Cumulonimbus
rises from near the ground to the tropopause ceiling, where
it hits the stable stratosphere and spreads into the flat
anvil top that passenger jets see from above (see
`atmosphere.md`). Inside, air races up and down at highway
speeds, lightning fires thousands of times a day across the
tropics, and hail and tornadoes are born. From orbit, a field
of anvils marks the day's storm line.

The highest clouds of all — glowing-blue noctilucent wisps
near 80 km — live in `atmosphere.md`.

Sources: NOAA JetStream cloud pages; NASA Earth Observatory
cloud pages.

What moves the clouds is the planet-scale engine: the Sun
heats the equator more than the poles, warm air rises,
cold air sinks, and rotation bends every flow sideways. The
result is three great rolling cells per hemisphere instead
of one simple loop:

**The Hadley cell — the tropical roller.** Air rises near
the equator almost daily in towering storms, flows poleward
high up, and sinks around 30 degrees north and south. The
rising branch is the cloudy Intertropical Convergence Zone;
the sinking branches are the clear belts where most deserts
sit — Sahara, Arabian, Australian, Kalahari — dry because
the air arrives already wrung out.

**The Ferrel cell — the middle-latitude mixer.** Driven more
by friction with the cells on either side than by heat, it
carries surface air poleward and eastward between about 35
and 60 degrees — the stormy westerlies that sweep weather
systems across North America, Europe, and the southern
ocean.

**The polar cell — the small cold cap.** Air sinks over the
poles under the polar highs and flows outward as the polar
easterlies, meeting the westerlies along the stormy
50–60-degree fronts.

Between the cells run the surface wind belts sailors named:
the northeast and southeast trade winds blowing toward the
equator, the mid-latitude westerlies, and the polar
easterlies. High above, four jet streams — two polar, two
subtropical — race west-to-east around 9 km up where hot and
cold air masses meet, averaging about 110 miles per hour and
topping 250 in winter polar jets, steering every storm below
them.

Sources: NOAA JetStream global-circulation and jet-stream
pages.

## Storms — the engine showing off

Storms are heat moving in a hurry, and each kind has its
characteristic size:

**Thunderstorms (10–30 km) — the daily workhorses.** Single
cells live under an hour; lines and complexes march for
hundreds of kilometers along fronts and out of mountain
heat. The planned L12 sky page holds the close-up.

**Tropical cyclones (500–1,000 km) — the great spirals.**
Born over warm ocean water, spinning around a calm eye,
they are the largest storms that fit inside an L11 frame —
which is why their full portrait waits for the planned L11
weather page. From the whole disk they read as perfect white
comma-spirals trailing across the tropics in late summer.

**Mid-latitude cyclones (1,000–3,000 km) — the weather
makers.** Where polar and subtropical air collide along the
jet, low-pressure systems coil clouds into comma shapes big
enough to cover a continent's third, dragging cold fronts
and warm fronts behind them. Most of a week's weather
outside the tropics is one of these passing over.

**Tornadoes (under ~2 km) — the violent footnote.** Born
inside the fiercest supercells where winds shear with height.
Invisible at L10; their parent storms are not.

**Dust storms — the dry extreme.** Where the air sinks and
the land is bare, wind lifts the ground itself. Saharan dust
crosses the whole Atlantic yearly to fertilize the Amazon
(see `atmosphere.md`). On Mars the same physics scales up
without limit: thin air, no oceans to wash dust out, and
every few years a regional storm grows until it blots the
Sun from the entire disk for months (see `atmosphere.md`).

**Jupiter — weather as permanent geography.** With no solid
surface to slow them, winds split into alternating dark
belts and bright zones flowing opposite ways, reaching 539
km per hour at the equator, with jet streams plumbing 3,200
km deep. The Great Red Spot — an anticyclone wider than
Earth, watched for over 300 years since its confirmed
sighting in 1831 — is the largest storm known: 25,500 miles
across in the late 1800s, 14,500 in the 1979 Voyager
flybys, about 10,250 in 2014, shrinking some 580 miles per
year while growing taller, its roots reaching about 500 km
below the cloud tops. Juno found the poles ringed with
networks of cyclones that never leave. Saturn wears the
same banded style with its own polar hexagon and pale
gold veils.

Sources: NOAA JetStream tropical and thunderstorm pages;
NASA Jupiter facts page; NASA Great Red Spot shrinking
pages; NASA Juno mission pages.

## How it changes over time

Weather is today; climate is the decades-long average (see
`atmosphere.md`). The bridge between them is wobble: the
jet streams bulge north and south in great Rossby waves, so
storms wander; every few years the tropical Pacific warms
(El Nino) or cools (La Nina) and the whole tropical storm
pattern shifts with it, drying some continents and
flooding others.

The year's big rhythm is tilt, not distance: 23.4 degrees of
lean steadied by the Moon (see `atmosphere.md`), swinging
strong sunshine between hemispheres. The monsoons — whole-
ocean winds that reverse with the seasons and drench South
Asia, West Africa, and northern Australia — are the
Hadley cell breathing in and out; their regional portrait
waits at L11.

The slow change is warming. Hotter air holds more vapor, so
the same storms drop harder downpours; warmer oceans feed
stronger tropical cyclones; the ice that steadied the polar
jet shrinks each decade (see `surface.md` and
`atmosphere.md`). The circulation keeps its shape while its
extremes grow teeth.

Sources: NOAA Climate.gov jet-stream pages; NOAA JetStream
climate pages; NASA Earth Observatory pages.

## How it was created

Sunlight and spin made the winds before there was anyone to
feel them. Uneven heating drives rising and sinking;
rotation splits the flow into three cells and four jets;
water closes the loop — evaporating from the oceans of
`surface.md`, condensing into clouds, falling as rain, and
returning to the sea. The engine needs only three parts: a
heat difference, a spin, and something to condense.

The details are younger. The Himalayas bent the Asian
monsoon into shape as they rose; the closing of Panama
redirected ocean currents and sharpened Atlantic storm
tracks; ice ages swung the desert belts and storm paths
hundreds of kilometers with each freeze and thaw. The
timeline in `lifetime.md` carries the dates; this page
carries the pattern.

## How it interacts with the others

The blanket sets the ceiling (see `atmosphere.md`). Every
tower stops at the tropopause; the ozone layer above steers
stratospheric winds that nudge storms below through the
quasi-biennial oscillation; aurorae light the thermosphere
far above the highest cloud.

The face feeds and is carved by the sky (see `surface.md`).
Oceans evaporate the moisture every cloud is made of and
take rain back; mountains force air upward into rain on one
side and desert shadow on the other; ice reflects sunlight
and anchors the polar highs; forests breathe vapor back and
dust fertilizes them across oceans.

The Moon steadies the rhythm (see `moons.md`). By holding
Earth's tilt stable it keeps the seasons — and therefore the
monsoons and storm tracks — regular over thousands of
years. Its tug on the thin air itself is tiny; weather
barely notices the tides.

The engine below is felt only at second hand (see
`interior.md` and `tectonics.md`). Volcanoes seed clouds
with ash and cool the planet after great blasts, but no
mantle heat reaches the winds directly — sunlight owns the
sky, inside heat owns the ground.

Sources: NOAA JetStream pages; NASA Earth facts page; NASA
Moon facts page.

## Key numbers

- Cells: three per hemisphere (Hadley, Ferrel, polar);
belts: trades toward the equator, westerlies at 35–60
degrees, polar easterlies.
- Jets: four (two polar, two subtropical), near 9 km up,
~110 mph average, 250+ mph in winter polar jets,
west-to-east.
- Storm sizes: thunderstorms 10–30 km; tropical cyclones
500–1,000 km; mid-latitude lows 1,000–3,000 km.
- Jupiter: equatorial winds to 539 km/h; jets 3,200 km deep;
Great Red Spot ~10,250 miles across in 2014 (from 25,500 in
the 1800s), ~500 km deep, shrinking ~580 miles per year.
- Spin behind it all: Earth day 23.9 hours; Jupiter day 10
hours; tilt 23.4 degrees.
- Anchor world: Earth 12,756 km across; all weather in a
troposphere averaging 12 km — a skin a thousand times
thinner than the world is wide.

Sources: NOAA JetStream global-circulation and jet-stream
pages; NOAA NESDIS jet-stream pages; NASA Jupiter facts;
NASA Great Red Spot pages; NASA Earth facts.

## Example objects

Earth is the anchor: three cells, four jets, tower anvils
along the equator, spiral cyclones in late summer,
comma-lows marching through the middle latitudes — the
living standard.

Jupiter is the banded extreme. Ten-hour spin, no surface,
stripes forever: belts and zones in opposite flows, the
Great Red Spot wider than Earth and centuries old, polar
cyclone rings that never wander off.

Saturn is the pale extreme. The same banded plan in muted
gold, a hexagon standing over the north pole, great white
storms erupting once a generation and wrapping the globe.

Venus is the veiled extreme. Slightly smaller than Earth
under permanent sulfuric-acid cloud, its cloud tops race
around the planet far faster than the ground below turns —
one unbroken blanket, no breaks, no blue.

Mars is the dusty extreme. Thin carbon-dioxide air, pink
dust skies, and storms that can grow from a canyon streak
into a planet-swallowing veil for months.

Titan is the methane extreme. Nitrogen air with methane
playing water's role: methane clouds, methane rain, methane
rivers into polar lakes under orange smog (see
`atmosphere.md`).

Sources: NASA Jupiter, Saturn, Venus, Mars, and Titan facts
pages; NASA Juno pages.
