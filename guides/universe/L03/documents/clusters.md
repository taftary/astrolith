# Clusters — cities of galaxies

Rich clusters are the great cities
of L3: hundreds to thousands of galaxies bound in a ball a few million
light-years across, glowing in X-rays, bending the light of everything
behind them. Sibling docs: `groups.md`, `icm.md`, `members.md`; bridge in
`previous-l2.md`, entry in `entry.md`, timeline in `lifetime.md`.

## What they are and how they look

In optical light a rich cluster reads as a swarm of yellowish ellipticals
crowding the center with blue spirals thinning toward the edges — the
morphology-density relation (Dressler 1980, 55 clusters, 6,000+ galaxies):
elliptical and S0 fractions rise steeply with density while spirals fall.
The rule of thumb: most galaxies inside clusters are ellipticals, most
outside are spirals. The relation is already in place at z~0.8 for
massive galaxies and builds over the ~7 Gyr before that as the
elliptical fraction in the densest regions rises. Sources for this
paragraph: morphology-density evolution (Dressler et al. 1997;
Holden et al. 2007; DEVILS 2025, arXiv 2508.10285). The swarm sits in a dark-matter halo holding ~85–90% of
the mass, with hot intracluster gas (~5–15%) outweighing all the stars
(~1–2%) several times over; typical total masses run 10^14–10^15 solar
masses across 1–5 Mpc (3–16 million light-years).

Catalog identity: rich clusters carry Abell numbers — Coma is Abell
1656, the lensing giant below is Abell 1689. George Abell's 1958
northern survey listed 2,712 rich clusters, extended to 4,073 across
the whole sky in the Abell–Corwin–Olowin catalog (1989); each holds
at least 30 members in the magnitude range m3 to m3+2. Abell richness
groups run 0–5 by member count (Coma is richness 2; Abell 1689 is
richness 4), and the Bautz–Morgan form classes run I (central
supergiant cD galaxy) through II (intermediate, like Coma) to III (no
dominant member, like Virgo) — shape as a readout of how relaxed the
system is. Sources: Abell, Corwin & Olowin 1989 (ApJS 70:1, NASA
HEASARC ABELL database); Bautz & Morgan 1970 (ApJ 162:L149).

Sources: Wikipedia "Galaxy cluster"; Swinburne Cosmos
morphology-density relation; Dressler 1980 (NASA/IPAC database Level 5);
Max Planck MPE clusters-and-groups page.

## Example objects

- Coma Cluster (Abell 1656): the archetype — thousands of galaxies in a
  sphere 20+ million light-years across, ~320 million light-years away
  (99 Mpc), velocity dispersion ~1,000 km/s, binding mass ~7 x 10^14
  solar masses, ICM at 8–9 keV. Its core is ruled by two supergiant
  ellipticals, NGC 4874 and NGC 4889, with ~22,000 globular clusters and
  a bridge between the pair. Sources: Wikipedia "Coma Cluster";
  ESA/Hubble heic0813a and potw1849a.
- Virgo Cluster: home — 1,300–2,000 members centered on M87, 54 million
  light-years away, still assembling now (infalling N/S clouds, Virgo E,
  Coma I groups; the W-group is being tidally stripped in the
  outskirts). Sources: Wikipedia "Virgo Cluster"; A&A 2026
  (aa59334-26, Virgo W-group first infall).
- Bullet Cluster (1E 0657-558): the merger caught in the act, 3.7
  billion light-years away (z=0.296, 1.14 Gpc comoving) — optical galaxies decoupled from
  the red X-ray plasma by ram pressure while the blue lensing mass map
  follows the galaxies, an 8-sigma offset and direct proof that nearly
  all the mass is dark matter. The two galaxy concentrations sit 0.72
  Mpc apart on the sky: a cooler ~6 keV western subcluster that has
  just punched through a hotter ~14 keV eastern main cluster, each
  plasma cloud lagging its galaxies toward the system's center of mass.
  Source: Clowe et al. 2006 (ApJ);
  NASA APOD 2006-08-24.
- El Gordo (ACT-CL J0102-4915): the distant heavyweight at z=0.87 (light
  traveled 7 billion years) — several hundred galaxies, ~2.1 million billion
  solar masses (~2,000 Milky Ways, mostly dark matter; early
  estimates ran to ~3 x 10^15 before the 2021 wide-field HST
  re-analysis), two
  subclusters colliding at several million km/h, the hottest and most
  X-ray-luminous cluster known at that epoch, with Bullet-like
  gas–dark-matter separation. Discovery data (Menanteau et al. 2012):
  89 member redshifts give z=0.870 with velocity dispersion
  1,321 ± 106 km/s; Chandra finds integrated gas temperature
  14.5 ± 1.0 keV swinging from a 6.6 keV cool core to 22 keV shocked
  gas, and 0.5–2 keV luminosity 2.19 x 10^45 erg/s. Archival 843 MHz
  radio shows a double relic — merger shocks lighting up at the
  highest redshift then seen. JWST PEARLS imaging (2022) confirms 23
  known plus dozens of new multiply-lensed background families and a
  double-peaked dark-matter map, keeping El Gordo the most massive
  cluster known beyond z=0.8 — massive enough that some analyses argue
  it strains vanilla structure-formation expectations (an open
  cosmological question, not a settled verdict). Sources: NASA Hubble El Gordo asset page;
  ESO eso1203; Chandra press release 2012-01-10; Menanteau et al. 2012
  (ApJ 748:7, arXiv 1109.0953); Diego et al. 2022 (JWST PEARLS lens
  model, arXiv 2210.06514).
- Typical size: 1–5 Mpc across; masses 10^14–10^15 solar masses; galaxy
  velocity dispersions of order 1,000 km/s; X-ray luminosities 10^44
  erg/s and up at 10^7–10^8 K. Source: Wikipedia "Galaxy cluster";
  Allen/Sarazin review (NASA/IPAC database Level 5).

## How they were created

Clusters assemble hierarchically and only recently — they are the
youngest, still-forming level of the cosmic web. Small groups form first,
then merge and collapse into larger virialized systems along the
filaments, dark matter leading and baryons falling into its wells. Matter
flows voids to sheets to filaments to the dense clumps at filament
intersections, where galaxies form in virialized halos tracing that web.
A caught-in-the-act example: a 13-million-light-year filament streaming
into MACS J0717 fed a four-cluster pile-up, mapped by Hubble in 2-D plus
redshift distances. Simulations count up to ~45% of cluster galaxies
arriving via filaments (the rest as group members, backsplash, or
filament-processed). Sources: Princeton galaxy-cluster notes (Burrows);
A&A 2026 (aa59334-26); NASA Hubble cosmic-web mapping highlight;
arXiv 2111.11467 (filament infall fractions).

## How they change over time

Relaxed clusters show a bright X-ray cool core and regular shape, with a
central iron peak inside 0.2 R500; disturbed young ones show low surface
brightness, ragged shapes, and radio halos/relics — mergers homogenize
the metals. The central giant keeps growing: stellar mass builds by major
mergers down to redshift ~1, then mainly by minor mergers and dynamical
friction dragging satellites in (the cannibalism behind Abell 3827's
30-trillion-solar-mass behemoth). In the far future, bound clusters keep
growing significantly to the present day as the last hierarchical stage,
roughly doubling before freeze-out, then evolve as island universes as
accelerating expansion carries everything else beyond the horizon (~100
Gyr scale). Sources: arXiv 2606.17700 (relaxed vs disturbed);
A&A 2025 (aa51801-24, iron peak); RASPUTIN 2014 (BCG mass growth);
arXiv 2605.30595 (growth to present day, freeze-out).

## How it interacts with the others

Clusters are the nodes of the web made concrete: they form at filament
intersections and grow by accreting groups and field systems along the
filaments, turning infall kinetic energy into gas heat. What falls in
gets weathered — Norma-cluster spiral ESO 137-001 plows at ~4.5 million
mph through 180-million-degree gas, ram pressure stripping its gas into
star-forming jellyfish tendrils while the disk starves. And clusters
return the favor to the background universe: Abell 1689's gravity acts as
a 2-million-light-year-wide lens, bending background galaxies (some 13+
billion light-years out, z~6) into arcs — 10x more arcs than
ground-based telescopes resolve. Abell 1689 (richness class 4,
Bautz–Morgan II-III, z=0.1832) also hosts 160,000+ globular clusters,
the largest such population ever found, while its visible galaxies
supply only ~1% of the lensing mass, the rest dark matter mapped by the
arcs themselves. The same lens revealed A1689-zD1, at discovery among
the most distant galaxies known. Sources: arXiv 1906.07584 (node accretion); NASA
ESO 137-001 feature; NASA Hubble Abell 1689 lens asset;
NASA APOD 2013-09-17 (mass budget); Wikipedia "Abell 1689".

## How they are weighed

Three independent scales weigh a cluster, and they agree only because
dark matter is real. Gravitational lensing maps total mass directly
(Abell 1689's visible galaxies supply ~1% of its lensing mass; the
Bullet's blue lensing map sits 8 sigma off the pink gas). X-ray
temperature/density profiles give hydrostatic masses (Coma's 8–9 keV gas
demands ~7 x 10^14 solar masses). The SZ shadow counts clusters across
cosmic time independent of distance (Planck PSZ2: 1,653 detections,
1,203 confirmed — raised to 1,334 validated with fresh redshifts for
399 systems in the 2024 UPCluster-SZ compilation; ACT DR6 SZ catalog,
ACT-DES-HSC Collaboration 2026, now public on LAMBDA). When all
three point the same way — as in El Gordo, weighed by lensing plus
X-ray plus SZ at ~2.1 x 10^15 solar masses (early estimates ~3 x 10^15) — the mass is trustworthy.
Sources: NASA APOD 2013-09-17; Clowe et al. 2006; Wikipedia
"Coma Cluster"; HEASARC Planck PSZ2.

## How they are counted (the 2020s catalog era)

Cluster science now runs on surveys, not single objects. Planck's
PSZ2 (2015: 1,653 SZ detections, 1,203 confirmed — the first SZ sample
past a thousand) remains the deepest all-sky SZ list; the 2024
UPCluster-SZ refresh validated 139 more systems (1,334 total) and
updated redshifts for 399 using spectroscopic literature. ACT's DR6 SZ
catalog (ACT-DES-HSC Collaboration 2026, maps 2008–2022 over ~19,000
square degrees) extends the SZ census with optically confirmed
redshifts plus legacy cosmology and multiple-system subsamples,
public on LAMBDA. From X-rays, eROSITA's first all-sky survey
(eRASS1, data release January 2024: ~930,000 X-ray sources in six
months, more than the previous 60 years combined) yields 12,247
optically confirmed groups and clusters spanning z=0.003–1.32, with a
5,259-system cosmology subsample and 1,338 supercluster systems —
the X-ray counterpart the SZ catalogs cross-match against (ACT DR6
ships eRASS1 cross-match flags). Sources: Planck Collaboration 2015
(A&A 594:A27); UPCluster-SZ 2024 (arXiv 2403.03818); ACT DR6 SZ
catalog 2026 (Open J. Astrophys. 9:155863; LAMBDA DR6 SZ page);
eROSITA DR1 (Merloni et al. 2024, A&A 682:A34; Bulbul et al. 2024,
A&A 685:A106; Liu et al. 2024, A&A 683:A130).

## Key numbers

- 1 Mpc = 3.26 million light-years = 3.09 x 10^22 m.
- Typical cluster: 1–5 Mpc across, 10^14–10^15 solar masses.
- Mass split (convention, varies per cluster): ~85–90% dark matter, ~5–15% hot gas, ~1–2% stars.
- Coma: 20+ Mly across, ~320 Mly away, dispersion 1,000 km/s, ~7 x 10^14 solar masses.
- Bullet: z=0.296, 3.7 Gly; El Gordo: z=0.87, 7 Gly light-travel, ~2.1 x 10^15 solar masses (early estimates ~3 x 10^15).
- Abell catalog: 2,712 (1958) to 4,073 all-sky (1989); richness 0–5, Bautz–Morgan I–III.
- Survey counts: PSZ2 1,653/1,203 (UPCluster-SZ 1,334); eRASS1 12,247 groups/clusters, z=0.003–1.32.
- Relaxed iron peak: inside 0.2 R500; mergers erase it.

Sources: pages named above; 1 Mpc conversions standard.
