# Brown dwarfs and white dwarfs — the faint endpoints

Loop doc for Issue #223 (Round 2 loop, iteration 3). The failed
stars and the stellar embers of the neighborhood: what they look
like, how they change through time, how they are created, and how
they interact with the others. Home examples: Luhman 16 (nearest
brown-dwarf pair) and Sirius B (the weighed white dwarf).

## How it looks

Brown dwarfs give off almost no visible light: their birth glow
fades past red, and they shine only in the infrared, cooling through
the spectral sequence M (hotter than 2,000 K) into L (water and metal
compounds in the air), T (methane), and Y (the coldest). They can be
striped like Jupiter — Luhman 16B wears cloud bands with bright and
dark patches shifting on global winds. White dwarfs are the opposite
kind of faint: tiny blue-white embers, Earth-sized, ten thousand
times dimmer than Sirius A and usually drowned in a companion's
glare, slowly reddening as they cool. Sirius B is the archetype: a
12,000 km cinder holding nearly the Sun's mass.

## How it changes over time

Brown dwarfs never settle: with no sustained hydrogen fusion they
only contract and cool, young ones briefly rivaling red dwarfs in
brightness before sliding down L into T into Y as their clouds clear
to blue skies. White dwarfs only fade: radiating leftover heat with
no new source, a 0.59-solar-mass ember takes 1.5 billion years to
reach 7,140 K and cools ever slower after — so slowly that no black
dwarf (below ~3,050 K) can exist yet; the universe is too young.
Sirius B's cooling age runs ~126 million years: its ~5-solar-mass
parent star lived fast and died ~100 million years ago while Sirius
A still shines. Second pass: Webb's infrared spectra now dissect
the coldest Y dwarfs layer by layer — water, methane and ammonia
bands marking each cooling step — confirming from orbit the
cloud-clearing sequence the weather maps found from the ground.

Sources: Chandra stellar-story pages (brown-dwarf cooling);
Montreal brown-dwarf cloud-clearing work; white-dwarf cooling data;
Sirius B age data; ESA Webb brown-dwarf spectra.

## How it is created

Brown dwarfs are failed stars: gas clumps that collapse like stars
but stall below ~80 Jupiter masses, their cores never reaching the
~3 million K needed for hydrogen fusion (a few may instead condense
out of disks like planets). White dwarfs are stellar remains: stars
under ~8–10 solar masses swell into red giants, shed their envelopes
as planetary nebulae over tens of thousands of years, and their
carbon-oxygen cores collapse to Earth size (see `lifetime.md` for
the dated stages).

Sources: NASA stellar-types page; Chandra formation pages; ESA Webb
formation release.

## How it interacts with the others

At neighborhood scale both are lightweights with outsized gravity
tricks. Brown dwarfs drift mostly alone or in pairs (Luhman 16AB
circles every 27.5 years at 3.56 AU); their heat and drag are
negligible. White dwarfs shred what wanders too close: asteroids and
comets torn apart settle into dusty rings, and their metals pollute
the ember's spectrum (van Maanen 2 shows the scars). Both set the
faint end of every census (see `stars.md`) and both keep planets or
debris from their former lives (see `systems.md`).

Sources: Luhman 16 orbit data; NASA stellar-types page (white-dwarf
debris rings); van Maanen 2 data.

## Size and mass

Brown dwarfs: ~13–80 Jupiter masses (a Webb record-holder reaches
3–4), about one Jupiter radius regardless of mass, surface
temperatures from ~2,000 K (L) down to ~250 K (Y). White dwarfs:
~0.6 solar masses typical in an Earth-sized ball (0.008–0.01 solar
radii) — and more massive means smaller. The examples span both
families.

## Example objects

- Luhman 16AB — L+T brown-dwarf binary, ~33+28 Jupiter masses,
  6.51 light-years away. The nearest failed stars, with mapped
  weather.
- WISE 0855-0714 — Y4 brown dwarf, ~5 Jupiter masses, ~7.4
  light-years away, coldest known at ~250–285 K (near freezing).
  Colder than some planets.
- Sirius B — white dwarf, ~0.98–1.02 solar masses in ~12,000 km at
  ~25,000 K, 8.6 light-years away. The ember that weighed general
  relativity.
- van Maanen 2 — solitary white dwarf, ~0.68 solar masses, 14.1
  light-years away. The closest ember on its own.

Sources: Luhman 16 data (ESO release); WISE 0855 data (NASA/JPL);
ESA Sirius pages; van Maanen 2 data.
