# Binary and multiple systems — suns in orbits

Loop doc for Issue #223 (Round 2 loop, iteration 2). The bound pairs
and triples of the stellar neighborhood: what they look like, how
they change through time, how they are created, and how they
interact with the others. Home example: the Alpha Centauri triple.

## How it looks

Resolved multiples show contrasting colors side by side: Alpha
Centauri A glows yellow-white (G2V, ~5,748 K, 1.51 solar
luminosities), B shines orange (K1V, ~5,154 K, half the Sun's
light), and Proxima smolders faint red (M5.5Ve, a sixth of a percent
of the Sun's light) — all three in one ESO wide field, the bright
pair flanked by a faint red spark. The Sirius showcase is starker:
brilliant Sirius A overexposed beside a tiny dot — Sirius B, ten
thousand times fainter, a white-dwarf ember in the glare
(diffraction spikes and rings are telescope artifacts). Most
neighbors are single: only about 27% of systems within 10 parsecs
are multiple (85 of 317 in the RECONS census — 66 doubles, 14
triples, a few quadruples and quintuples).

## How it changes over time

Wide pairs age much like single stars: Alpha Centauri A and B circle
every 79.8 years on a stretched orbit (~23 AU apart), each burning
steadily, while Proxima loops the pair on a ~550,000-year circuit.
Close pairs live harder — gas spills from star to star through the
Roche lobe, orbits shrink or widen, envelopes strip, and mergers or
white-dwarf pairs can end as Type Ia supernovae, the candles that
measure the universe. Second pass: the spilled gas does not vanish —
it lights novae on white-dwarf surfaces, spins companions up to
breakneck rotation, and in the tightest pairs gravitational waves
carry the merger away within cosmic time. Sirius shows the role
reversal: B was born heavier (~5 solar masses), died first ~126
million years ago, and now packs ~0.98 Suns into 12,000 km at
~25,200 K while A still shines on the main sequence.

Sources: Alpha Centauri orbit data (79.76-year period); NASA Sirius
companion page; compact-binary review (mass transfer, mergers).

## How it is created

Multiples are born the way singles are — collapsing cloud knots —
plus fragmentation: a turbulent core splits into two or more
collapsing centers, or the disk around a young star breaks up into a
companion. A slightly spinning cloud flattens into a disk that
fragments, so pairs and triples arrive together from the same knot,
which is why companions usually share an age and a motion through
the neighborhood (see `lifetime.md`).

Sources: NRAO binary-formation gallery (core and disk
fragmentation); NASA Imagine stellar-objects page.

## How it interacts with the others

Mutual gravity binds every orbit, from days-long close pairs to
Proxima's half-million-year loop. In close systems the interaction
turns physical: transferred gas spins companions up, stripped
envelopes feed disks, novae flash, and gravitational waves carry
mergers away. Each member still blows its own wind into the shared
surroundings, carving the local bubble together (see `ism.md`), and
their combined light sets the climate of any planets caught between
them (see `systems.md`). Sirius B's mass, weighed through its
gravitational redshift, doubles as a test of general relativity.

Sources: Proxima wide-orbit data; NASA heliosphere resource page;
NASA Sirius companion page (redshift weighing).

## Size and mass

Separations run from fractions of an AU to ~13,000 AU (Proxima's
distance from the AB pair); periods from days to hundreds of
thousands of years; mass ratios roughly 0.1 to 1. The examples below
span the range.

## Example objects

- Alpha Centauri triple — A 1.079 solar masses (G2V) + B 0.909
  (K1V) + Proxima 0.122 (M5.5Ve), 4.37 light-years away (Proxima
  4.246). The nearest system and our home portal.
- Sirius A+B — A1V ~2 solar masses + white dwarf ~0.98, 50-year
  orbit, 8.6 light-years away. The weighed ember beside the
  brightest star.
- Luhman 16AB — brown-dwarf binary (L+T types, ~33+28 Jupiter
  masses), 27.5-year orbit, 6.5 light-years away. Pairs come small
  too (see `dwarfs.md`).

Sources: Alpha Centauri and Proxima data pages; NASA Sirius
companion page; Luhman 16 data (ESO release).
