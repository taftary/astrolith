# Planetary systems — what the nearby suns hold

Loop doc for Issue #223 (Round 2 loop, iteration 5). The planets and
debris disks around neighborhood stars: what they look like, how
they change through time, how they are created, and how they
interact with the others. Home preview: our own system, one zoom
deeper at L7.

## How it looks

The catalog holds 6,000+ confirmed exoplanets and thousands more
candidates — and the nearby sample is dominated by small worlds on
tight orbits: super-Earths (rocky, larger than Earth, smaller than
Neptune, up to ~10 Earth masses) circling red dwarfs in days or
weeks at hundredths of an AU, where Mercury needs 88 days at 0.39
AU. Red dwarfs, three quarters of all stars, burn their full
hydrogen supply through convection and hold their planets close and
long. Where formation left leftovers, debris disks glow: diffuse
rock-and-dust belts, first found in infrared, like the inner belts
and outer Kuiper-like ring around Epsilon Eridani.

## How it changes over time

Close-in worlds weather hard: Proxima b takes hundreds of times
Earth's ultraviolet thousands, stripping hydrogen then oxygen and
nitrogen across the aeons. Disks clear young — protoplanetary gas
dissipates within 3–10 million years, accretion stopping around 2.3
million — while old white dwarfs often keep dusty debris rings and
planets from their red-giant days. Passages reshape the outskirts:
in ~1.3 million years Gliese 710 will thread the Oort cloud and
shake comets and dust loose (see `lifetime.md`).

Sources: Proxima b modeling (ultraviolet thousands); CfA T-Tauri
disk work; NASA stellar-types page (white-dwarf rings); Gliese 710
passage data.

## How it is created

Planets arrive with their stars: a dusty clump collapses, the core
reaches millions of degrees, hydrogen fusion starts — nine in ten
stars sit on this main sequence — and the leftover disk flattens,
clumps, and builds worlds within a few million years. Epsilon
Eridani (~800 million years old, 0.83 Suns) is the nearby laboratory
for Sun-like formation, Jupiter-mass world plus belts included. The
young T-Tauri phase (under ~10 million years) clears the envelope
while the disk still feeds the star; about half of such stars still
show disks.

Sources: NASA stellar-types page; CfA T-Tauri work; Epsilon Eridani
stellar data.

## How it interacts with the others

Systems never sit alone. Proxima b's sky holds Alpha Centauri A and
B — its bound triple companions — and Proxima's flares rake the
planet's air. Tau Ceti's massive debris disk (6–52 AU) likely rains
impacts onto its candidate worlds. Passing stars stir the outer
belts (see `lifetime.md`), stellar winds sculpt every bubble (see
`ism.md`), and dead stars keep their rubble: white-dwarf rings from
the red-giant phase. Our own system's eight planets, belts, and
Oort shells are the fullest example — resolved one level down, at
L7/L8.

Sources: ESO Proxima b release (triple sky, flares); Keck Tau Ceti
work (debris bombardment); NASA stellar-types page.

## Size and mass

Nearby compact systems pack sub-Earth to super-Earth worlds (fractions
to ~10 Earth masses) into 0.01–0.06 AU orbits of a few days; Jovian
companions (~1.5 Jupiter masses) circle farther out over years;
debris belts span single to tens of AU. The examples below anchor
the range.

## Example objects

- Proxima b — super-Earth, ~1.06 Earth masses, 11.2-day orbit at
  0.048 AU in its star's temperate zone, 4.2 light-years away. The
  nearest known exoplanet, under a flare star in a triple sky.
- Barnard's star planets — FOUR sub-Earth worlds (b, c, d, e at
  0.19–0.34 Earth masses) on few-day orbits, 6 light-years away,
  all too hot despite sitting near their star. Small star, small
  system, confirmed across 2024–2025 campaigns.
- Epsilon Eridani — K2V star, 10.5 light-years away, ~800 million
  years old, with the closest known debris disk (inner belts + outer
  ring) and a Jupiter-like candidate (~1.55 Jupiter masses, 6.85
  years). Formation caught in the act.
- Tau Ceti — solitary G-type star just under 12 light-years away
  with four Earth-size candidates (two near its temperate zone) and
  a massive debris disk. Candidate worlds under bombardment.
- TRAPPIST-1 (contrast, out of volume) — ultra-cool dwarf at 40.7
  light-years with seven rocky Earth-size planets in 1.5–19-day
  orbits. What compact systems look like taken to the extreme.

Sources: NASA Proxima b catalog; NASA Barnard's-planets discovery
alert; Arizona Epsilon Eridani work; Keck Tau Ceti work; NASA
TRAPPIST-1 page.
