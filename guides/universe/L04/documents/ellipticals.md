# Ellipticals — smooth round giants

Elliptical galaxies are smooth,
nearly featureless balls and eggs of old stars: no arms, little gas,
quiet today. They form one arm of the Hubble tuning fork, graded E0
(nearly round) to E7 (very stretched), alongside spirals and the
lenticular S0 bridge between them. The biggest sit at the centers of
clusters, grown fat by eating their neighbors. How an elliptical
interacts with the other L4 parts — spirals it is built from, dwarfs it
swallows, nuclei it hosts, halos it sheds into the cluster — runs
through every section below.

This document is the L04 elliptical reference: what they look like,
how they age, how both formation roads run, how they connect to the
other parts, how astronomers weigh them, and which example objects
anchor each claim. For disks and arms see [spirals](spirals.md); for
the small faint majority see [dwarfs](dwarfs.md); for central black
holes see [nuclei](nuclei.md); for stripped stars and streams see
[halos](halos.md); for the dated timeline see
[lifetime](lifetime.md).

## How it looks

A smooth ellipsoid glow, brightest at the center and fading outward,
with no disk, no arms, no dust lanes. The Hubble tuning fork grades
them E0 (nearly round) to E7 (very stretched) by the isophote axis
ratio, with type number 10 × (1 − b/a): a perfectly round image is E0,
an image one-third as thick as it is wide is about E7. The grade mixes
true shape with viewing angle, so a single number never gives the 3D
body — an E0 on the sky can still be a flattened system seen face-on.

In three dimensions ellipticals are triaxial ellipsoids held up by
random stellar motions, not by spin: unlike spiral disks, their stars
swarm on tilted, scrambled orbits, so the body is genuinely puffy
rather than a thin wheel seen edge-on. Their outline details carry
history. Some have slightly pointed, lemon-like ("disky") isophotes
that betray a buried disk; others have squared-off ("boxy") contours
built by unequal random motions along different axes. Since 1966 work
confirmed by stellar-rotation spectra and the ATLAS3D census, the
flattest grades E4–E7 are mostly tilted lens-shaped disks (S0 and the
intermediate ES scale) in disguise rather than true footballs, so the
cleanest elliptical sample sits at E0–E3 with the flat tail treated as
a disk-contaminated extension.

Their light is warm and yellow-red: old low-mass stars, almost no young
blue ones, few gas clouds. Large systems carry rich globular-cluster
retinues in two color families — redder metal-rich clusters born with
the galaxy and bluer metal-poor ones accreted from swallowed dwarfs —
and M87 holds about 15,000 of them against the Milky Way's 150–200.
The giant M87 spans about 132,000 light-years at Virgo's
center with a small blue jet flicking out of its core: the full-galaxy
view below, not the jet close-up (that framing belongs to L03
`../../L03/documents/members.md`). Its de Vaucouleurs E0p tag records
exactly this combination: round shape plus a peculiar jet.

## How it changes over time

Ellipticals are the burned-out end state: they finished most star birth
early. Hubble, Spitzer, and Herschel together traced the sequence —
dust-choked submillimeter starbursts burning thousands of times faster
than the Milky Way for only ~40 million years, then compact
quiescent balls already dead by age 3 Gyr, running 10–100 times
denser than today's giants — and reddened as their stars aged. Only
brief merger bursts relight them; about a quarter keep a little
residual gas and low star birth (ATLAS3D molecular-gas census), with
the central hole's beating feedback keeping most of that gas too hot
to collapse. The biggest keep growing without new
stars at all: dry minor mergers puff them up from compact seeds into
today's giants, and the stars they strip
from victims drift off to become intracluster light glowing between the
cluster's galaxies — up to several million light-years across around a
cD, contributing a few percent of the cluster's baryons.

Massive cluster ellipticals run older, redder, and more metal-rich
than field ones of the same mass, and nearby samples show the most
massive systems are typically the oldest: downsizing written in star
light.

Sources: NASA "burned-out galaxies" (early quenching, Toft et al.
sequence); Wikipedia "Elliptical galaxy" (red colors, minor-merger
puffing, residual gas); A&A brightest cluster galaxy sample (growth
history); Wikipedia "Type-cD galaxy" (intracluster envelope).

## How it is created

Two roads lead here, and both run. Some ellipticals condensed fast in
the young universe, burning their gas in one early burst — the red
nuggets and blue nuggets caught by Hubble and JWST are that road
caught in the act. Most big ones
were assembled: disk galaxies collided, and when neither could escape,
their orbits scrambled into a ball — larger ellipticals swallowed more
progenitors (De Lucia et al. formation histories). Gas-rich (wet)
mergers spin up lower-mass, fast-rotating ellipticals; gas-poor (dry)
mergers stack mass into the slow-rotating giants without fresh star
birth. Fornax's NGC 1316 still shows the crime scene: dark dust
against a glowing nucleus, with faint clusters orphaned from a
companion destroyed 100 million years ago.

The brightest cluster giants (cD / central dominants, ~20% of brightest
cluster galaxies) grew latest and largest, by cannibalism plus cooling
flows plus dynamical friction dragging victims inward. The traffic
works like a wake: a big galaxy's passage piles smaller galaxies and
dark matter behind it, the wake's pull brakes it, and it spirals to
the cluster center where victim after victim joins — stars, gas, dust,
and dark matter all added, second-brightest cluster members left
under-luminous because they were eaten first. Merged nuclei can linger
for a long time as multiple cores inside one envelope, and stripped
stars that never settle become the intracluster envelope that belongs
as much to the cluster as to the galaxy.

Sources: Wikipedia "Elliptical galaxy" (both pictures, wet/dry split);
STScI galactic cannibalism release (NGC 1316); De Lucia formation
history; Wikipedia "Type-cD galaxy" (cannibalism, dynamical friction,
multiple nuclei, fossil-group link).

## How it interacts with the others

- **With spirals:** major mergers of spirals are the main factory (see
  [spirals](spirals.md)); the elliptical keeps the victims' globular clusters as
  trophies and sometimes a dust scar. M87's own halo chevron records a
  swallowed star-forming spiral from the last billion years, and its
  25%-level residual-gas cases are the wet-merger tail of the same
  process.
- **With dwarfs:** giants gobble dwarfs whole — tidal ripples and shell
  arcs around ellipticals are the half-digested remains (see
  [dwarfs](dwarfs.md)). M32 is the debated middle case: prototype
  compact elliptical (cE2) hugging Andromeda, half its stars inside a
  330-light-year core at extreme central density, with a faint outer
  disk, a 1.5–5-million-solar-mass hole (M32*), and two live origin
  stories — threshed spiral/lenticular stripped by M31's tides versus
  a surviving compact seed, possibly the remnant core of the lost
  M32p spiral that merged with Andromeda ~2 Gyr ago.
- **With nuclei:** nearly every big elliptical hosts a supermassive
  black hole; M87's 6.5-billion-solar-mass one (EHT 2019, polarized
  follow-up 2021, PRIMO-sharpened 2023) fires a jet at least ~4,900
  light-years long that heats the surrounding cluster gas, and its
  LINER nucleus records weak-line ionization from shocks plus jet
  photoionization (see
  [nuclei](nuclei.md)). IC 1101's tens-of-billions hole is the
  overmassive extreme of the same co-evolution.
- **With halos:** stripped stars escape into the intracluster light, so
  the brightest ellipticals literally dissolve at the edges into their
  cluster (see [halos](halos.md); L03 `../../L03/documents/icm.md`).
  Photometry alone cannot split cD envelope from intracluster stars;
  kinematics can. M87's envelope already reaches ~490,000 light-years
  with streams and a possible shredded-gas filament to the northeast.

Sources: NASA galaxy evolution page; Chandra M87 jet tracking; L03
`../../L03/documents/members.md`; Wikipedia "Messier 32" (cE2, core,
hole, threshed-vs-seed debate); Wikipedia "Messier 87" (chevron,
LINER, EHT timeline).

## Reading an elliptical (second pass)

Ellipticals are measured by glow and jitter. Their light follows a
steep profile — bright core fading outward roughly as the fourth root
of radius (the de Vaucouleurs law, one case of the Sersic family) — so
a single fit gives the effective radius holding half the light. In the
general Sersic form the concentration index n runs from about 1 for
disks and faint dwarfs up to 4 for classic ellipticals like M87 and
toward 10 for the most concentrated giants: bigger, brighter systems
have larger n. The brightest cluster giants need one more piece — a
flattened, low-density core (the core-Sersic model) carved when merging
black holes flung stars outward — and faint dwarfs often add a compact
central nucleus on top of the main profile.

Their stars' random speeds give the mass: hotter jitter means deeper
gravity. Ellipticals sit on a tight fundamental plane linking
effective radius, surface brightness, and velocity spread, with the
luminosity scaling roughly as the fourth power of spread (the
Faber-Jackson relation). And the two measures meet at the center: the
black hole's mass tracks the bulge's velocity spread (the M–sigma
relation, M scaling as sigma to roughly the fourth to fifth power),
which is how astronomers weighed holes like M87* before any shadow was
imaged. The mean hole-to-bulge ratio is now about 1:200 in massive
early types, falling lower in smaller galaxies; active nuclei scatter
more around the quiescent relation and need separate calibration.

Sources: Wikipedia "Sersic profile" (n ladder, de Vaucouleurs n = 4,
core-Sersic, nuclei); Wikipedia "M–sigma relation" (slope, feedback,
1:200 ratio, AGN scatter); Wikipedia "Fundamental plane".

## Dwarfs vs giants: one ladder, two regimes

Dwarf and giant ellipticals sit on one Sersic-index-vs-luminosity
sequence (Graham–Guzman Coma work: no structural dichotomy), but the
ends behave differently, so this page and [dwarfs](dwarfs.md) split the
ladder deliberately. Ordinary ellipticals (blue absolute magnitudes
brighter than about −18) follow de Vaucouleurs-like n ≈ 4 profiles,
sit on the fundamental plane, and hold boxy/disky isophote families
tied to anisotropic versus rotating builds. Dwarf ellipticals (−18 to
−14) slide toward exponential n ≈ 1 profiles, frequently carry a
compact nucleus, rotate coherently in isolated cases like CG 611
(LEDA 2108986) — whose counter-rotating gas disk proves disks can grow
by accretion without any cluster — and leave the fundamental plane
defined by the giants. Still fainter, the dwarf spheroidals are treated
as a genuinely distinct class closer to irregulars and late spirals
than to M87.

Two origins therefore overlap at the faint end. Some dwarfs are
primordial building blocks from hierarchical merging; others are
harassed spirals reshaped by repeated cluster encounters and
ram-pressure stripping of their gas — but harassment cannot be the whole
story, because isolated rotating dwarfs like CG 611 exist far from any
cluster. Compact ellipticals (M32 the prototype) are the rare third
case: dense, high-surface-brightness, and easily confused with neither
branch — do not file M32 as an ordinary dwarf elliptical.

Sources: Wikipedia "Dwarf elliptical galaxy" (magnitude cuts, Sersic
continuity, harassment vs CG 611); Graham–Guzman 2003; Janz et al.
2017; Graham et al. 2017 (CG 611 counter-rotation).

## Colors, spin, and address

On the galaxy color–magnitude diagram ellipticals own the red sequence:
massive, quenched, metal-rich systems reddened by old stars, set apart
from the star-forming blue cloud with a sparsely populated green valley
between them. Early-type quenching runs fast (merger plus active-nucleus
shutdown); late-type quenching runs slow (gas shutoff, then exhaustion
over billions of years). Recent dust corrections complicate the clean
split: massive dust-rich lenticulars shift into a "green mountain"
beside the classic dust-poor red sequence, so color alone never
classifies a galaxy — shape, spin, and gas content must agree.

Spin splits the red sequence itself. The ATLAS3D census replaced the
flat E-grade picture with fast versus slow rotators: lower-mass,
disky, gas-bearing systems that spin coherently against massive, boxy,
slowly tumbling giants built by dry mergers. Address follows the same
split (the morphology–density relation): ellipticals prefer cluster
centers and compact groups — about 10–15% of the Virgo Supercluster
population yet dominant at cluster cores — while field ellipticals are
rarer and more likely to retain gas and spin.

Sources: Wikipedia "Galaxy color–magnitude diagram" (red sequence,
green valley/mountain, Graham 2024); Wikipedia "Elliptical galaxy"
(cluster preference, Virgo fraction); ATLAS3D angular-momentum census.

## What is new (2020–2026)

- **Red nuggets as living fossils.** JWST plus local relic hunts
  confirm the two-phase picture: compact quiescent cores ("red
  nuggets" like NGC 1277, Mrk 1216) formed in a fierce central
  compaction-and-quench event — inflow plus disk instability, extreme
  star-formation and feedback, likely active-nucleus help — then grew
  inside-out by dry minor mergers into today's giants. About 85% of
  massive disk galaxies assembled inside-out around pre-existing
  compact cores; roughly a third of those cores are ancient relics
  from z ~ 6 that spent billions of years "naked" before acquiring
  disks. Chandra hot halos around Mrk 1216 and PGC 032673 show the
  hole's heat still suppressing star birth today.
- **M87* sharpened.** The 2019 first shadow (6.5 billion solar masses)
  gained a 2021 polarized-light view mapping magnetic fields that
  launch the jet, a 2021 multi-wavelength campaign tying horizon to
  kiloparsec jet scales, and a 2023 PRIMO re-analysis sharpening the
  ring for theory tests. Apparent few-parsec hole-vs-center offsets
  are jet-brightness flicker, not displacement (2018 high-resolution
  verdict over the 2010 claim).
- **Overmassive cluster holes.** IC 1101's depleted 4.2-kpc core and
  Holmberg 15A-class systems push core-break hole masses to tens of
  billions of solar masses — near cosmological bounds — while the
  M–sigma relation itself frays for active and jetted systems, which
  scatter wider and may follow offset tracks.
- **Sizes depend on method — say which.** IC 1101 reads
  ~400,000–550,000 light-years by standard isophotes but ~1.7 million
  light-years (520 kpc) at the 30 mag/arcsec^2 ultra-deep limit, with
  halo light past 2 million light-years; M87 reads 120,000 (heritage
  release) versus 132,000 (NED isophote) versus ~490,000 with envelope.
  Report the isophote with the number.
- **Dwarf census tension.** 2024 surveys find some volumes hold *too
  many* faint satellites for the simplest models while deep-wide work
  (Hyper Suprime-Cam) shows 30–50% of field dwarfs are quenched —
  internal feedback and holes matter alongside environment. See
  [dwarfs](dwarfs.md) for the count; it sets how many victims the
  giants had to eat.

Sources: Wikipedia "Red nugget" (compaction, quenching, relics,
inside-out growth); Wikipedia "Messier 87" (EHT timeline, offset
verdict); Wikipedia "IC 1101" (Dullo core, Kluge halo, size-method
spread); ESA/Hubble heic0815f (heritage M87 numbers as published).

## Size, mass

Ellipticals span the widest mass ladder of any galaxy type, from dwarf
systems of tens of millions of stars up to central supergiants past
100 trillion. Isophotal diameters run from a few thousand light-years
to several hundred thousand; the diffuse stellar halo of a brightest
cluster galaxy reaches further still, dissolving into intracluster
light that photometry alone cannot separate from the galaxy. Masses run
from about 100,000 solar masses of stars in the faintest dwarfs (inside
far heavier dark halos) to nearly a trillion solar masses in stars for
the largest cluster centrals, with total gravitating masses an order of
magnitude higher again. The center of the ladder is the classic giant:
tens to hundreds of billions of stars inside a 30–130,000-light-year
body with a mass-to-light ratio near 6, meaning only about one part in
six is shining stars and the rest is dark matter plus remnants.

Dwarf ellipticals (dE) sit at the small smooth end of this ladder, but
they are not simply scaled-down giants: their Sersic index drops toward
n = 1, many carry a compact nucleus, and the faintest spheroidals blur
into the dwarf family covered in [dwarfs](dwarfs.md). The M32 compact
elliptical bridges both pages — only ~6,500–8,000 light-years wide yet
dense,
with its own few-million-solar-mass hole. At the top end the cD
supergiants (D and cD classes) add an extended dustless envelope that
belongs as much to the cluster as to the galaxy; IC 1101 is the
textbook case.

## Example objects

- **M87** — giant elliptical (E0p / cD) near Virgo's center, ~53.5
  million light-years away (16.4 Mpc), isophotal diameter ~132,000
  light-years with a diffuse envelope reaching ~490,000 light-years,
  trillions of stars, ~15,000 globular clusters, and a 6.5-billion-
  solar-mass black hole (EHT 2019) firing a plasma jet at least ~4,900
  light-years long at relativistic speed. Slowly rotating, nearly
  spherical star swarm; absorbed a medium spiral within the last
  billion years, leaving a chevron shell in its halo. The L04 home
  bridge for ellipticals.
  Sources: Wikipedia "Messier 87" (distance, size, envelope, clusters,
  hole, jet, merger shell); NASA black-hole history page; NASA SVS M87.
- **IC 1101** — supergiant cD/S0 at Abell 2029's center, ~1.13 billion
  light-years out, isophotal diameter ~400,000–550,000 light-years with
  faint halo light traced past 2 million light-years in the deepest
  imaging. Largest known depleted core (~4.2 kpc radius, stellar
  deficit near 5 × 10^11 solar masses) and an overmassive central hole
  estimated at tens of billions of solar masses (core-break fits reach
  ~40–100 billion, with upper fits higher). Boxy outer isophotes,
  twisted halo, no dust lanes, no recent star birth: the cannibalism
  end state after ~10 or more mergers.
  Source: Wikipedia "IC 1101" (Dullo et al. 2017 core, Kluge et al.
  2020 halo, size-method dependence).
- **M32** — compact elliptical satellite of Andromeda, ~2.5 million
  light-years away, only ~6,500–8,000 light-years wide (bright core vs
  NED estimate) with a dense nucleus
  holding a few-million-solar-mass black hole. The dense extreme of
  the dwarf-elliptical line; bridges this page and [dwarfs](dwarfs.md).
  Sources: NASA Messier 32; SEDS M32.
- **NGC 1316 (Fornax A)** — merger-built elliptical with the crime
  scene still visible: dark dust against a glowing nucleus, faint
  orphan clusters from a companion destroyed ~100 million years ago.
  Source: STScI galactic-cannibalism release.
- **Maffei 1** — nearest giant elliptical (E3 boxy core-type), hidden
  behind the Milky Way's plane in the Zone of Avoidance (~4.7 mag
  extinction), ~9.3 million light-years away (2.85 Mpc), ~75,000
  light-years across. Boxy shape with an underluminous core, old
  metal-rich stars past 10 Gyr, a tiny 1.2-parsec blue nucleus with
  recent star birth but no active nucleus, and a large expected
  globular retinue (~1,100; ~20 confirmed centrally by Hubble against
  heavy foreground confusion). Would rank among the northern sky's
  brightest galaxies unobscured. Source: Wikipedia
  "Maffei 1" (Fingerhut, Buta, Davidge work).

## A note on disagreeing numbers

M87's published size and distance move with method and date: the 2008
heritage release (heic0815f) gives 54 million light-years,
120,000 light-years, 13,000 clusters; current NED-weighted work gives
53.5 million light-years (16.4 Mpc), 132,000 light-years isophotal
with a ~490,000-light-year envelope, ~15,000 clusters. Both are kept
above with the method named because the heritage numbers are what the
displayed image was released with. The same rule covers IC 1101:
standard isophotes versus ultra-deep 30 mag/arcsec^2 limits differ by
a factor of three — always read the isophote first.
