# Nuclei — galactic centers and black holes

Nearly every big galaxy hosts a
supermassive black hole at its center; when matter falls in, the nucleus
lights up and can outshine the whole galaxy. Quasars, jets, and quiet
dark hearts are one family seen at different dinners. How a nucleus
interacts with the other L4 parts — disks it feeds from and quenches,
ellipticals it anchors, halos it heats — runs through every section
below.

This document is the L04 nuclei reference: what galactic centers look
like, how active nuclei change over time, how black holes and nuclear
star clusters are built, how nuclei connect to the other parts, how
astronomers weigh the dark heart, and which example objects anchor
each claim. For disks and arms see [spirals](spirals.md); for smooth
merger-built giants see [ellipticals](ellipticals.md); for the small
faint majority see [dwarfs](dwarfs.md); for stripped stars and gas
halos see [halos](halos.md); for the dated timeline see
[lifetime](lifetime.md).

## How it looks

A bright compact core where the galaxy's light spikes. In quiet nuclei
like ours the spike is starlight crowding around a dark mass: the
Milky Way's nuclear star cluster packs tens of millions of Suns of
old and young stars into a few parsecs around Sagittarius A*. In
active nuclei the spike is the engine itself. The engine has three
nested pieces: a supermassive black hole, a hot accretion disk of
infalling gas rubbing itself incandescent, and (often) a dusty torus
plus a broad-line and a narrow-line gas region around them. What we
call the display depends mostly on viewing angle and jet power (see
"The AGN zoo" below).

The Event Horizon Telescope resolved the shadow itself. M87* is a
6.5-billion-solar-mass ring about 55 million light-years away,
imaged in 2019 at 42 microarcseconds across with a brighter southern
crescent, then our own Sagittarius A* — about 4.297 million Suns at
8,277 parsecs (about 27,000 light-years), a 51.8-microarcsecond
bright ring around darkness imaged in 2022 from 2017 data, flaring
daily in X-rays and hourly in the infrared. Polarized light then
mapped the magnetism: the March 2024 EHT view shows strong,
organized spiral magnetic fields around Sgr A* with peak linear
polarization near 40 percent in the western ring, strikingly similar
to M87*'s 2021 polarized ring — evidence that ordered fields are
common to black holes, favoring magnetically arrested disks that can
launch jets, and a hint of a hidden jet in our own center.

## The AGN zoo: one engine, many costumes (second pass)

Quasars, Seyferts, radio galaxies, and blazars are one family seen at
different angles and powers. The unified model says every active
nucleus has the same core parts — hole, disk, broad-line clouds deep
inside, dusty torus, narrow-line gas far outside — and appearance
follows orientation plus jet strength:

| Family | Power and host | What we see | Why it looks that way |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seyfert 1 | Nearby, modest; host spiral plainly visible | Bright blue disk plus broad (up to 10,000 km/s) and narrow lines | Face into the torus: we see the fast inner clouds directly |
| Seyfert 2 | Same engine, edge-on | Bright core but only narrow lines in direct light; broad lines return in polarized (mirrored) light | Torus hides disk and broad-line region; hot gas above the torus mirrors it |
| Quasar (radio-quiet and radio-loud) | 10 to 100,000 Milky Ways from a light-days-wide core; distant so the host drowns in glare | Star-like point, strong continuum plus broad and narrow lines, sometimes jets | Luminous Seyfert-1-like engine far away; jets at assorted angles |
| Blazar (BL Lac, optically violent variable) | Jet aimed at us | Violently variable, polarized, sometimes nearly featureless | Relativistic beaming down our line of sight swamps the lines |
| Radio galaxy (for example M87) | Jet near the sky plane | Giant double lobes and jets thousands of light-years long, weaker glare | Side view of the jet plus a low-luminosity core (M87 is a LINER nucleus) |

Radio-loud versus radio-quiet is the second axis: only about one in
ten active nuclei drives a powerful relativistic jet, likely tied to
spin and magnetic flux rather than fuel alone. LINER nuclei such as
M87's sit at the faint end — weak-line ionization from shocks plus
jet photoionization, not a hidden quasar.

Sources: NASA Webb AGN explainer (Seyfert vs quasar vs blazar);
ESO unified-model video eso2203c; UCL unified-model pages (Type
1/2, beaming); Wikipedia "Active galactic nucleus" (classes).

## How it changes over time

Black holes grow with their galaxies, and the quasar era is behind us.
Accretion power peaked at cosmic noon, redshifts ~2–3 (about
10–11 billion years ago), when gas-rich mergers funneled gas
inward and nuclei blazed — Hubble sees double quasars burning in
merging hearts that long ago, and luminous red quasars at redshift
~2 are the same merger-driven picture caught in the act. Quasar
light then poured out 100 to 1,000 times a 100-billion-star galaxy
from a region light-days to light-years across. Today most giants
are quiet; Sgr A* sips gas at far below its Eddington limit and
flares roughly daily. But quiet is not dead: the 2013 September
Chandra megaflare from our center ran 400 times brighter than its
silent state (peaks above 600 times quiescence in later analysis),
with a second giant flare 200 times brighter in October 2014 —
hour-long events from within about an astronomical unit, unrelated
to the G2 cloud. Deeper history is written in light echoes: iron
fluorescence in clouds 30–100 light-years out records outbursts a
million times brighter than today within the past few hundred
years. M87* still fires its jet, and Markarian 573 shows an active
nucleus heating and expelling gas in real time.

Sources: Wikipedia "Quasar" (peak ~10 Gyr ago); Hubble quasar-merger
science page; NASA Chandra flare release; NASA SVS Markarian 573.

## How it is created

A nucleus is built from the bottom up and the top down. Galaxies
assemble around seed black holes — collapsed massive stars, runaway
mergers in dense clusters, or direct collapse of early gas clouds —
and every gas-rich merger dumps fresh fuel that grows hole and bulge
together. The tight mass match between the two is the fingerprint:
hole mass scales roughly as bulge velocity spread to the fourth to
fifth power (the M–sigma relation, scatter ~0.4 dex), with a mean
hole-to-bulge ratio near 1:200 in massive early types, falling lower
in smaller galaxies. Active and jetted systems scatter wider and may
follow offset tracks, and local low-luminosity broad-line active
nuclei can sit an order of magnitude below the canonical line — so
read the bulge type before weighing by proxy. Between feasts the
hole waits, dark, bending the orbits of nearby stars: the S-stars
whipping around Sgr A* weighed it before any telescope saw its
shadow, with S2's 16-year orbit plus S29, S38, and S55 pinning
4.297 million Suns at 8,277 parsecs and capping any extended mass
inside S2's orbit near 1,200 Suns.

Sources: Hubble quasars science page (merger-driven growth at z~2);
Wikipedia "Active galactic nucleus" (types, fueling); GRAVITY
Collaboration multi-star fits (mass, distance, extended-mass cap).

## The other nucleus: star clusters around the hole (second pass)

Not every galactic center is just a hole. About three in four
galaxies host a nuclear star cluster — a dense ball of millions of
stars a few parsecs across, one to two orders brighter and heavier
than a globular — and some, including the Milky Way, host both
cluster and hole side by side. Ours weighs about 25 million Suns
inside a half-light radius near 4 parsecs, flattened along the
Galactic plane and spinning with it, wrapped inside a ~200-parsec
nuclear disk; the 4.3-million-Sun hole sits at its center. The two
grew together: most of the cluster's stars are metal-rich and about
5–6 billion years old with a younger in-situ disk inside half a
parsec, fed by gas that fell along the plane and by swallowed
clusters spiraling in through dynamical friction. Above about 100
million Suns of hole mass the tides win — infalling clusters shred
before they arrive, so the biggest ellipticals keep the hole and
lose the cluster. Omega Centauri in [halos](halos.md) is likely the
stripped nucleus of such a system, and M32's dense core with its own
few-million-Sun hole is the compact survivor of the same trade.

Sources: Schodel et al. nuclear-cluster work (mass, size, rotation);
2023 star-formation-history study (ages, metallicity); Graham and
Spitler 2009 (hole-cluster coexistence).

## How it interacts with the others

- **With spirals:** disk gas spiraling inward feeds the hole; the lit
  nucleus answers with winds and radiation that can heat or expel the
  disk's gas and quench star birth (see [spirals](spirals.md)).
  Markarian 573 is the feedback caught working: twin cones and
  bubbles heating and removing gas along the jet axis.
- **With ellipticals:** the biggest holes live in the biggest
  ellipticals — M87* anchors M87, and its jet at least ~4,900
  light-years long heats the whole surrounding cluster gas (see
  [ellipticals](ellipticals.md); L03
  `../../L03/documents/icm.md`). IC 1101-class holes reach tens of
  billions of Suns near cosmological bounds.
- **With dwarfs:** giants eat dwarfs and their holes merge or linger
  as multiple cores; stripped nuclei like Omega Centauri join the
  halo as fossils (see [dwarfs](dwarfs.md)).
- **With halos:** jets and winds dump energy far outside the galaxy,
  shaping the circumgalactic gas future generations of stars will
  form from (see [halos](halos.md)). The Fermi and eROSITA bubbles
  above our own center are past outburst receipts written across
  tens of thousands of light-years.

Sources: NASA SVS Markarian 573 (feedback in action); Chandra M87 jet
tracking; ESO Sgr A* release.

## Every giant was active once (second pass)

Quasars are not a rare species — they are a phase. The duty-cycle
picture says nearly every massive galaxy lit its nucleus at least once,
when gas was plentiful; today's quiet ellipticals are retired quasars,
their holes grown fat in youth. That is why hole mass tracks bulge mass
even where no jet has fired for billions of years: the M–sigma relation
in [ellipticals](ellipticals.md) is the fossil of ancient feasts.

Sources: Wikipedia "Quasar" (duty cycle, evolution); Wikipedia
"M–sigma relation".

## What is new (2020–2026)

- **Sgr A* polarized.** The March 2024 EHT polarized image (2017
  data, papers VII–VIII) shows the ring almost fully polarized with
  a spiral pattern and ~40 percent peak in the west, plus a
  5–10 percent circular-polarization dipole. Magnetically arrested
  disk models fit best; the match to M87* argues ordered fields and
  jet launching are universal across three orders of mass.
- **M87* confirmed and sharpened.** The 2019 shadow (42
  microarcseconds, 6.5 billion Suns) held steady in 2018 with the
  same diameter but a brightest point rotated ~30 degrees — shadow
  fixed by relativity, glare wobbling with turbulence. A 2021
  polarized view mapped jet-launching fields, a 2021
  multi-wavelength campaign tied horizon to kiloparsec jet, and a
  2023 PRIMO re-analysis thinned the ring to 41.5 microarcseconds
  with fractional width under ~0.23 for tighter gravity tests.
- **Sgr A* weighed to sub-percent.** GRAVITY interferometry of S2
  plus S29, S38, and S55 gives 4.297 million Suns at 8,277
  parsecs, detects Schwarzschild precession (~12 arcmin per S2
  orbit, now ~10 sigma), and caps extended mass inside S2's orbit
  near 1,200 Suns — no dark spike or second hole needed. A newly
  found 8.7-year star, S301, dives to ~140 Schwarzschild radii at
  8.5 percent of light speed and may feel black-hole spin.
- **Scaling relations under audit.** Active nuclei scatter wider
  around M–sigma than quiet ones and need separate calibration;
  low-luminosity broad-line samples sit an order of magnitude below
  the early-type line. Velocity spread remains the tightest
  predictor, but read bulge type first.
- **Quiet is flickering.** Chandra's 2013–2014 megaflares plus the
  2012 visionary-project census (39 flares in 3 megaseconds, a
  third of the X-ray output in flares) and GRAVITY flare loops at a
  few Schwarzschild radii point to magnetic-reconnection hot spots
  orbiting near the innermost stable orbit.

Sources: EHT Sgr A* papers VII–VIII (2024, eso2406); EHT M87*
2018 campaign (2024); PRIMO 2023; GRAVITY 2022–2024 mass papers;
Molina et al. 2024 and Sturm-Reines 2024 (AGN offsets); Chandra
flare spectroscopy 2013–2019.

## Size, mass, example objects

Black-hole masses ladder from stellar-mass holes of a few Suns
through intermediate-mass candidates of hundreds to hundreds of
thousands, to supermassive holes of millions to tens of billions.
The Milky Way's nuclear star cluster (~25 million Suns in stars)
outweighs its hole (~4.3 million); in giant ellipticals the hole
dominates instead. The shadow imaged is ~40–50 microarcseconds
across in both famous cases — 42 for M87*, 51.8 for Sgr A* —
physics repeating at wildly different scales because mass over
distance happens to match.

Example objects:

- **Sagittarius A*** — home nucleus, 4.297 million solar masses at
  8,277 parsecs, ring 51.8 microarcseconds (2022), faint with
  daily X-ray flares and GRAVITY-tracked S-stars. Source: ESO
  eso2208-eht-mw; GRAVITY mass papers.
- **M87*** — 6.5 billion solar masses, 55 million light-years away,
  first shadow ever imaged (2019, 42 microarcseconds), polarized
  2021, PRIMO-sharpened 2023, jet at least ~4,900 light-years near
  light-speed. Source: EHT April-2019 release; [ellipticals](ellipticals.md).
- **Markarian 573** — Seyfert-2 active nucleus caught heating and
  removing its galaxy's gas along twin cones, quenching star birth.
  Source: NASA SVS 12657.
- **Milky Way nuclear star cluster** — ~25 million Suns within ~4
  parsecs, flattened and rotating with the plane, hosting Sgr A*
  at its center: the both-nucleus case. Source: Schodel et al.
- **Double quasars in merging hearts** — Hubble-caught pairs under
  one galaxy's width apart 3 billion years after the Big Bang, the
  merger-fueling picture live. Source: Hubble quasar-merger page.

## A note on disagreeing numbers

Three numbers on this page move with method and date; read the
method first. **Sgr A* mass and distance** run from 3.95 million
Suns at 7,935 parsecs (Keck) through 4.297 million at 8,277
(GRAVITY VLTI, statistical errors only; systematics ~30 parsecs
and ~40,000 Suns) to ~4.5 million in older press material — the
EHT ring mass (~4.0 million) agrees but far looser. **M87* ring
diameter** reads 42 microarcseconds (2019 pipeline) versus 41.5 in
the PRIMO re-analysis; the mass stays 6.5 billion either way
pending recalibration. **M–sigma and hole-to-bulge ratios** hold at
~1:200 only for massive early types with classical bulges; active
and pseudobulge systems scatter wider and sit lower — never apply
the canonical line to them without the AGN calibration.
