# Heliosphere — the wind bubble with the Voyager edge

The heliosphere is the Sun's magnetic bubble: solar wind inflating
against interstellar pressure out to the heliopause near 120 AU —
the L8 anchor — with both Voyager craft beyond it in interstellar
space and IBEX plus IMAP mapping the edge from inside. This page
covers how it looks, how it changes over time, how it was created,
and how it interacts with the rest of the planetary system, plus
the preview toward L9. The wind source itself is in `sun.md`; the
debris it blew past sits in `belts.md`; the giants whose fields it
envelops are in `giants.md`.

## How it looks

Nothing here is visible to the eye — the bubble shows only in
diagrams. It reads in three layers: the termination shock inside,
where the supersonic wind drops subsonic and piles its field,
stronger and redirected, pushing against the interstellar medium;
the turbulent heliosheath still ruled by the Sun, where wind
particles roam some six months before some collide into energetic
neutral atoms (ENAs); the heliopause outside, where hot thin solar
plasma meets cold dense interstellar medium. Only two craft have
touched these layers in place: Voyager 1 felt the shock slow under
it in December 2004 at 94 AU, Voyager 2 in August 2007 at 84 AU,
proving the bubble asymmetric. Long-baseline IBEX data shapes it
like a comet: the Sun near the front, a long tail (heliotail)
trailing behind, against older spherical-globe and croissant
proposals — the asymmetric response to the 2014 solar-wind surge
settles the debate toward the comet shape. The boundary glows in
all-sky ENA maps wrapped by the bright winding IBEX ribbon of
returning energetic atoms that maps the interstellar field: a
secondary population that crosses the heliopause outward, is
re-ionized and tied to the interstellar field, then neutralized
again, taking about 2 extra years to return — the ribbon lagged the
rest of the sky by about two years after the surge. IBEX, a
bus-tire-size explorer in Earth orbit since October 19, 2008 with
its IBEX-Hi and IBEX-Lo single-pixel ENA cameras, catches only
about one atom every other second, yet built the first all-sky maps.
The Kuiper doughnut at 30 to 50 AU sits deep inside — the bubble
runs three times Pluto's distance before the medium stops it.

## How it changes over time

The bubble breathes with the 11-year solar cycle. Weak wind from
2009 to 2014 let it contract; pressure up 50 percent in late 2014
inflated it again, the heliosheath glowing two years later and the
nose filling two years after that while the far tail still waits.
The crossings prove the asymmetry and are confirmed in place:
Voyager 1 met the shock in December 2004 at 94 AU, Voyager 2 in
August 2007 at 84 AU — then Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause on
August 25, 2012 at about 122 AU (about 11 billion miles, 18 billion
km), confirmed almost a year later when its plasma-wave subsystem
caught rising plasma oscillations, its plasma instrument having
failed in 1980; and Voyager 2 crossed on November 5, 2018 at about
119 AU, confirmed at once by both particle and plasma instruments —
the only two craft ever in interstellar space. Both still climb
outward — about 3.6 AU per year for Voyager 1, about 3.3 for
Voyager 2 (rounded to 3.5 and 3.1 in the FAQ) — still reporting
through the Deep Space Network, with Voyager 1 to reach one
light-day of distance from Earth (16.094 billion miles, 25.902
billion km) at 2:16:07 a.m. PST on November 18, 2026, the first
human-made object at that range. Power fades about 4 watts a year
from the radioisotope generators, so instruments switch off one by
one to keep fields-and-particles science alive: Voyager 1 lost its
cosmic-ray subsystem on February 25, 2025 and its low-energy
charged-particle instrument on April 17, 2026; Voyager 2 lost
plasma science on September 26, 2024 and low-energy charged
particles on March 24, 2025; both were still returning science in
April 2026, with engineering range through about 2036.

Sources: NASA IBEX 11-year boundary study (June 11, 2020); NASA
Voyager fast facts and interstellar mission pages; NASA Voyager
status page (updated April 17, 2026 for instruments, August 20,
2026 for the light-day milestone).

## How it is created

The bubble is as old as the wind: the young Sun's early outflow
blew the leftover nebula gas and dust away, and today's supersonic
wind leaving the corona keeps inflating the magnetic cavity against
interstellar pressure. The wind races out at about a million miles
per hour, taking roughly a year to travel some 100 AU to the edge,
picking up interstellar pickup ions along the way so the wind that
arrives differs from the wind that left the Sun. The boundary sits
where the two push equal — about 120 AU upwind — and has sat there,
breathing, ever since the system settled. The Sun wades through the
Local Fluff, a cloud of hot interstellar gas, piling a dense wave
ahead like a boat bow wave. Earth itself lives inside the Sun's
atmosphere in this sense: the wind's bubble extends past every
planet, three times Pluto's distance before the medium stops it.

Sources: NASA heliosphere page; NASA Sun facts page; NASA IBEX
11-year boundary study (June 2020).

## How it interacts with the others

Shielding is the interaction: the heliosphere acts as a giant
shield, turning away galactic cosmic radiation before it reaches
the planets, while each planet's own field (or lack of one) decides
what gets through — compare Earth's magnetosphere with Mars's
stripped husk (see `terrestrials.md`). The Voyagers are the
interaction made visible: the only craft outside, tasting plasma
that never knew the Sun, while IBEX watches the whole boundary glow
from home orbit — taking a full 11-year solar cycle of data,
published June 10, 2020 in the Astrophysical Journal Supplements —
and IMAP, launched September 24, 2025 at 7:30 a.m. EDT on a Falcon 9
from Kennedy LC-39A alongside the Carruthers and SWFO-L1
spacecraft, arrived at Sun-Earth L1 (about a million miles
sunward) on January 10, 2026 and began its two-year primary science
mission on February 1, 2026 — maps it far sharper with 10
instruments (IMAP-Hi, IMAP-Lo, IMAP-Ultra, SWAPI, SWE, MAG, HIT,
CoDICE, GLOWS, IDEX), first-light images in December 2025, seven
instruments' data through April 30 public by August 7, 2026, plus
30-minute solar-storm warning through the I-ALiRT real-time feed on
the side. Beyond the edge: the Oort Cloud's
gravity still rules to 100,000 AU — the Voyagers need 300 years to
reach its inner rim at about 1,000 AU and 30,000 to leave it. And inward the preview:
the L9 star close-up dives into the wind's source itself, the G2V
furnace this bubble breathes from.

Sources: NASA heliophysics heliosphere pages; NASA Voyager mission
overview and FAQ; NASA IBEX mission page and 11-year boundary

12, 2026; science start February 2, 2026; data release August 7,

## Size and mass

- 1 AU is 93 million miles (150 million km); Neptune rides 30 AU.
- L8 anchor: heliopause near 120 AU — 120 times 150 million km is 18 billion km, 1.80 x 10^13 m.
- Termination shock crossings: 94 AU (Voyager 1, December 2004) and 84 AU (Voyager 2, August 2007).
- Heliopause crossings: about 122 AU (Voyager 1, August 25, 2012) and about 119 AU (Voyager 2, November 5, 2018).
- Outbound rates: about 3.6 AU per year (Voyager 1, 35 degrees north of the ecliptic toward Ophiuchus and the solar apex) and about 3.3 (Voyager 2, south toward Sagittarius and Pavo); FAQ rounds these to 3.5 and 3.1.
- Light-day milestone: 16.094 billion miles (25.902 billion km) from Earth, to be reached by Voyager 1 at 2:16:07 a.m. PST on November 18, 2026.
- ENA timing: about a year outward for the wind, about six months in the heliosheath, about a year back — two to three years at high energies, longer at low energies and from the tail; ribbon atoms take about two years more.
- IBEX: launched October 19, 2008 on a Pegasus XL; 176 lbs (80 kg); Earth orbit; IBEX-Hi and IBEX-Lo ENA cameras.
- IMAP: launched September 24, 2025; L1 arrival January 10, 2026; primary science from February 1, 2026 (two years); 10 instruments; 900 kg; real-time I-ALiRT feed.
- Oort inner rim about 1,000 AU (estimates vary, up to 5,000 AU): 300 years for the Voyagers; outer rim 100,000 AU: 30,000 years.

Sources: NASA Oort scale infographic (PIA22921, December 2018);
NASA Voyager fast facts, FAQ, interstellar mission and status
pages; NASA IBEX and IMAP mission and IMAP blog pages (January 12,
February 2, and August 7, 2026).

## Example objects

Example boundaries: the termination shock both craft felt slow
under them, field strengthening and turning; the heliopause plasma
jump Voyager 2's working plasma instrument caught in full while
Voyager 1 needed plasma-wave oscillations a year later; the IBEX
ribbon winding across all-sky maps, lagging the surge by two years;
the heliosheath glow brightening two years after the 2014 pressure
rise and the nose filling two years after that, tail still awaited;
the Oort rim where the Sun's gravity finally yields. Example craft:
Voyager 1 up 35 degrees out of the plane toward Ophiuchus and the
solar apex, Voyager 2 down toward Sagittarius and Pavo — each
within 1.7 light-years of another star 40,000 years from now
(AC+79 3888 in Ursa Minor for Voyager 1 in year 40272; Ross 248 in
Andromeda for Voyager 2). Example watchers: IBEX from Earth orbit
since 2008; IMAP at L1 since January 2026 with MAG, SWAPI, HIT,
GLOWS, SWE, CoDICE, and IDEX data public and I-ALiRT space-weather
feed live.

Sources: NASA Voyager interstellar mission, fast-facts, and FAQ
pages; NASA IBEX pages and IBEX 11-year study; NASA IMAP mission
and blog pages.
