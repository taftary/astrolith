# Lifetime — the dated story of the planetary system

This page runs the full dated timeline the part docs tell piece
by piece: from the solar nebula's collapse through planet
building, migration, bombardment and belt sculpting, the long
stable era, the Voyager crossing era, and the Sun's red-giant
fate with the far future after. Dates carry sources; the reading
guide maps each stage to its part doc.

## 4.567 Ga — solar nebula collapses, Sun and planets form

A dense cloud of interstellar gas and dust collapses — possibly
triggered by the shockwave of a nearby supernova — into a
spinning solar nebula, a swirling disk of material. At the
center, gravity piles matter inward until hydrogen fuses into
helium and the Sun is born about 4.6 billion years ago,
eventually amassing more than 99 percent of the available
matter. Matter farther out clumps and smashes together into
larger bodies, some growing massive enough for gravity to shape
them into spheres. Nearest the Sun only rocky material withstands
the heat, so the first four planets come out small and rocky (see
[terrestrials.md](./terrestrials.md)); ice, liquid and gas settle
outside into the giants (see [giants.md](./giants.md)); Jupiter's
pull freezes the belt as rubble that never quite comes together
into a planet (see [belts.md](./belts.md)); the young Sun's early
wind blows the leftovers out and inflates the first bubble (see
[heliosphere.md](./heliosphere.md)). The system
settles into its current layout about 4.5 billion years ago when
Earth forms as the third planet; the Moon-forming impact follows
when a large body strikes the young Earth and the debris gathers
239,000 miles out.

Sources: NASA solar system facts page; NASA Sun, Earth and Moon
facts pages; NASA Oort cloud facts page.

## 4.5–4.4 Ga — giants migrate, belts sculpted, Oort emplaced

The four giants shift orbits from an initial compact
configuration: Uranus and Neptune are forced outward through the
icy disk while Jupiter slingshots 7 to 10 Earth masses of it —
most to the Oort Cloud or out of the system entirely, the rest
sculpted into classical, resonant and scattered Kuiper
populations under Neptune's stirring. In the Nice-model picture,
slow planetesimal-driven migration ends when Jupiter and Saturn
cross their mutual resonance, destabilizing the system and
scattering the disk; modern timing estimates range from an early
instability within about 100 million years of formation (before
about 4.48 Ga on meteorite chronometers, or 60–100 Myr on EL
chondrite and Athor-family evidence) to later variants, so the
timeline keeps the broad migration window and notes the debate.
Neptune's outward plow bends countless icy paths sunward and
drives its own orbit farther out; the cold classicals at 40 to
50 AU stay circular and untouched while hot ones are excited, the
plutinos lock into 3:2 resonance, and the scattered disk —
Eris its largest member — is thrown outward. Ceres freezes in the
belt as an embryo that never finished, formed 4.5 billion years
ago and settled into its belt location about 4 billion years ago.
The Oort shells take their spherical shape from about
2,000–5,000 AU at the inner edge (about 1,000 AU on the low
Voyager-clock estimates used with the crossing rates; NASA's
facts overview gives 5,000 AU) to 10,000–100,000 AU at the outer
edge (1.6 light-years), the boundary of the Sun's gravitational
influence — a thick bubble of distant icy debris orbiting under,
over and at all inclinations, home to hundreds of billions or
trillions of bodies and the source of the long-period comets.
Sunlight itself needs 10 to 28 days to reach the inner edge and
up to a year and a half to clear the outer rim; Voyager 1 at
about a million miles a day needs 300 years to enter and perhaps
30,000 to exit.

Sources: NASA Kuiper Belt facts page; NASA Ceres facts page;
Nice-model instability and migration-timing studies.

## 4.1–3.8 Ga — bombardment, comet delivery, first life

Migration's wreckage rains inward in a bombardment recorded on
the Moon, Mercury and Mars while Jupiter corrals the flow. The
classic Late Heavy Bombardment picture puts a cataclysmic spike
around 4 billion years ago — near-side Apollo impact melts
clustering near 3.9 Ga, basins from Serenitatis to Imbrium in a
20- to 200-million-year pulse driven by the giant-planet shift —
but the cataclysm is now debated: basin dates are hard to pin
down, contamination spreads ages, and newer measurements find
older melt components, so alternatives run from a gradual
decline since formation to segmented pulses to no spike at all,
with far-side samples (notably the South Pole–Aitken basin)
named as the decisive test. Comets from the fresh reservoirs
deliver water and organics through it all; Earth's vast oceans
gather and life begins about 3.8 billion years ago, surviving or
quickly following whatever the bombardment's true profile was.
The Kirkwood gaps are carved where Jupiter's resonances — 3:1 at
2.50 AU, 5:2 at 2.82 AU, 2:1 at 3.28 AU — make orbits chaotic
and drop bodies planetward.

Sources: NASA Late Heavy Bombardment explainer; NASA Moon facts
page (magma-ocean crystallization, maria 4.2–1.2 Ga); NASA Earth
facts page; Nice-model references.

## 3.8 Ga–present — stable era, life, slow erosion

The system settles into the long calm: planets on fixed tracks,
belts grinding slowly down through collisions and Yarkovsky drift,
storms evolving on the giants, the bubble breathing with each solar
cycle. Earth alone blooms. Humanity arrives late: Sputnik on
October 4, 1957; Voyager 2 on August 20, 1977 and Voyager 1 on
September 5; Jupiter in March 1979 and Saturn in 1980–81; Uranus
on January 24, 1986 and Neptune in 1989 — each the only visit;
the Pale Blue Dot portrait on February 14, 1990 from 3.7 billion
miles (6 billion km) from the Sun — one frame of the 60-image
family portrait, cameras powered off forever 34 minutes later;
revisited in 2020 with modern processing for the 30th
anniversary; Pluto on July 14, 2015 and Arrokoth on January 1,
2019. The small-body record keeps paying off: OSIRIS-REx touched
Bennu on October 20, 2020 and delivered 121.6 grams to Utah on
September 24, 2023 — the largest asteroid sample yet — with
life-ingredient molecules and a saltwater past reported in January
2025; DART's deflection at the 160-meter moonlet Dimorphos on
September 26, 2022 shortened its 11-hour-55-minute orbit by about
33 minutes (over 25 times the 73-second success bar) and, in the
March 2026 analysis, measurably changed the binary's 770-day
orbit around the Sun by 0.15 seconds with momentum enhancement
near two — the first human alteration of a celestial orbit around
the Sun; Lucy rehearsed on Dinkinesh with its contact-binary moon
Selam (November 1, 2023) and Donaldjohanson (April 20, 2025)
before its Trojan flybys open at Eurybates in August 2027, while
Psyche cruises toward its metal world for August 2029.

The 2020–2026 watch extends the timeline into the present:
OSIRIS-REx's Bennu sample lands September 24, 2023; ESA's JUICE
launches April 14, 2023 for Jupiter arrival July 2031; Europa
Clipper launches October 14, 2024 for Jupiter arrival April 2030
(49 Europa flybys; Earth gravity assist December 3, 2026); ESA's
Hera launches October 7, 2024 and reaches the Didymos system in
November 2026 to survey DART's crater with its Milani and
Juventas CubeSats; IMAP launches September 24, 2025 and begins
primary science February 1, 2026; ESCAPADE twins arrive at Mars
in September 2027 to watch the wind strip it in real time.

Sources: NASA solar system and Earth facts pages; NASA Voyager,
Uranus, Neptune, New Horizons, OSIRIS-REx and DART pages; NASA
March 2026 DART heliocentric-orbit result; ESA Hera mission pages;
NASA Europa Clipper and JUICE pages; Voyager Pale Blue Dot page.

## 2004–2026 — Voyager era: launches, shock, heliopause, IMAP

The edge is touched in place by the only two craft ever in
interstellar space: Voyager 1 crosses the termination shock in
December 2004 at 94 AU and the heliopause on August 25, 2012 at
about 122 AU (confirmed almost a year later through plasma-wave
oscillations, its plasma instrument having failed in 1980);
Voyager 2 crosses the shock in August 2007 at 84 AU and the
heliopause on November 5, 2018 at about 119 AU (confirmed at
once by particle and plasma instruments together). Both keep
climbing — about 3.6 AU per year for Voyager 1, about 3.3 for
Voyager 2 — while IBEX maps the breathing boundary from home
orbit since October 19, 2008 and IMAP, launched September 24,
2025 at 7:30 a.m. EDT on a Falcon 9 from Kennedy LC-39A, takes
station at Sun–Earth L1 (about a million miles sunward) for the
sharp view with 10 instruments. Power fades about 4 watts a year
from the radioisotope generators, so instruments switch off one
by one to keep fields-and-particles science alive: Voyager 1
lost cosmic-ray science on February 25, 2025 and low-energy
charged particles on April 17, 2026; Voyager 2 lost plasma
science on September 26, 2024 and low-energy charged particles
on March 24, 2025; both still return science with engineering
range through about 2036. Milestone watch: Voyager 1 reaches one
light-day of distance from Earth (16.094 billion miles, 25.902
billion km) at 2:16:07 a.m. PST on November 18, 2026 — the first
human-made object at that range.

Sources: NASA Voyager fast facts, interstellar mission and status
pages (instrument table updated April 17, 2026; light-day page);
NASA IBEX and IMAP mission and IMAP blog pages.

## 1.3 Myr–5 Gyr future — passages, red-giant fate, stripped cloud

Stellar passages come first: Gliese 710, a 0.6-solar-mass orange
star now 62 light-years out in Serpens Cauda, passes about
10,520 AU out (0.051 parsecs, one twenty-fifth of the Proxima
distance; Gaia DR3 refines this to 10,635 ± 500 AU) in about
1.29 million years, plunging deep into the Oort Cloud and
stirring the outer shells into comet showers. The Voyagers reach
the Oort inner rim at about 1,000 AU in 300 years and leave the
rim in 30,000 — but need many thousands of years to exit the
cloud at all. Then the Sun's fate, a little less than halfway
through its life today: in about 5 billion years it runs out of
core hydrogen, expands into a red giant so large it engulfs
Mercury and Venus and possibly Earth as well, before settling as
a white dwarf — the surviving planets' orbits shifting through
the mass loss while passing stars strip the Oort Cloud away
entirely beyond the Sun's gravitational boundary.
