# The Sun — the star at the center

The Sun is the one
star of L8: a 4.6-billion-year-old G2V yellow dwarf — a hot glowing
ball of hydrogen and helium plasma holding 99.8
percent of the system's mass. G2 sets its surface-temperature class
near 5,500 degrees C; V marks it a main-sequence dwarf fusing
hydrogen into helium in its core. This page covers how it looks
from the inside out, how it changes over time, how it was created,
and how it interacts with
the rest of the planetary system. The bridge behind us is in
[previous-l7.md](./previous-l7.md); the dive that brings you here
is in [entry.md](./entry.md).

## How it looks

The Sun is a blinding disk about 1.4 million km across — about 100
times wider than Earth and 10 times wider than Jupiter — with no
solid surface anywhere. From the inside out it reads in stacked
regions. The core is the furnace: hydrogen fusing into helium near
27 million degrees F (15 million degrees C), about 86,000 miles
(138,000 km) thick, near 150 grams per cubic centimeter — about 8
times the density of gold. Energy crawls out through the radiative
zone, bouncing for about 170,000 years toward the surface. Above
it the convection zone boils: below about 3.5 million degrees F
(2 million degrees C) great bubbles of hot plasma rise toward the
light. The part we see is the photosphere, a 250-mile-thick
layer of plasma at about 10,000 degrees F (5,500 degrees C)
that emits most of the visible light — hot enough to boil carbon,
yet the coolest layer of the atmosphere above the interior. Above it stretch the thin
chromosphere, the narrow transition zone where heating runs steep,
and the huge corona, reaching 3.5 million degrees F
(2 million degrees C) — hotter the farther out it goes, one of the
Sun's biggest unsolved puzzles. Once plasma leaves the corona
supersonic it becomes the solar wind (see
[heliosphere.md](./heliosphere.md)).

The disk carries structure:
sunspots like dark holes (cooler magnetic patches, 1,000 to 100,000
miles across — 1,600 to 160,900 km — living days to months, where
field lines poke out and mark active regions), coronal holes as dark
UV splotches where field lines open straight to space and fast wind
escapes, flares as the most powerful explosions in the solar system
(flashes of radiation across X-rays to radio; classes run A, B, C,
M, X with each class 10 times the last — an X outshines an M
tenfold and a C a hundredfold; the cycle's strongest so far was X9.0
on October 3, 2024, and the overwhelmed 2003 sensors stopped near
X28), coronal mass ejections as magnetized clouds of billions of
tons blasted out over a million miles per hour — expanding to
millions of miles across and reaching Earth in 15 hours at fastest
or days at slowest — prominences as snaking plasma loops (filaments
seen head-on against the disk) that can erupt when their fields go
unstable, and up to 10 million spicule jets bursting at any moment,
grass-like tendrils to 6,000 miles (9,700 km) at up to 60 miles per
second that then collapse. Visible light from the high layers hides
against the photosphere except in eclipse, when the chromosphere
rims red and the white corona crowns outward in petal streamers.

## How it changes over time

The Sun spins, but not as one piece: plasma at the equator turns
once in about 25 Earth days, at the poles in about 36, tilted 7.25
degrees to the planets' plane — the shear that tangles the field
like an uncombed head of hair until lines snap and reconnect.
Every 11 years or so it runs its activity cycle: poles swap magnetic
polarity while spots, flares
and ejections ramp from quiet calm to violent maximum. Cycle 25 ran
from minimum in December 2019 into a maximum phase NASA and NOAA
declared in October 2024 — SDO's bare December 2019 disk against its
freckled May 2024 disk is the before-and-after — with sunspot counts
slightly above forecast, the exact peak identifiable only after a
sustained decline, and another year or so of maximum expected before
the long fall back to minimum; large storms also strike in the
declining phase. The cycle's proof came in May 2024: from May 3 to
May 9 SDO logged 82 notable flares from active regions AR 13663
and AR 13664, eight of them X-class to X5.8, then at least seven
CMEs bunched at up to 3 million mph into a G5 geomagnetic storm —
the highest rung, unseen since 2003 — with auroras to 26 degrees
magnetic latitude, among the lowest-latitude sightings in 500 years,
and a follow-up X8.7 on May 14, the cycle's largest flare at the
time. The October 3, 2024 X9.0 then took the cycle record. On the
long clock it circles the Milky Way at 450,000 miles per hour,
taking 230 million years per orbit from its seat in the Orion Spur.
And on the longest clock it ages: a little less than halfway
through its life, with about 5 billion years left before it swells
into a red giant — engulfing Mercury and Venus and possibly Earth —
and ends as a white dwarf. Watchers keep it
honest: SOHO — the ESA/NASA Solar and Heliospheric Observatory,
the longest-lived Sun watcher — has stared since December 1995
(25 years marked December 2, 2020) with its EIT imager and LASCO
coronagraph; the Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO, launched February
11, 2010) watches the disk in extreme ultraviolet, tracks active
regions and magnetic fields, and feeds the flare and CME movies;
the Solar TErrestrial RElations Observatory (STEREO) watches from
ahead of Earth in its orbit; Solar Orbiter flies the close
complementary pass; Hinode, IRIS and Wind cover fine structure and
particles — while NOAA's Space Weather
Prediction Center turns active-region watches into alerts for the
grid, satellites and GPS users below. Parker Solar Probe closes the
loop by flying through the storm source itself: launched August 12,
2018 near solar minimum, first through the corona in 2021, then to
a record 3.8 million miles from the surface at 430,000 mph on
December 24, 2024 — matched on seven flybys since, most recently
the 29th encounter on September 4, 2026, sampling nearly 40 percent
of the solar circumference in a day with its north-pole camera —
its 29 encounters spanning quiet to very active Sun, extended into
2029, with telemetry and science returns running mid-September 2026
for the latest pass. Its 2022 reconnection flyby, published March
2026, caught protons fanning like a flashlight while heavy ions
lased straight — against same-manner theory — sharpening the
magnetic-reconnection models behind every flare and CME forecast.

## How it is created

The Sun formed about 4.6 billion years ago when a giant spinning
cloud of gas and dust — the solar nebula, perhaps kicked by a
nearby supernova shock — collapsed under its own
gravity, spun faster and flattened into a disk. Most of the cloud
fell to the center until hydrogen fused into helium and the star
lit; the leftovers made the
planets or were blown away by the young Sun's early wind — the same
outflow that inflated the first heliosphere (see
[heliosphere.md](./heliosphere.md)). A trace of that disk survives
as dust rings circling the Sun along planetary orbits, herded by
planetary gravity. That is
why one object holds nearly everything: the Sun accounts for 99.8
percent of the solar system's mass, and the planets, belts and
comets share the rest. Rocky material alone withstood the inner heat,
so the first four worlds came out small and solid (see
[terrestrials.md](./terrestrials.md)); ice, liquid and gas settled
outside into the giants (see [giants.md](./giants.md)); Jupiter's
pull froze the belt as rubble (see [belts.md](./belts.md)).

Sources: NASA Sun facts page (nebula, 99.8 percent, red giant and
white dwarf); NASA solar system facts page; NASA dust-ring finding
(planetary orbits).

## How it interacts with the others

Gravity is the first interaction: the Sun's mass holds the whole
system together, and every planet, belt and comet orbits it — eight
planets, at least five dwarf planets, tens of thousands of
asteroids, perhaps three trillion comets and icy bodies. Light
is the second: its energy sets the habitable zone — the range of
distances where liquid water can exist — and without it life on
Earth could not exist; nothing could live on the star itself in its
heat and radiation. Wind is the third: supersonic plasma leaving
the corona at over a million miles per hour — protons and electrons
from coronal holes, active regions and streamers — inflates the heliosphere, a magnetic bubble past the
planets bent into a Parker spiral by the Sun's rotation (named for
Eugene Parker, whose 1958 theory predicted the wind, confirmed in
1959), shielding
the system from galactic cosmic rays (see [heliosphere.md](./heliosphere.md)).
Earth lives inside the Sun's atmosphere in this sense, past every
planet to three times Pluto's distance before the medium stops it.
The
same wind drives space weather in three forms: the flare flash
(light at light speed, 8 minutes to Earth), the radiation storm
(energetic protons and electrons in 30 minutes or less, piercing
satellites and threatening astronauts and polar flyers), and the
coronal mass ejection (days or 15 hours at fastest, with embedded
fields that compress Earth's field into geomagnetic storms). The
record runs from the 1859 Carrington
Event that set telegraph paper on fire — telegraph shocks, planet-wide
red-green-purple auroras to Cuba, Hawaii and El Salvador bright
enough to read by — through the 1989 storm that
blacked out 6 million people in Quebec for 9 hours and melted New
Jersey transformers, the December 2005 X-ray burst that cut
satellite-to-ground and GPS links for 10 minutes, to May 2024's G5
storm that pushed ICESat-2 to safe mode on atmospheric drag while
STEREO kept watch a day past Earth's view and the same region
turned to face Mars. Names carry the history: sol in Latin gives
solar, Helios in Greek gives heliosphere and heliophysics.

## Size and mass

- Type: G2 V yellow-dwarf main-sequence star; age 4.6 billion years, a little less than halfway through its life; another about 5 billion years to red giant, then white dwarf.
- Diameter about 865,000 miles (1.4 million km; radius about 435,000 miles / 700,000 km); mass over 330,000 Earths (99.8 percent of the system); volume 1.3 million Earths.
- Distance to Earth: 93 million miles (150 million km), exactly 1 AU; 8 light-minutes away.
- Nearest neighbor: Proxima Centauri 4.24 light-years away (Alpha Centauri A and B at 4.37); one light-year is about 6 trillion miles (9.5 trillion km).
- Spin: 25 Earth days at the equator, 36 at the poles; tilt 7.25 degrees to the planets' plane.
- Orbit: Orion Spur off the Sagittarius arm; 450,000 mph (720,000 kph) around the galaxy; one circuit about 230 million years.
- Core over 27 million degrees F (15 million degrees C), 86,000 miles thick, near 150 g per cubic centimeter; radiative crawl about 170,000 years; convection below 3.5 million degrees F; photosphere 10,000 degrees F (5,500 degrees C), 250 miles thick; corona to 3.5 million degrees F (2 million degrees C).
- Cycle: about 11 years; Cycle 25 minimum December 2019, maximum phase from October 2024; May 2024 G5 storm; X9.0 October 3, 2024; Parker at 3.8 million miles and 430,000 mph.

Sources: NASA Sun facts page (type, size, distance, temperatures,
rotation, orbit); NASA Parker Solar Probe page (3.8 million miles,
430,000 mph); NASA May 2024 storm tracking (G5, X8.7).

## Example objects

Example features: a large sunspot group (cooler magnetic patch,
days to months, 1,000 to 100,000 miles);
a coronal hole (fast-wind source, dark in ultraviolet); an X-class flare with coronal
mass ejection (space-weather driver — AR 13663/13664 in May 2024,
X8.7 on May 14, X9.0 on October 3); a filament eruption seen as a
prominence at the limb; spicules to 6,000 miles at 60 miles per
second; the Parker-spiral field lines carried by
the wind. Example numbers: one 11-year cycle; one 25-day equatorial
spin; Parker through the corona since 2021 at 3.8 million miles;
one red-giant fate engulfing Mercury and Venus and possibly Earth.
Example watchers: SOHO since December 1995; SDO since February 2010;
Parker since August 2018 (29th pass September 4, 2026); Solar
Orbiter and STEREO alongside.
