# Entering L8 — the dive from Outer solar system

This page walks the L7-to-L8
dive in plain steps: what you approach, what changes on entry, and
the numbers behind it. The bridge behind us lives in
[previous-l7.md](./previous-l7.md); the part docs and the timeline now exist — the
cross-links below say where each sight belongs.

## The approach, in plain steps

1. **Pick the home portal.** Start in the L7 cell holding the
   outer solar system — the Oort-cloud-edge anchor of L7.
   32 markers share the cell (one star plus shell populations);
   only portals open, populations stay points (ADR 0010). Target the
   portal with the familiar yellow glare: home. It holds one L8
   planetary system at a true size ratio of 1.20e-3.
2. **Watch the preview.** Above 0.02 rad the marker draws its
   interior inside itself at the exact positions the open cell will
   show, at true size (`marker + child x ratio`, ladder R7): first
   the Sun's disk with light and wind, then the four rocky worlds,
   then the four giants as disks with rings and large moons, then
   the asteroid belt with dwarf planets, then the Kuiper edge past
   Neptune, then the wind bubble itself with termination shock and
   heliopause — growing as the dot fades from full brightness
   toward a faint floor (0.15) at the open angle while its children
   brighten from 0 to 1, both curves continuous, so the moment of
   opening is a pure origin shift with no pop. At most 6 markers
   (largest on screen) preview at once. Only portal markers
   preview: field populations are displayed and counted but never
   open and never preview.
3. **Fall with two milestones.** The L7–L8 span is crossed through
   two invisible magnification milestones, because its ~2.9-decade
   span (ladder anchors e 16.17 down to e 13.25) exceeds the
   1.5-decade single-crossing limit. The dive pushes by proximity
   to the targeted portal and unwinds the same way. You notice it
   only as a long smooth fall across nearly three decades of scale
   (ladder gap note) — one of the longest legs of the journey. On
   the way, the distant spheres thin out into background while the
   home glare swells: first one yellow glare among the shells, then
   the only thing ahead, its order filling half the sky.
4. **Open at 0.14 rad.** Past the open angle (about a third of the
   view) the marker's dot eases to zero as the camera passes
   inside; the open cell closes back into its marker below 0.10
   rad, re-targeted. You are now in L8, inside the
   planetary-system cell. The parent's siblings hang 1/ratio cells
   away (about 830 L8-cell widths for the 1.20e-3 ratio); nothing
   shallower is drawn.

## Effects and visuals on entry

**What appears.** The order swells into view around the glare:
first the Sun's disk with light and wind — the G2V dwarf that is
ours, holding 99.8 percent of the system's mass (see
[sun.md](./sun.md)) — then the four rocky worlds, Mercury, Venus,
Earth with its Moon, Mars, small with solid surfaces where only
rock withstood the early heat (see
[terrestrials.md](./terrestrials.md)). The giants resolve next as
disks: Jupiter at 5.2 AU and Saturn near 9.5 AU as banded gas
giants, Uranus near 19 AU and Neptune near 30 AU as ice giants,
with rings and large moons now round worlds of their own (see
[giants.md](./giants.md)). The debris shows after them: the
asteroid belt with dwarf planet Ceres, then the Kuiper doughnut
from 30 to about 50 AU with Pluto among its dots (see
[belts.md](./belts.md)). The bubble and the edge resolve last: the
solar wind out to the termination shock at 80–100 AU and the
heliopause near 120 AU where both Voyager craft crossed into
interstellar space (see [heliosphere.md](./heliosphere.md)).

**What fades.** The shell count falls away: the Oort shells from
about 1,000–5,000 AU (inner edge, estimates vary) to 100,000 AU,
the long-period reservoir the comets fall from — still there, now
behind you, reduced to a reference shell. The shell-census story
(who the belt populations are, how the scattered and detached
strays drift, how the comet traffic falls) stops being terrain and
becomes somebody else's story; here the story is the ordered
worlds of one system themselves.

**What the markers do.** The L7 marker you entered closes behind
you into an ordinary portal, re-targeted, sitting 1/ratio cells
away among its siblings. Ahead, the L8 cell offers its own
markers: 32 per L8 cell (one star, its planets, belt populations)
at ratio 7.76e-5 with three zoom gaps — the next dive is the star
close-up of L9. What waits inside is now written up part by part:
the star ([sun.md](./sun.md)), the rocky worlds
([terrestrials.md](./terrestrials.md)), the giants
([giants.md](./giants.md)), the belts
([belts.md](./belts.md)), and the bubble with the boundary
([heliosphere.md](./heliosphere.md)).

In order, on the way in:

1. The shells thin and go: the Oort sphere and scattered dots
   dissolve into black, their faint light now behind you — the
   reservoir reduced to a reference shell.
2. One glare takes center: the Sun, blinding up close, a 4.6
   billion-year-old G2V yellow dwarf about 1.4 million km across
   (about 865,000 miles, about 100 times wider than Earth),
   holding 99.8 percent of the system's mass, with light setting
   the habitable zone and wind blowing the bubble.
3. The rocky worlds appear: Mercury, Venus, Earth with the Moon,
   Mars — small with solid surfaces, nearest the Sun where only
   rock withstood the early heat.
4. The giants resolve as disks: Jupiter at 5.2 AU and Saturn near
   9.5 AU as banded gas giants, Uranus near 19 AU and Neptune near
   30 AU as ice giants, with rings and large moons now round worlds
   of their own.
5. The debris shows: the asteroid belt with dwarf planet Ceres,
   then the Kuiper doughnut from 30 to about 50 AU with Pluto among
   its dots — with the scattered disk beyond and detached strays
   such as Eris and Sedna far at this zoom (see
   [belts.md](./belts.md)).
6. The bubble and the edge resolve: solar wind out to the
   termination shock at 80–100 AU (Voyager 1 in December 2004 at
   about 94 AU, Voyager 2 in August 2007 at about 84 AU) and the
   heliopause near 120 AU where Voyager 1 crossed on August 25,
   2012 at about 122 AU and Voyager 2 on November 5, 2018 at
   about 119 AU — with the preview toward the L9 star close-up
   (see [heliosphere.md](./heliosphere.md)).
7. The markers fill again: from 32 portals per L7 cell to 32 per L8
   cell (one star, its planets, belt populations) at ratio
   7.76e-5 with three zoom gaps — the next dive is the star
   close-up of L9.

## Survey context

The order you dive into is counted and visited, not decoration.
The star is weighed: a G2V yellow dwarf of about 4.6 billion years
holding 99.8 percent of the system's mass, watched continuously by
SOHO since 1995 with Parker Solar Probe, STEREO, Solar Orbiter,
SDO and others keeping it honest (see [sun.md](./sun.md)). The
inner order is mapped rock by rock: the main asteroid belt between
Mars and Jupiter holds leftovers that never became a planet —
Vesta the largest at about 530 km across, the total mass of all
asteroids combined less than Earth's Moon — with Ceres the dwarf
planet among them (see [belts.md](./belts.md)). The outer order is
flown: Voyager 1 and 2 toured all four giants and 48 of their moons
between them, New Horizons crossed the Kuiper doughnut (Pluto in
July 2015, Arrokoth on January 1, 2019), and Cassini spent 13 years
at Saturn — so the banded gas giants, the ice giants, the rings
and the large moons resolve as photographed worlds, not artist
guesses (see [giants.md](./giants.md), [belts.md](./belts.md)).
And the edge is weighed in place, not assumed: Voyager 1 crossed
the termination shock in December 2004 at about 94 AU and the
heliopause in August 2012 at about 122 AU, Voyager 2 crossed in
August 2007 at about 84 AU and in November 2018 at about 119 AU,
and both keep reporting from interstellar space at more than 3 AU
per year (see [heliosphere.md](./heliosphere.md)). None of this
changes the entry mechanics above; it is why the preview's
planetary geography can be trusted as measured structure.
Sources: NASA Sun and solar system facts pages; NASA asteroids
overview; NASA Kuiper Belt facts page; NASA Voyager fast facts;
L08 part docs ([sun.md](./sun.md), [belts.md](./belts.md),
[giants.md](./giants.md) and [heliosphere.md](./heliosphere.md)).

## Timing and scale

The L7 anchor (Oort cloud outer edge, 1.50 x 10^16 m) gives way to
the L8 anchor (heliopause, 1.80 x 10^13 m) — nearly three decades
closer in characteristic size (e 16.17 down to e 13.25), ratio
1.20e-3 per rung table, one of the longest legs of the journey.
Travel time per rung runs `Δe · ln 10 / k`, so this leg runs long;
the longest leg stays the four-decade heliopause-to-Sun run just
below it (L8–L9, ratio 7.76e-5). The seeded autopilot runs L1 to
L10 in about 40 seconds (ladder R8); this ~2.9-decade leg is one of
its longest crossings. That compression is honest: rung gaps on
screen stay proportional to the real decade gaps, so L8 sits far
below L7 — the heliopause really is about a thousand times closer
in than the Oort rim in linear terms. Inside L8, distances read in
astronomical units: 1 AU for Earth, 5.2 to 30 AU for the giants,
30 to 50 AU for the main belt (scattered disk beyond, out to
nearly 1,000 AU), near 120 AU for the heliopause, out to
100,000 AU for the Oort rim far behind. One AU is about 93
million miles (150 million km).

## What entry proves

Entry is complete when the shells are gone, one star commands the
view, and the planets stand ordered: you count worlds and belts,
not shells. If you can name the yellow dwarf that is ours, the four
rocky worlds in order with the Moon, the four giants in order with
rings, the belt with Ceres, the Kuiper edge with Pluto, and the two
craft at the wind edge — you are in L8. The detailed looks,
lifetimes and interactions of each kind live in the part docs:
[sun.md](./sun.md) for the star, [terrestrials.md](./terrestrials.md) for the rocky worlds,
[giants.md](./giants.md) for the outer disks, [belts.md](./belts.md) for the belts and dwarf
planets, [heliosphere.md](./heliosphere.md) for the bubble and the boundary — with the
dated story in [lifetime.md](./lifetime.md) — start with its reading guide to map
each sight to its stage.

## Key numbers

- L7 anchor: 100,000 AU (1.50 x 10^16 m); L8 anchor: 120 AU (1.80 x 10^13 m); ratio 1.20e-3.
- Portals: 32 per L7 cell (one star plus shell populations) → 32 per L8 cell; L7–L8 takes two zoom gaps, L8–L9 takes three.
- Open angle 0.14 rad, close 0.10 rad, preview from 0.02 rad (at most 6 markers).
- Sun: G2V yellow dwarf, 4.6 billion years old, 99.8 percent of system mass, diameter about 1.4 million km (about 865,000 miles).
- Planets: Mercury Venus Earth Mars rocky; Jupiter 5.2 AU, Saturn about 9.5 AU, Uranus about 19 AU, Neptune about 30 AU.
- Kuiper Belt 30–50 AU (main belt; scattered disk beyond, out to nearly 1,000 AU); termination shock 80–100 AU (Voyager 1 in December 2004 at about 94 AU, Voyager 2 in August 2007 at about 84 AU); heliopause about 120 AU (Voyager 1 on August 25, 2012 at about 122 AU, Voyager 2 on November 5, 2018 at about 119 AU); Oort inner edge about 1,000–5,000 AU (estimates vary), outer rim near 100,000 AU.
