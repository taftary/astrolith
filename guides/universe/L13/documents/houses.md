## How it looks

A house is living quarters for one or more families: walls that carry the floors and roof,
a roof overhead, windows and a door to the street. Common American forms read clearly from
the ground — the Ranch on one level with a low roof and open rectangular plan and little
ornament; Colonial symmetry with classical detail; Cape Cod, Craftsman, Tudor Revival,
Victorian gables; Mid-Century Modern flat planes. Roofs run asphalt shingle, wood, clay tile,
slate, or metal over gabled, flat, domed, or vaulted shapes; the first roofs were thatch,
branches, timber, brick, and stone. From above, closely packed neighborhoods read light grey
against leafy suburbs dark green, with drainage canals, golf courses, and lakes threaded
between the subdivisions; by night the street grid glows and the desert around it stays dark.

## How it changes over time

Houses age in place: the median owner-occupied US home is about 40 years old, built around
1981, and almost half the stock predates 1980. Styles march by era — Colonial 1600s–1700s,
Victorian late 1800s, Craftsman early 1900s, Cape Cod and Colonial Revival 1920s–40s, Ranch
with the car and suburb 1950s–70s, larger two-story suburban into the 2015 peak, smaller again
after. Change comes by renovation and addition — extra bathrooms, heat pumps replacing
furnaces, finished basements — and by lot splits and infill that shrink lots to record lows.
Whole communities are built at once for one stage of life, like the 1969 manufactured-home
town of Barefoot Bay with its 10,000 people, recreation halls, and canals.

Sources: NAHB housing-stock age pages; Census year-built and rooms pages; NASA Barefoot Bay page.

## How it is created

The predominant method is light wood frame: 94 percent of completed US single-family homes
in 2024–25 are wood-framed. The sequence runs permit authorization, then start — nearly half
start the same month as the permit — then framing (a small one-story shell in 2–3 days, a
larger multi-story in 2–3 weeks), sheathing and roof, plumbing and electrical, finishes, and
sale. Permit to completion averages 9.1 months; built-for-sale is fastest at 7.6 months and
owner-built slowest at 15.1. Heating splits by region — gas furnaces in the Northeast, heat
pumps across the South — and nine in ten new homes connect to public sewer, the rest to
individual septic.

Sources: NAHB wood-framing and build-time pages; Census new-housing highlights; Census time-to-complete tables.

## How it interacts with the others

Houses line the subdivided lots along the street grids: 65 percent of new spec homes sold in
2023 sit on lots under 9,000 sq ft, 40 percent under 7,000. Many answer to homeowners
associations, and multifamily buildings cluster 50 or more units to a block. Between the detached
house and the tower stands the missing middle — duplexes, townhouses, and low-rise apartments
that take house-scale lots at block-scale density (see `blocks.md` for the tower end of that
ladder, including offices converting into apartments). Every house plugs
into the same buried and wired systems — public water and sewer or septic, paved streets,
drainage canals, power lines — systems laid down over centuries from bored-log water pipes to
universal electricity by 1960. Lawns, streets, and septic fields shed nutrients and sediment,
so the runoff of a suburb can cloud a river and feed algae blooms downstream.

Sources: NAHB lot-size pages; Census new-housing highlights; HUD history of housing systems; NASA Barefoot Bay page.

## Typical size and mass

A completed new single-family home carries a median 2,142 sq ft of floor area (about 199 m2),
down from the 2,466 sq ft peak of 2015; the median sold price in 2025 is $417,400. A two-story
2,100 sq ft home covers roughly a 1,050 sq ft footprint, one to two floors, about 5–10 m tall.
New multifamily units run a median 1,000 sq ft for rent and 1,170 for sale, most in buildings
of three floors or more. Spec lots run under a fifth of an acre; custom owner-held land
medians a full acre.

Sources: Census new-housing highlights; NAHB home-size pages; Census rooms pages.

## Example objects

- Fallingwater, Mill Run, Pennsylvania — Frank Lloyd Wright's 1935–38 weekend house over Bear Run waterfall, three stories in about 2,885 sq ft, building and site as one composition.
- Biltmore House, Asheville, North Carolina — Richard Morris Hunt's 1889–95 French Renaissance chateau for the Vanderbilts, 250 rooms, the largest privately owned house in the US.
- Monticello, Charlottesville, Virginia — Jefferson's Neoclassical plantation home redesigned over forty years (1768–1809), 43 rooms in about 11,000 sq ft under a dome room.

Sources: Library of Congress Fallingwater survey; Biltmore history pages; Monticello house pages.
