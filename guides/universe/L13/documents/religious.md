# Religious buildings — the sacred types

This page is the L13 part doc for religious buildings at
10–100 m: temples, churches, cathedrals, mosques, shrines,
and their yards — the scale where one sacred building with
its court fills the view. Civic neighbors live in
`civic.md`; houses around them in `houses.md`; the village
church as first building in L12 `towns.md:15-16`; the dated
arc (shrines, temples, churches, cathedrals) in
`lifetime.md:39-66`.

From 100 m up, sacred buildings break the street pattern
on purpose: a spire over the roofline, a dome over the
crossing, a minaret beside the hall, a gopuram over the
gate, a courtyard where the grid expects a block. Every
faith solves the same problem — a room for gathering under
a sign visible for miles — in a different silhouette, and
the silhouette dates the town.

## The types

**Churches and cathedrals** — nave halls with steeples:
timber village churches (the white box with a tower that
anchors L12 `towns.md`), stone parish churches, cathedral
cruciforms with crossing towers and spires that stayed a
town's tallest for centuries. Masonry arms limited width,
so builders went up: arcades, vaults, flying buttresses
carrying the thrust outward. Lifetimes in centuries;
cathedrals in continuous use for 800 years with
Victorian-to-modern restorations layered on.

**Mosques** — prayer halls with minarets: hypostyle
courts, domed chambers, courtyard fountains for washing,
one minaret or four marking the skyline. Interiors avoid
images, so geometry and calligraphy carry the ornament;
acoustics carry the voice. Friday crowds spill into
streets — the building's parish is the neighborhood's
rhythm.

**Temples** — houses for gods, not congregations: Hindu
gopuram towers over gate courts, Buddhist stupas and
pagodas stacking stories to the sky, Shinto shrines in
cypress with gravel courts rebuilt on cycle (Ise every 20
years — impermanence as doctrine), village shrines as
single roadside boxes (`lifetime.md:39`).

**The rest** — synagogues centered on the ark and reading
table, Sikh gurdwaras with kitchen halls feeding all
comers, meetinghouses plain by principle. Each reads from
above by its court and its roof, not its size.

## How sacred ground works

A sacred precinct is boundary plus approach: walls, gates,
courts, then the hall — circumambulation paths, washing
places, shoe racks, bell ropes. Sound matters more than
sightlines: bells, calls, drums, and silence each own an
hour. Festivals invert the plan: processions take the gods
to the streets, crowds fill courts, temporary stalls ring
the walls — the year's biggest crowd outside stadiums
(`stadiums.md`). Maintenance is worship: gilding, washing,
re-thatching, re-gilding — buildings kept new for
millennia by never being finished.

## Lifetimes

Timber shrines: decades per rebuild, millennia per site.
Masonry churches: centuries per fabric, restorations each
generation. Concrete mosques and temples: decades old and
designed for centuries. Desecration and reuse layer
meanings: temples into churches, churches into mosques,
all into museums — the plan remembers every tenant.
Abandoned faiths leave the most permanent shells: stone
circles and pyramids outlast their theologies by thousands
of years.

## How it interacts with the others

Houses cluster (`houses.md`): parish, congregation, and
festival crowds live within walking distance; church bells
set the audible parish wider than the walk. Blocks bow
(`blocks.md`): setbacks and height limits defer to spires.
Commerce serves (`commercial.md`): flower, candle, and
food stalls at every gate. Civic shares (`civic.md`):
schools and halls in church rooms, bells as the town
clock. Industry avoids (`industrial.md`): quiet zones and
height caps. Stadiums borrow (`stadiums.md`): procession
routes, parking, and crowd practice. Towns began here
(L12 `towns.md:15`): the church first, then the houses.
The L14 room inside — nave, prayer hall, shrine room —
waits at L14.

## Key numbers

- Village church: one hall, one tower — the town's first
building and longest clock.
- Cathedrals: centuries in use; spires the skyline until
steel.
- Mosques: courts, domes, 1–4 minarets; Friday streets.
- Shrines: boxes to complexes; Ise rebuilt every 20 years.
- Stupas/pagodas: stacked stories to the sky.
- L13 frame: 10–100 m — one sacred building with its
court per view.

Sources: L13 `civic.md` and `lifetime.md`; L12 `towns.md`.

## Example objects

A village church is the parish standard: white box, tower,
bells — first built, longest heard.

A cathedral is the masonry standard: nave, crossing,
spire — centuries in stone.

A Friday mosque is the court standard: hall, dome,
minarets — geometry for ornament.

Ise is the impermanence standard: rebuilt every 20 years —
eternity by renewal.

A roadside shrine is the smallest standard: one box, one
offering — faith at walking scale.
