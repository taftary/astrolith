# Works — warehouses, factories, sheds, chimneys

One of six L13 parts: industrial buildings at 10–100 m.
The bridge from L12 lives in `previous-l12.md`; the dive lives in `entry.md`; the timeline in `lifetime.md`.
This document covers the made: distribution warehouses and fulfillment centers, factory
complexes and power stations, the freight corridors they live on, and the second lives
old works grow into — from the Houston Ship Channel to the Rouge, Bankside, and Battersea [S01] [S12].

## How it looks

A warehouse is a large plain box on the city's edge: white or metal roof, rows of dock doors,
staging aprons for trucks, pallet racks and forklifts inside. Docks stand about 4 ft above
the pavement for trucks — the common working height is 48–52 in, with 55 in the typical
design figure — and about 3 ft 9 in above the rails for railcars, with one electro-hydraulic
leveller for every five truck bays [S09]. A factory is a building or group where machines
make goods, crowned by the chimney that carries smoke above the roof. Classic plants sprawl —
boiler house, turbines, transformers, tank farms, rail sidings — and the great ones become
landmarks: Battersea's four white chimneys on the Thames, dismantled and rebuilt during
restoration, still standing over shops and homes today, with the Lift 109 viewing platform
riding 109 m up one stack [S11]. From orbit the works read as bright clusters on the water:
refineries, wakes, and dredge islands strung along the channel.

## How it changes over time

Warehouses are the newest buildings in America — about half (51 percent) raised since 1990 —
and the most common commercial type: just over a million warehouse and storage buildings
holding 17.4 billion sq ft, the first time since the survey began in 1979 that warehouses top
both the building count and the floorspace ranking [S04]. Most are small — 69 percent under
10,000 sq ft, 44 percent between 1,001 and 5,000 — and fewer than 1 percent pass 500,000,
against an average of 17,400 sq ft per building [S04]. Firms build new rather than retrofit,
chasing placement, power, access, and layout — and since 2023 the flight to quality shows in
the ledger: pre-2000 buildings shed more than 100 million sq ft of absorption in 2024 while
post-2022 buildings gained more than 200 million [S05].

The 2020–2026 cycle bent the whole stock upward. Nearly a billion sq ft of new industrial
supply landed after Q1 2023; vacancy climbed, then fell to 6.5 percent in Q2 2026, the first
decline since Q2 2022, as construction starts dried up and big-box demand tightened the
market [S06]. Market rents reset about 5 percent down in 2024 after a decade of growth, with
recovery expected as first-generation space leases through [S06]. Demand never fell back to
pre-pandemic levels: leasing runs near 800 million sq ft a year, third-party logistics holds
about 35 percent of it, and e-commerce — 16.3 percent of US retail in Q2 2025 at $304
billion, a record 23.2 percent ex-autos-and-gasoline in late 2024 — takes a fifth of new
leasing and needs roughly three times the logistics space per sale that store sales do,
projecting 250–350 million sq ft of further demand over five years [S05] [S07].

The old giants get second lives instead of demolition: Bankside's oil power station went dark
on 31 October 1981 and reopened on 11 May 2000 as Tate Modern, gallery floors threaded
inside Giles Gilbert Scott's brick shell by Herzog and de Meuron [S11]; Battersea closed in
1983 and reopened on 14 October 2022 as shops and homes on a 42-acre neighborhood, its
chimneys rebuilt and its Turbine Hall A staging the chimney-lift exhibition [S11]; Ford's Rouge
grew a 10.4-acre living sedum roof over its truck plant, part of a natural stormwater system
with a Gold LEED visitor center [S12]. A shed that cannot adapt is a shed that falls — and
the adaptation increasingly runs both ways, with dead retail boxes becoming warehouses and
warehouses feeding the stores that survive (see `commercial.md` for the mall afterlives and
`civic.md` for the civic ones).

Sources: EIA warehouse pages [S04]; CBRE industrial outlook [S05]; CBRE Q2 2026 market report and Prologis rent review [S06]; Census e-commerce release and Prologis e-commerce review [S07]; Tate Modern history, Battersea masterplan, and BBC reopening pages [S11]; Henry Ford Rouge pages [S12].

## How it is created

Distribution sheds go up fastest as pre-engineered metal buildings: factory-toleranced frames
with clear spans economical to about 60 m (specialist heavy frames to 90 m), bays every
6–9 m, and eaves stepped to the use — 6–9 m for dry goods, 9–12 m for cold chain,
12–18 m for the e-commerce fulfillment centers with their multi-tier racking and mezzanine
pick floors [S10]. A 100,000 sq ft box rises in seven to ten months against ten to fourteen
conventional [S10]. Tilt-up concrete competes where walls run tall and security matters:
panels cast on site and craned upright, at a premium to the prefab price [S10]. Cold chains
add insulated sandwich panels; fulfillment floors are engineered for mezzanine live loads of
5–7.5 kPa on top of the roof load [S10]. The design rule is growth: size for today's goods
plus tomorrow's, keep the bays high and the berths clear of structure.

Heights keep climbing with the racking. Pre-1980s sheds stood under 20 ft clear; 24–28 ft
became standard in the 1980s–90s with pallet racking and counterbalance forklifts; 28–32 ft
in the 2000s–10s; today's Class-A distribution centers offer 32–36 ft, and large-format
e-commerce buildings reach 40 ft and above for automated storage and retrieval [S08].
Column grids widened with them — roughly 50-by-50 ft to 56 ft and beyond — and flat,
heavily reinforced slabs, high-speed fiber, RFID-ready tracking, added power capacity, and
EV-charging readiness have moved from upgrades to baseline in competitive stock [S08].
Inside, the robots multiply: autonomous mobile robots that pick alongside people, pallet
shuttles, automated forklifts, and robotic packaging under warehouse execution software,
with billions of robot-assisted picks logged across customer fleets by 2026 [S14].

Sources: PEB Steel warehouse pages [S10]; ACI tilt-up guide and WBDG warehouse pages [S09]; Link Logistics clear-height and modern-feature pages [S08]; Prologis automation pages [S14].

## How it interacts with the others

Works live on freight corridors: Houston's ship channel runs 50 miles from the interior to the
Gulf, 530 ft wide and 45 ft deep, carrying petrochemicals out and Midwestern grain in, with
refineries planted by the Texas oilfields along its banks [S01]. The channel is being remade
around them: the $1-billion Project 11, the waterway's eleventh major expansion, widens the
Galveston Bay reach from 530 to 700 ft and deepens upstream sections toward 46.5 ft, with
the first bay segments accepted, the Bayport-to-Barbours-Cut reach completing, daylight
restrictions cut by up to two hours each way, and full completion targeted for 2029 [S02].
Through it all the port keeps breaking records — 4.14 million TEU and 53 million tons across
the public terminals in 2024, then 4.30 million TEU and 54.5 million tons in 2025 — on a
complex of more than 200 private and eight public terminals that remains the nation's
largest port for waterborne tonnage [S03]. Bayport now takes 15,000–17,000-TEU
neo-Panamax vessels, and its Wharf 7 added 1,000 ft of berth and half a million TEU of
capacity at the end of 2025 [S02] [S03].

Ports burn all night — Antwerp's dock facilities outshine its city center, Long Beach glows
orange with round-the-clock sodium cargo light [S13]. The self-made extreme is the Rouge:
a mile and a half by a mile with its own docks on the dredged river, its own rail network,
its own power plant, its own bus network and fifteen miles of paved road, where 5,000 of
some 100,000 peak workers did nothing but keep the place clean, painted, and maintained
[S12]. What the works exhale, the neighborhood breathes. The newest sheds serve the newest
shops: the fulfillment warehouse is the big box's back room, and the `commercial.md` power
center is its front — one frame of trucks between them — with dedicated reverse-logistics
floor for the returns flow, dark stores and micro-fulfillment pushing into the
urban last mile, and free returns expected by three-quarters of shoppers [S05] [S07]. And
civic buildings increasingly arrive second-hand from the same stock: retired power stations
become the galleries of `civic.md`'s campuses.

Sources: NASA ship-channel, night-cities, and Antwerp pages [S01] [S13]; Port Houston Project 11 and tonnage releases, Waterways Journal funding pages [S02] [S03]; Henry Ford Rouge pages [S12]; CBRE outlook and Prologis e-commerce review [S05] [S07].

## Typical size and mass

The average US warehouse covers 17,400 sq ft; most run small (69 percent under 10,000 sq
ft) while the distribution stock steps up through mid-size sheds to big boxes past 100,000
toward 500,000 — fewer than 1 percent pass half a million — and occupiers now generally
demand 200,000 sq ft and up for big-box product [S04] [S05]. Clear spans reach 60 m with
eaves 6–12 m by use (to 18 m for fulfillment); prefab steel runs about 25–45 kg per square
meter of frame [S10]. The Rouge at full stretch held 93 buildings and some 16M sq ft of
floor — about a square mile and a half — with peak employment near 100,000 turning raw
materials into finished cars on one continuous line [S12]. Battersea's stacks are crowned by
the 109-m chimney-lift ride to the top [S11].

Sources: EIA warehouse pages [S04]; CBRE age-of-inventory and outlook pages [S05]; PEB Steel warehouse pages [S10]; MotorCities Rouge pages [S12]; Battersea chimney-lift pages [S11].

## Example objects

- Ford River Rouge Complex, Dearborn — land from 1915, built out through the 1920s, a mile and a half by a mile, 93 buildings, once the world's largest integrated factory, the template for Wolfsburg, Mirafiori, and Ulsan.
- Bankside Power Station to Tate Modern, London — 1947–63 oil station to 2000 Herzog and de Meuron gallery, machinery out and gallery floors in, collection free on the upper levels.
- Battersea Power Station, London — 1929–55 coal giant, a fifth of London's power, gas-washing pioneer, chimneys dismantled and rebuilt, reopened 2022 as the centerpiece of 42 acres.

Sources: MotorCities Rouge pages [S12]; Tate Modern history pages [S11]; Battersea heritage and masterplan pages [S11].
