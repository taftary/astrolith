# Shops — storefronts, malls, commercial cores

One of six L13 parts: shops and commercial buildings at 10–100 m.
The bridge from L12 lives in `previous-l12.md`; the dive lives in `entry.md`; the timeline in `lifetime.md`.
This document covers the trade: high-street shopfronts, big boxes, enclosed malls, and the downtown
commercial cores that anchor them — including the Chicago Loop, the 1.58 sq mi central business
district that forms the second-largest business district in North America after Midtown
Manhattan [S02].

## How it looks

A shop is a glass box for display: aluminum and glass facades, big clear windows, doors with
glass in them, and brand layered on top — fascia sign, perpendicular blade, awning, window
graphics. Three forms share the trade. The high street lines narrow units side by side along
the sidewalk, one to four floors, shop below with offices or homes above, in continuous active
frontage. The big box stands alone: one huge single-floor shoebox on a concrete slab, flat
roof on steel trusses, block walls in metal siding, ringed by parking, built for cars. The
enclosed mall turns the street inside out: stores facing a common indoor walkway one to three
levels deep, parking wrapped around the outside, department anchors at the ends. By night the
downtown core glows white — commercial, industrial, and transport light — against the warm
sodium of the residential streets around it.

## How it changes over time

Shops turn over every few years; a share of leases now track tenant revenue instead of fixed
rent to survive the churn — about 15 percent of UK shopping-centre leases are turnover-based,
concentrated in fashion and food-and-beverage units [S09]. Interiors are stripped and rebuilt on a five-to-ten-year beat:
first the base shell with air, sprinklers, and grid ceilings, then the tenant's own millwork,
lighting, and branding. Malls live and die by their anchors: when the department stores close,
vacancies spread and foot traffic thins — the dead-mall cycle — pushed by online retail, which
reached 16.4 percent of US sales in Q3 2025 on a seasonally adjusted basis [S07]. The afterlife is conversion: dead malls become logistics, housing, and health uses — the Puente Hills Mall site drew a 2025 warehouse-or-data-center proposal after its 2024 sale [S13], a Sears anchor at Oakdale became the Lourdes Pavilion wellness and clinical center [S13], and former Sears boxes elsewhere became senior apartments [S13] — with data centers reusing the shell and power feeds, and clinics, senior housing, and even schools moving into the empty anchors after heavy refit (see `civic.md` for the campuses that receive them and `industrial.md` for the warehouses that restock the survivors). Young shoppers
still buy most general merchandise in stores, for trying on and instant taking home.

Sources: RICS turnover-lease pages [S09]; shell-and-core fit-out pages [S10]; Census e-commerce release [S07]; mall-reuse cases [S13].

## How it is created

The lineage runs fixed-price Paris department store in 1852, to the first unified open mall
outside Kansas City — Country Club Plaza, planned in 1922 and opened in 1923 — to the first fully enclosed air-conditioned mall near Minneapolis
in 1956, to the 1980s megamalls with hotels and water parks inside [S06]. Strip malls and big boxes
typically rise as Type II non-combustible frames — noncombustible columns, floors, partitions,
roofs, and walls under IBC 602.2 — metal skeleton, block walls, sprinklers
buying extra area — while small restaurants and offices go up cheapest in Type V wood, where
structural elements and walls may be any material the code permits [S11]. The
developer delivers structure, envelope, and shared core; the landlord delivers the base shell;
each tenant funds its own fit-out. Planners weigh community support, access, site size,
zoning, and economics before a single footing is dug.

Sources: Britannica shopping-centre pages [S06]; IBC construction-type pages [S11]; shell-and-core fit-out pages [S10].

## How it interacts with the others

The high street is pedestrian-first: streets are over 80 percent of urban public space, and
shared commercial streets, parklets in old parking bays, and active glass fronts turn foot
traffic into the economic metric. The big box and power center are car-first: huge footprints
force highway sites with oceans of surface lot, often beyond transit's reach. The mall runs a
controlled interior street where anchors pay less rent because they drag the crowds past the
small shops, and food courts and cinemas stretch the stay. Above and behind, downtown office
space feeds the whole system its daytime population of workers, diners, and errand-runners —
and post-2020 remote work thinned that crowd: US office vacancies hit a 30-year high of
18.2 percent, near 20 percent nationally by end-2023, cutting foot traffic to the shops,
restaurants, and services around the office [S14].
Dead malls increasingly feed the civic stock instead: empty anchors become clinics,
senior housing, and even schools after heavy refit (see `civic.md` for the
campuses that receive them and `industrial.md` for the warehouses that restock the survivors).

Sources: NACTO street-guide pages [S03]; Michigan Journal anchor-rent pages; office-vacancy and office-adjacent economy pages [S14].

## Typical size and mass

A single high-street shop runs about 12–18 ft wide and 50–80 ft deep; independents hold
1,000–5,000 sq ft. A big box starts past 50,000 sq ft and can approach 200,000. Centers step
up by class [S04] [S05]: neighborhood 30,000–125,000 sq ft, community to 400,000, regional mall
400,000–800,000, super-regional past 800,000 averaging 1,255,000. A power center of
category-dominant big-box anchors runs 250,000–600,000 sq ft with three or more anchors
holding 70–90 percent of space [S05]. The Mall of America totals
5.6 million sq ft with more than 520 stores [S16]; Macy's Herald Square holds 1.25 million sq ft of retail
in its 2.5-million-sq-ft 1902 block, the largest US department store [S15]; Oxford Street packs 300 shops into a mile and a fifth with some 300,000
visitors a day.

Sources: ICSC center-classification pages [S04]; MECSR center-type pages [S05]; Visit London Oxford Street pages [S12]; Mall of America press pages [S16].

## Example objects

- Fifth Avenue and Macy's Herald Square, New York — flagship canyon and the largest US department store, a 2.5M-sq-ft block opened 1902 with 1.25M sq ft of retail [S15].
- Oxford Street, London — Europe's busiest shopping street, some 300 shops over 1.2 miles from Marble Arch to Tottenham Court Road — footfall fell about 20 percent post-2020 before a billion-pound revival wave [S12].
- Ginza, Tokyo — high-end district of old department stores beside designer flagships, the Fifth Avenue of the east.

Sources: Storefront best-streets pages; Visit London pages [S12]; 34th Street Macy's pages [S15].
