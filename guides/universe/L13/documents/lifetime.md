# Lifetime — dated stages for buildings

before houses, permanent homes, ancient masonry, medieval timber, industrial brick and steel,
modern concrete and glass, the present, and the far-future fate. Each stage maps to its part
doc in the reading guide; timescales cross-check at the bottom.

## Shelters before houses — caves, huts, thatch (before ~10000 BCE)

The first buildings are found, not made: caves and rock overhangs, then huts of branches and
thatch — dried grasses and reeds tied in overlapping courses. Walls are branches, timbers,
then brick and stone as the materials allow; roofs run gabled, flat, domed, and vaulted from
the start. Nothing is dated to a year; the techniques persist into every later era as the
vernacular under the monuments. The thatch-to-timber-to-masonry sequence is the deepest
layer of the [houses](./houses.md) lineage: every later frame and roof form repeats these
first choices at larger scale.

Sources: Britannica construction and roof pages.

## First permanent houses (~10000–3000 BCE)

Farming fixes people in place and the house becomes permanent: mudbrick rectangles in the
first towns, rooms added as families grow, entry over the roof and down a ladder in the
earliest dense settlements. The house, the storehouse, and the shrine separate into distinct
buildings — the first civic and commercial parts. Walls divide into the two families that
persist today: load-bearing walls that carry floors and roofs, and nonbearing walls that
merely divide. This is the Neolithic-to-Chalcolithic transition: settled mudbrick towns
replace seasonal huts, and the courtyard house plan that will run through Rome appears.

Sources: Britannica house and architecture-methods pages.

## Ancient masonry — temples, houses, first blocks (~3000 BCE–500 CE)

Stone and fired brick raise the first monuments and the first cities: courtyard houses,
palaces, temples, and Rome's stacked rental insulae — the first apartment blocks, homes
piled floor on floor over shops. Aqueducts, sewers, and paved streets wire the buildings
together into the first serviced city. The civic center appears as a building type: forum,
basilica, baths, and the meeting hall. Buried systems start here and run unbroken to the
present suburb: bored-log pipes, gravity conduits, cast-iron mains (Philadelphia 1804),
citywide sewers (Chicago 1850s) — see [houses](./houses.md) and [civic](./civic.md)
for the full utilities lineage.

Sources: Britannica house and construction pages; HUD systems-history pages.

## Medieval timber and stone (~500–1500)

Timber framing fills the towns — oak skeletons with wattle-and-daub panels — beside stone
churches, guildhalls, and walls. The great halls stretch past 150 ft in stone that survives
fire and bombing; market halls double as government, like Faneuil Hall's 1742 ancestors.
Houses stay one or two rooms for most, with the chimney stack arriving to carry smoke above
the roof. Cathedrals push masonry to its limit while homes stay timber. London's Guildhall
crypt and great hall (over 150 ft) and the later 1869 Manchester Town Hall bookend the
civic lineage detailed in [civic](./civic.md); the Colonial (1600s–1700s) house forms in
[houses](./houses.md) inherit this timber frame directly.

Sources: Britannica architecture and Guildhall pages; NPS Faneuil Hall pages.

## Industrial brick and steel (1760–1914)

Mills and factories multiply on coal, iron, and the rail siding; warehouses adopt the dock,
the leveller, and the pallet. Steel framing frees the wall from carrying weight: Chicago's
1885 Home Insurance Building stacks ten stories on a near-all-metal frame, Cincinnati pours
the 16-story Ingalls in concrete in 1903, and the first all-glass faces appear — Kansas
City's Boley in 1908, San Francisco's Hallidie in 1918. Houses industrialize too: balloon
framing, manufactured ornament, Victorian rows, and the first suburbs on the streetcar.
Biltmore rises 1889–95, the largest private house in America, while tenements stack the
workers. Built for George W. Vanderbilt to Richard Morris Hunt's French Renaissance design
with Frederick Law Olmsted grounds, the 250-room house took six years, about 1,000 workers
and 60 stonemasons, with its own brick kiln, woodworking factory, and a 3-mile rail spur;
opened Christmas Eve 1895, it remains the largest privately owned US house at about
178,926 sq ft. Ford's Rouge land assembly starts in 1915 and builds out through the 1920s
toward 93 buildings and some 16M sq ft — see [industrial](./industrial.md).

Sources: Britannica steel-frame pages; Skyscraper Center criteria pages; Biltmore history pages.

## Modern concrete and glass (1918–2000)

The curtain wall wins: Equitable Portland 1948 seals the first fully air-conditioned tower,
Mies raises the Lake Shore glass slabs 1949–51, the Empire State retrofits 6,514 windows
toward LEED Gold. Commerce invents its own buildings: the fixed-price Paris department
store of 1852 leads to Country Club Plaza (planned 1922, opened 1923) as the first unified
open mall, then Southdale 1956 encloses the first fully air-conditioned mall, and big boxes
colonize the highways. Schools and hospitals accrete into campuses; Boston pours its
concrete City Hall in 1968. Crowds get roofs: the Astrodome — completed 1964, opened
9 April 1965 as the world's first domed air-conditioned stadium, 710 ft outer diameter
with a record 642-ft clear-span lamella truss — domes 70,000 seats, Maracana holds 200,000
standing in 1950, Michigan fills 107,601 from 1927. Suburbs peak in floor area — 2,466 sq ft
median in 2015 — on lots shrinking toward a fifth of an acre. Wembley (old 1923–2000,
rebuilt 2002–07, 90,000 seats under the 315-m arch) and Brasilia (1974 bowl demolished and
rebuilt 2010–13 to 72,788 seats under a 55,155-m2 membrane) close the century — see
[stadiums](./stadiums.md) and [commercial](./commercial.md).

Sources: Skyscraper Center Empire pages; Chicago landmarks pages; StadiumDB and Library of Congress stadium pages; Census and NAHB housing pages.

## Present — you are here (2000–2026)

Houses: of the 1,005,000 single-family homes completed in 2025, the median carried
2,142 sq ft of floor area; of the 678,000 new single-family homes sold that year, the median
price was $417,400 and the average $523,800, at a median sold size of 2,194 sq ft. Sizes keep
falling from the 2015 peak near 2,466 sq ft — the 2024 median of 2,150 sq ft was the lowest
in 15 years. Wood framing holds 94 percent of completions in both 2024 (about 959,000 homes,
up 3 percent on 2023) and 2025 (about 947,000 homes), with concrete at 5 percent and steel
under half a percent, concentrated in the South for resilience. Building took 9.1 months
permit to completion on average in 2024 (7.6 built-for-sale, 15.1 owner-built), quickening in
2025 to 7.4 months built-for-sale and 14.3 owner-built. The stock ages in place: the median
owner-occupied home is 40 years old, about three-fifths built before 1980, and 2020–22 added
only about 2 percent to the stock — see [houses](./houses.md). Lots keep shrinking: 65 percent
of new detached spec homes sold in 2023 sit under 9,000 sq ft, the median sold lot measured
8,543 sq ft in 2025.

Towers are composite steel and concrete, reclad as often as raised; offices convert to
apartments where the plates are narrow. Post-2020 pressure accelerated every fate: US office
vacancy climbed from about 12 percent at end-2019 to 19.6 percent (Moody's, end-2023, a record
since 1979) and 20.9 percent (Cushman and Wakefield, end-2024), with CBRE forecasting a 19.8
percent peak; 1271 Avenue of the Americas reclad from vacant to nearly full, and New York's
City of Yes targets 20,000 homes from conversions — see [blocks](./blocks.md). Malls convert to
data centers, clinics, and senior housing — Puente Hills drew a 2025 warehouse-or-data-center
proposal, a Sears anchor at Oakdale became a wellness pavilion — as online retail reached
16.4 percent of US sales in Q3 2025; see [commercial](./commercial.md).

Power stations convert to galleries and neighborhoods — Bankside to Tate Modern in 2000,
Battersea in 2022. Warehouses are the newest stock — just over a million buildings holding
17.4 billion sq ft, 51 percent raised since 1990 — and the 2020–26 cycle bent them upward:
nearly a billion sq ft of new supply after Q1 2023, vacancy falling to 6.5 percent in
Q2 2026 (first decline since Q2 2022) as starts dried up; see [industrial](./industrial.md).
Stadiums rebuild on decade beats: Houston's Reliant opens 2002 with the first retractable NFL
roof, Wembley's arch since 2007, Brasilia rebuilt 2010–13, the 1965 dome awaiting
its second life since the teams left. Supertalls pass 300 m, 173 and counting.

Sources: Census new-housing highlights (2025: 1,005,000 completed; 484,000 multifamily units; 678,000 sold); NAHB framing pages (2024: 94%, ~959,000; 2025: 94%, ~947,000) and build-time pages (2024: 9.1 mo; 2025: 7.4 mo built-for-sale); EIA warehouse pages; Tate and Battersea pages; CBRE and Cushman office and Q2 2026 industrial reports.

## Far-future fate — growth, climate, abandonment

Three trajectories share the century. Growth: towers add floors, suburbs infill, warehouses
swell past 500,000 sq ft, stadiums rebuild again. The industrial survey points the way:
leasing runs near 800 million sq ft a year with third-party logistics near 35 percent, and
e-commerce needs roughly three times the logistics space per sale that store sales do,
projecting 250–350 million sq ft of further demand over five years. CBRE expects industrial
vacancy to stabilize in the mid-6 percent range as the pipeline rebalances.
Climate: roofs whiten and open, cooling loads rise, flood lines redraw the ground floor, and
the 9-percent energy share of hospitals forces the retrofit first — inpatient blocks are
only about 6 percent of health-care buildings yet burn 68 percent of the sector's
electricity at 193.3 kBtu per sq ft; see [civic](./civic.md). Abandonment: the Astrodome's
proposals are the template — mothball, repurpose, or demolish — and every dead mall and dark
factory rehearses the choice. The buildings that survive will be the adaptable ones: high bays,
clear spans, narrow plates, and stone meant to outlast its builders.

Sources: EIA energy pages; Tate and Battersea reuse pages; NASA Reliant caption pages; CBRE industrial outlook.

## Cross-check of aligned timescales

- Houses: thatch/huts (undated) → mudbrick towns (~10000 BCE) → insulae/courtyards (ancient) → timber framing (medieval) → balloon frame/Victorian (industrial) → Ranch/suburb (1950s–2015 peak 2,466 sq ft) → wood-frame present (2024–25: 94%, 9.1 mo, 2,142 sq ft; 1,005,000 completed and 678,000 sold in 2025).
- Blocks: insulae (ancient) → steel frame 1885 → concrete 1903 → glass curtain 1908–51 → reclad/retrofit/convert (present: vacancy ~20–21% 2023–24, conversions in pipeline).
- Commerce: market hall 1742 → department store 1852 → open mall 1922–23 → enclosed 1956 → big box/power center → dead-mall reuse (present: online 16.4% Q3 2025).
- Civic: forum/basilica (ancient) → guildhall (medieval) → municipal precinct (1869–1968, incl. Manchester Town Hall 1869, Boston City Hall 1968) → campus accretion (present).
- Works: mill/forge (industrial) → Rouge 1915–28 → PEMB/tilt-up (present: 1M+ buildings, 17.4B sq ft) → station-to-gallery reuse 2000/2022; vacancy 6.5% Q2 2026.
- Bowls: Maracana 1950 → Astrodome 1965 (completed 1964, opened 9 April 1965, 642-ft span) → Big House 1927 onward → Wembley 2007 → Brasilia 2013 → next rebuild (future).
- No stage overlaps or gaps: each part doc carries the same dates.
