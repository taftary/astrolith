## How it looks

Light, air, and power read as points and grilles on the shell: ceiling fixtures and hanging
lamps, wall switches, duplex outlets along the baseboards, registers and returns in floor and
wall, radiators under windows. Room lamps use the Edison screw — E26 (26 mm, North American
120 V) and E27 (27 mm, European 230 V), the medium-screw family at seven threads to the inch
(about 3.63 mm pitch), interchangeable in practice although the 1 mm diameter and slight pitch
difference make an E40 cap fit an E39 holder more readily than the reverse; the small-screw
family runs nine threads to the inch. The family spans candelabra E12 (North America) and E11,
intermediate E17 and E14, standard E26/E27, to the big Mogul E39/E40 screws serving 250 W and
up, street and industrial lamps including high-intensity discharge types. For AC supply the
thread shell connects to neutral and the bottom-tip contact to live, and three-way variants
(E26d/E27d) add a ring contact for the second filament. Wall power is the
fixed female receptacle on its branch circuit, the plug its male counterpart with protruding
pins — North America general-purpose is the grounded NEMA 5-15, 15 A class, its sockets backed
by recessed contacts, shutters, covers, and switches. Polarization keeps the wide neutral
blade and the live side distinct so switches break the live conductor and screw shells stay
near neutral potential; earthing connects first and protects Class I equipment, while
double-insulated Class II needs none. Early two-pin wall formats reached Britain near 1885
(the lampholder plug descends from the same socket-as-outlet era), three-pin earthed near
1910, the first national plug standard 1915. Air shows as registers: a register is a
grille plus a damper (a grille alone has none), supply low — floor or low wall, near window or
door where loss is greatest — returns central, high or low, about one per room; registers
vary with room load, velocity kept low enough to hide in background noise yet high enough to
reach temperature, and ducts must seal tight to the boot or air spills and efficiency falls.
Floor registers take foot traffic and
rarely sit within 6 in of a corner. Dampers meter room air, shut off unused rooms, and
rebalance seasonally; fire dampers in larger buildings close automatically against smoke
and heat so ducts do not spread fire. Heat emitters run cast-iron column (steam or hot water,
rare in new build now), fin-tube baseboard (copper pipe with aluminum fins, in at the bottom
and out at the top), welded steel panel (wall-hung, typed by panels and fin sets), plus
aluminum, fan-assisted, underfloor radiant, and skirting-board variants. Strictly, most
"radiators" heat mainly by convection and would logically be convectors; panel types stack
flat or corrugated steel panels and count them with a two-digit code (type 21 is two panels
with one fin set), output regulated by manual or thermostatic valves. The radiator name
reaches back to Denison Olmsted's 1834 stove patent, the room radiator itself to Franz San
Galli's 1855–57 St. Petersburg design, promoted late in the century by the American Radiator
Company over fabricated steel.

Daylight shares the ceiling with the lamp. A skylight (rooflight) is a light-permitting roof
structure in glass or translucent plastic for toplighting: open oculus (Pantheon) historically,
glazed closed units since the Industrial Revolution, ETFE and modern plastics since the
mid-20th century. Types read as fixed unit (frame plus glazing infill, no ventilation),
operable or roof-window (hinged sash), retractable (rolling on tracks), tubular daylighting
device (roof dome plus reflective tube to a diffuser, about 10 in residential to 22 in
commercial, for hallways and dark corners), sloped glazing (multi-panel site assembly), and
pavement or prism lights. Toplighting pairs with sidelighting (windows): it reaches central
floor areas, works all day from sky plus sun, and even overcast delivers three to ten times
the sidelighting efficiency; well-daylit commercial buildings cut lighting energy by up to
about a third, some by up to 80 percent. Modern glass uses sealed insulating units with
low-emissivity coatings, inert-gas fills, and rated visible transmittance, U-factor, and solar
heat-gain coefficient; plastic domes shed water and embers, with acrylic most common and
polycarbonate or copolyester where impact resistance matters. The Dobson hallway below pairs
a ceiling skylight with one hanging electric lamp — the layered condition the room now
assumes as normal.

## How it changes over time

Light climbs a life-and-efficacy ladder. Incandescent runs about 1,000 hours at 10 to 17 lumens
per watt, under 5 percent of its energy leaving as visible light. Fluorescent runs 6,000 to
15,000 hours at 50 to 100 lumens per watt (its mercury making it hazardous waste). The best
commercial LEDs pass 200 lumens per watt, over half the input turned to light, fading gradually
instead of burning out suddenly; 2021 production reached about 210 lm/W, and 2023 EU class A
requires at least 210 lm/W. Household LED retrofit lamps reuse the old bases — Edison screw,
MR16 bi-pin, GU5.3 and GU10 — with driver circuitry (usually switched-mode) rectifying mains
AC to regulated DC inside or beside the lamp; heat leaves by conduction into a heatsink or
helium fill rather than radiation, so enclosed fixtures and insulation need care. White comes
two ways: blue emitter plus yellow phosphor (most retail lamps, CRI near 82, high-CRI past 95)
or mixed red-green-blue chips (wider color range, poorer rendering near 25–65). LEDs strike
instantly with no warm-up, and switching does not shorten life as with fluorescent. The market
grew from about 75.8 billion dollars in 2020 toward 160 billion in 2026. Regulation follows
efficacy: the EU phased general-purpose incandescents 2009–2012 and mains halogen from 2018,
while the United States banned most general-service incandescents effective 1 August 2023
(45 lm/W second tier, specialty lamps exempt). The filament history runs cotton thread (~14 hours, October
1879) to bamboo (~1,200 hours, the decade's standard), tungsten (1904) with Langmuir nitrogen
fill (1913, doubling efficiency) — yet by the 1950s only a tenth of incandescent energy became
light, pushing fluorescents then LEDs. The CFL bridged them: 1930s phosphor tubes near triple
the incandescent, dominant American light by 1951; the residential spiral shelved in the 1970s,
back mid-1980s at $25–35 with bulky fit complaints, mature at three-quarters less energy and
ten times the life. The blue LED (Nakamura, 1994; Nobel 2014 with Akasaki and Amano) made
white LEDs practical via phosphor conversion; the United States L Prize (EISA 2007, Philips
win 2009 after 18 months of testing) and Energy Star labeling then stabilized retail quality
after early chromaticity complaints, with ANSI C78.377 and LM-79 defining color and test
method. Heating ages noisily: steam systems develop water-hammer banging when
condensate pools (often after settlement breaks the pipe fall); hot-water radiators want
bleeding at the bleed screw; blocking a baseboard or register top or bottom kills its
convection loop — the heater simply stops working; heating air without humidifying drops
relative humidity, drying skin and shrinking wood floors.

## How it is created

Power met the room through the lamp socket first. When commercial power arrived in the 1880s
it sold for lighting, and small appliances plugged into light sockets — the Edison screw was
the only standard connector, so toasters, fuses, and adapters all used it; wall two-pin
plug-and-sockets reached Britain near 1885, three-pin earthed near 1910, the first national
plug standard 1915. The supply chain dates precisely: Swan's carbon lamp demonstrated December
1878, re-demonstrated January 1879, shown to some 700 at Newcastle on 3 February 1879 — that
hall the first public building lit by electricity, Swan's own house the first house lit,
Newcastle's Mosley Street the first electrically lit street in 1880. Edison's team tested
successfully 22 October 1879 (thirteen and a half hours), filed the carbon-filament patent 4
November 1879, settled on bamboo past 1,200 hours; the screw itself patented 1881 from an 1880
kerosene-can-lid prototype, E-designations standardized through DIN 1924–25, endorsed by the
IEC 1930–31. Central power starts 4 September 1882 at Pearl Street, New York — six 100 kW
dynamos on a 50-by-100-ft site, 400 lamps to 82 customers rising past 10,000 lamps to 508
customers by 1884, the world's first underground urban network; it burned in 1890 (one dynamo
survives at Greenfield Village), was rebuilt, and ran to decommissioning in 1895 when larger
plants nearby took over; 1:24 working models built in 1929 survive at the Smithsonian, the
Con Edison Learning Center, and the Henry Ford Museum. Holborn Viaduct proved the same model in London
the same year. Central heating matures alongside: Strutt's hot-air furnace 1793 (with
Sylvester's hospital application published 1819), Watt's piped steam with Buchanan and
Tredgold systematizing in 1807–1815, Perkins's high-pressure hot water in the 1830s (about
200 C through small-bore pipe, threaded joints bearing full pressure, boiler separated from
heat source), San Galli's room radiator 1855–57, the American Radiator
Company 1892.

## How it interacts with the others

Light and power obey the shell's spacing and serve the furniture's use. Receptacles land so no
point along a wall line sits more than 6 ft from one — 12 ft apart at most, every wall space 2
ft or wider served, the intent being to kill extension cords (see `shell.md` for the enclosure
rule and legal minimums). Modern duplexes add layers the 1880s socket never had:
tamper-resistant shutters, GFCI protection where water is near, AFCI against arc faults,
surge or USB charging variants, and switched or split outlets tying one half to a wall switch
for the lamp-plus-overhead scene. Air placement fights the cold: supplies near window and door where loss is
greatest, returns near the building's center; a heating register belongs across from its window
with enough throw to reach it, or the cold downdraught pools and circulation fails — and
furniture or covers blocking a baseboard or register, top or bottom, defeat the loop, so both
paths stay open. Electric light decoupled light from the room's air: gas burners each consumed
as much oxygen as several people plus great heat, while incandescents consumed none and gave no
perceptible heat — which is what allows lamp-plus-daylight layering today: skylight or window
for ambient top and side light, electric lamp for task and night, controls dimming or
switching the electric share as daylight varies (daylight harvesting). Room control runs from
stand-alone devices (occupancy sensors, timeclocks, photocells wired to fixed groups) to
networked scenes (relays, sensors, switches or touchscreens, fire and HVAC inputs, one user
interface driving many sources for work, rest, and away states). Automatic logic trims output
by time of day, sunrise and sunset, occupancy, available daylight with dead-band switching to
avoid flutter, and alarm overrides; field studies put occupancy-plus-personal control near a
third saved in open offices. Wired protocols descend from analog 0–10 V through DSI to DALI
(IEC 62386), wireless through ZigBee, Bluetooth Mesh, and Hue-style Bluetooth lamps needing no
bridge. On the wiring side,
polarized plugs and shelled sockets keep switches breaking the live side. Every fixture answers
the layout's zones (see `layout.md`), every outlet the furniture's arrangement (see
`furniture.md`), every vent the storage's walls (see `storage.md`).

Sources: IRC power-distribution pages; register and radiator pages; Savoy Theatre pages; plug-and-socket pages; lighting-control pages for scenes and daylight harvesting.

## Typical size and mass

Lamp bases E26/E27 at 26–27 mm; Pearl Street's whole station 50 by 100 ft for 600 kW DC; the
Savoy's 1881 load near 1,200 Swan lamps on an 89 kW outdoor generator, its proscenium 30 ft
high by 30 wide with a 27-ft stage. Edison's 1879 bamboo lamp reached 1,200 hours against the
modern incandescent's ~1,000 and fluorescent/LED's 6,000 to 25,000-plus. Lamp efficacy frames
the same set: 60–100 W incandescent near 15 lm/W, CFL equivalent near 73 lm/W, retail LED
replacements 70–120 lm/W averaging about 85. One room's served set
— a ceiling fixture, a lamp, four to six receptacles, one supply register and one return —
masses a few dozen kilograms of brass, copper, steel, and glass.

Sources: Edison screw and Pearl Street pages; Savoy Theatre pages; DOE light-bulb history.

## Example objects

- The Savoy Theatre, London, opened 10 October 1881 — the first public building in the world lit entirely by electricity, some 1,200 Swan incandescents on an 89 kW (120 hp) outdoor generator; the auditorium started at 1,292 seats, the proscenium 30 ft high by 30 wide with a 27-ft stage. Carte chose electric light because each gas burner consumed as much oxygen as several people plus great heat, while incandescents consumed none and gave no perceptible heat. The stage stayed gas until 28 December 1881, when Carte broke a glowing bulb on stage to prove its safety, and The Times judged the electric light visually superior to gas.
- Pearl Street Station, New York, 4 September 1882 to 1895 — the First District underground network (burned 1890, rebuilt); 1:24 working models survive at the Smithsonian, the Con Edison Learning Center, and the Henry Ford Museum, one original dynamo at Greenfield Village; the radiator era's marker is 1924's American Radiator Building.
- The Dobson skylight-and-lamp hallway, Los Angeles Union Passenger Terminal — ceiling daylight and hanging electric lamp sharing one third-floor hall, photographed as HABS found it.

Sources: Savoy Theatre and Pearl Street pages; American Radiator Company pages; HABS Union Terminal photo CAL,19-LOSAN,64-B-46.
