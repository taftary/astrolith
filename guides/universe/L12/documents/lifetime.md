# Lifetime — dated stages for cities and landscapes

This page runs the dated timeline through the L12 parts:
from lake and valley shaping and first farms to ancient cities, medieval towns, industrial growth,
modern sprawl, the present, and the far-future fate. Each stage maps to its part doc; timescales
cross-check across docs. Scope is cities and landscapes at L12 (10^3–10^5 m); for how each part
looks today see its part doc, for the L11 entry see the bridge docs.

## Geologic background — lakes and valleys before humans

About 2,580,000 to 11,700 years before present, the Pleistocene (Great Ice Age; Gelasian base
2.58 Ma, IUGS 2009) runs repeated glaciations that carve valleys, scour lake basins, and lay till
and outwash across North America and Europe; end-moraine and kettle lakes are typical, with the
Last Glacial Maximum about 29,000–19,000 years ago and final retreat by 11,700 years ago.
About 11,700 years before present the Holocene begins with glacier retreat; modern floodplains,
valley fills, and lake sedimentation start. Every lake and valley stage starts as carved in the
Pleistocene and stabilized in the Holocene — tens of thousands of years before the first farms.
The Grand Canyon downcutting itself runs 5–6 million years (Colorado River integration through
older palaeocanyons; Karlstrom et al. 2014) on rocks to about 1.84 billion years (Elves Chasm
Gneiss; inner-gorge basement near 2 billion years); Lake Superior is the youngest Great Lake,
forming in stages between 10,000 and 7,500 years ago (NPS Pictured Rocks).

## First farming settlements (~10000–3000 BCE)

About 10000 BCE the earliest Neolithic begins in the Fertile Crescent with glacier retreat and the
Holocene (all Neolithic phases fall inside the last 11,700 years). About 9500 BCE first cultivation
and animal domestication appear in southwest Asia, with Pre-Pottery Neolithic A in the Levant and
early temples such as Gobekli Tepe. From about 7000 BCE farming with settled villages spreads from
Greece across southeastern Europe, carried by Anatolian farming communities by demic diffusion;
the diffusion to Britain and Scandinavia takes at least 4,000 years, not complete until after
3000 BCE (Britain ~4000 BCE, southern Scandinavia ~4200 BCE). Millet and rice follow in the
Huang He and southeast Asia about 3500 BCE (rice paddies by at least 5700 BCE in China); maize
domestication begins from teosinte in the Balsas Valley of Mexico about 9000 years ago (~7000 BCE),
with sedentary maize-bean-squash villages developing over the following millennia.

Sources: Britannica Neolithic and History-of-Europe Neolithic pages [S007]; PNAS Balsas maize studies.

## Ancient cities — Uruk and Rome (~4500 BCE–500 CE)

About 4000–3100 BCE the Uruk Period runs in southern Mesopotamia (Early 4000–3500, Middle
3800–3400, Late 3500–3100 BCE); Uruk forms from merged Ubaid settlements to about 250 ha in Late
Uruk with an estimated 25,000–50,000 residents, conventionally about 40,000 at 3100 BCE with
80,000–90,000 in city plus environs — the first-city anchor, largest urban area in the world at the
time, with proto-cuneiform writing, monumental Eanna temples, and later the 9 km Jemdet Nasr wall.
Peak walled extent reaches about 6 km2 with 50,000–80,000 residents about 2900 BCE. Traditional
founding of Rome runs 753 BCE; kingdom to republic to empire follows. About 100–200 CE imperial
Rome holds conventionally about 1 million (11 aqueducts; a debated lower estimate near 450,000
from Ostia/Pompeii densities), the largest city in the Western world until the 19th century, with
aqueducts, roads, and masonry as the model for later infrastructure. In the 5th century the western
empire falls; Rome shrinks but persists as idea and papal seat; in 1870 Rome becomes capital of
unified Italy.

Sources: Met Museum Uruk essay and Uruk-period chronology; Britannica Rome page [S008]; Hanson 2016
cities database and Cambridge aqueduct-capacity study for the 1-million convention and debate.

## Medieval towns (~500–1500)

About 500–1000, post-Roman contraction leaves fortified burhs, monastic centers, and small walled
towns. About 1000–1300, High Medieval revival brings charters, fairs, the Hanseatic League (over
200 towns at peak; kontors at London-Steelyard, Bruges, Bergen-Bryggen, and Novgorod), and Italian
communes; Paris, London, Venice, and Milan reach tens of thousands on agriculture surplus and
trade. About 1347–1352 the Black Death cuts conventionally 30–50 percent of Europe (up to 50–60
percent in England, France, Italy, and Spain; recent pollen-based work stresses strong regional
variation with lighter impact in much of Central and Eastern Europe and Iberia) with town
abandonment and contraction — the abandonment precedent for the far future; cities on average
regain pre-plague populations within about two centuries. About 1400–1500 recovery and Renaissance
rebuilding follow.

Sources: Britannica Middle Ages, Hanseatic League, and Rome pages; Izdebski et al. 2022
pollen-mortality study; Jedwab et al. 2019 city-recovery data.

## Industrial growth (1760–1914)

About 1760–1840 the Industrial Revolution runs from Britain to continent and US with steam, rail,
and factory towns. From 1801 to 1901 the share of the English population in towns of 2,500-plus
rises from about 34 percent to about 77–78 percent (Law 33.8 percent in 1801; 78.0 percent in 1901);
London grows from just over 1.0–1.1 million in 1801 to about 6.2–6.5 million in 1901 at roughly
20 percent per decade — the classic industrial-urban curve. From 1790 to 1920 the US rises from
about 5.1 percent urban (places of 2,500-plus) to over 50 percent urban for the first time in 1920,
with mass immigration, tenements, and streetcar suburbs; the 1920 Census counts 106.0 million
Americans. From 1870 to 1914 the second wave of steel, electricity, piped water, and sewers
reshapes city layout.

Sources: Britannica Industrial Revolution page; UK 1801–1901 censuses via Centre for Cities
Cities Outlook 1901 and GLA historic population; US Census history and NBER Haines 1790–1920.

## Modern sprawl and farmland change (1945–present)

From 1945 to 1970 US post-war suburbanization explodes the metro footprint while core densities fall:
highways, Levittowns, and white flight. In 2007–2008 the world passes 50 percent urban for the first
time (about 3.3 billion urban of 6.6–6.7 billion total; UNFPA State of World Population 2007 and WUP
2007 Revision); by 2020 the US runs 80.0 percent urban under the 2020 definition (densely settled
core of 2,000 housing units or 5,000 people), down from 80.7 percent in 2010 even as urban population
grows 6.4 percent. US farm count peaks at 6.81 million (1935) then falls toward 1.88 million in 2024
(2.04 million in the 2017 Census of Agriculture) with average size rising to 466 acres; developed land
rises by about 44 million acres from 1982 to 2017 (72.1 to 116.3 million acres, NRI) while urban land
stays near 3 percent of the US total. Exurban fringe converts cropland to housing (11.1 million acres
of cropland developed 1982–2017), paired with night-lights and Landsat sprawl visuals. Lakes and
valleys in this stage gain dammed reservoirs, drained wetlands, channelized rivers, and eutrophication.

Sources: UN WUP 2007 Revision and UNFPA 2007 [S001]; US Census 2020 urban-area release [S003];
USDA ERS farm-count series and Major Uses of Land 2017; USDA NRI 2017 [S014];
NASA Earth Observatory pages [S011].

## Present — you are here (2026)

About 8.2 billion world population in 2024–2025 (UN World Population Prospects 2024), with about
57 percent urban by national definitions and about 45 percent living in cities proper under the
harmonized Degree of Urbanization (UN World Urbanization Prospects 2025: 33 megacities of
10 million-plus in 2025, Jakarta near 42 million the largest, over 12,000 cities of 50,000-plus;
two-thirds of population growth to 2050 projected in cities, one-third in towns). The US counts
about 331 million in 2020 with 80.0 percent urban under the 2020 Census definition (densely
settled core of 2,000 housing units or 5,000 people; urban population up 6.4 percent since 2010
while the urban share eased from 80.7 percent). Climate pressure shows as heat islands with flood
and drought on the same valley and lake system and record-low reservoirs. Use this as the
you-are-here beat: every part doc describes today, and the far-future fate below branches
from here.

Sources: UN World Urbanization Prospects 2025 and World Population Prospects 2024 [S001][S002];
US Census 2020 urban-area release [S003]; NASA Earth and climate pages [S011][S013].

## Far-future fate — growth, climate, abandonment

Growth: UN World Population Prospects 2024 peaks near 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s (about 8.2 billion
in 2024, about 9.7 billion in 2050, about 10.2 billion by 2100; 80 percent chance the peak falls in
this century); UN World Urbanization Prospects 2025 projects about 68 percent urban by 2050 on
national definitions (2018-revision headline retained in 2025 reporting) with over 15,000 cities by
2050 and 37 megacities → densification, megacity coalescence, and rewilded fringe. Small and
medium cities under 1 million (96 percent of the 12,000-plus) grow fastest, especially in Africa and
Asia. Climate: sea level along US coastlines rises 10–12 inches (25–30 cm) by 2050 on the 2022
interagency scenarios (NASA, NOAA, USGS) — as much as the last 100 years combined — forcing managed
retreat from floodplain and coast with reservoir loss and valley reshaping; Kennedy Space Center
dune-and-retreat planning is the worked coastal example. Heat, evaporation (about 1,500 km3 per year
from lakes and rising), and water stress compound the retreat. Abandonment: Detroit falls from
1,849,568 in 1950 to 639,111 in 2020 (about 65 percent loss; 70,000-plus abandoned buildings and
90,000 vacant lots at the blight peak) with first renewed growth only in 2023–2025; rust-belt and
mining towns, Angkor and Rome precedents lead to ruins, forest succession, and lake sediment burying
streets — Rome fell but persisted as idea, the strong closer. The fate of cities is thus growth with
retreat and ruins, consistent across [cities](./cities.md), [towns](./towns.md),
[networks](./networks.md), [farmland](./farmland.md), and [landscapes](./landscapes.md).

Sources: UN WPP 2024 Summary of Results and Our World in Data 2024-revision explainer [S002]; UN DESA WUP
2018/2025 68-percent headline [S001]; NASA–NOAA 2022 sea-level scenarios [S006]; US Census Detroit counts;
NASA climate pages [S013]; USGS pages [S012]; Britannica Rome page [S008].
