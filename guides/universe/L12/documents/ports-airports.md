# Ports and airports — the gates of the city

This page is the L12 part doc for ports and airports at
1–100 km: harbors, runways, terminals, and rail yards —
the scale where one gateway complex with its aprons fills
the view. Roads and rails feeding them live in
`networks.md`; the buildings in L13 (`industrial.md` for
warehouses, the planned transport page for terminals);
ships and aircraft as objects in L13; the dated growth in
`lifetime.md`.

From 10 km up, gateways read as geometry of movement:
dredged channels with turning basins, container yards
gridded in rainbow stacks, runways in parallel pairs with
taxiway ladders, rail yards fanning into dozens of
parallel throats, parking lakes around terminals. Every
gateway is a machine for changing vehicles — ship to
train to truck, air to ground — and the machine's size
sets the city's reach.

## Seaports — where ships become trains

A modern container port is three bands: the dredged
channel and turning basin (depth the limit — neo-Panamax
ships draw ~15 m, so harbors dredge endlessly), the
quay cranes unloading boxes in minutes per move, and the
stacked yard feeding on-dock rail. Long Beach and Los
Angeles — the busiest complex in the Americas
(`networks.md:156`) — read from orbit as rainbow grids
against grey water, with the Alameda rail corridor
stitching them to the continent. Tanker terminals differ:
piers, pipes, and white tank farms set apart for fire;
cruise terminals differ again: single berths with parking
lakes and downtown views. Boston's Logan sits in the
harbor itself (`networks.md:156-158`) — runways reaching
into water, the two gateway kinds sharing one frame.

## Airports — runways, terminals, noise

An airport is runways first: 3–4 km strips aligned with
prevailing winds (Denver's 16,000-ft high-altitude
runway among the longest), parallels for simultaneous
approaches, crosswinds for the rest, with taxiway ladders
and de-icing aprons between. Terminals arc or pier from
the landside — horseshoes, satellites, midfields — with
parking, rental lots, and hotels ringing outside security.
Cargo airports (Memphis, Louisville) glow at 3 a.m. with
sorter hubs; military fields add bunkers and blast pads.
Noise contours decide the neighbors: schools and suburbs
drawn outside the 65-decibel line, warehouses inside it —
the gateway zoning the city around itself. Fog closes them
first (`sky.md`): tule, marine layer, ice.

## Rail yards and inland ports

Where boxes change trains, yards fan out: hump yards
sorting cars by gravity, intermodal pads stacking boxes
two-high on well cars, auto racks loading new cars by the
hundred. Chicago's yards — the continent's throat — sprawl
kilometers of parallel steel; dry ports (Kansas City,
Dallas) replicate seaport yards a thousand kilometers
inland on cheap land. Canal locks lift ships where rivers
climb (`rivers.md` at L11): Panama's new locks, the Soo's
thousand-footers — the inland waterway's beating valves.

## How gateways change over time

Ports chase depth: sail to steam to container (1960s on),
each generation dredging deeper and stacking higher —
gantry cranes growing with the ships, yards automating to
driverless carriers. Airports chase length then noise:
jets needing 3 km, neighbors demanding curfews, hubs
moving outward (Denver 25 miles out, DFW between cities)
while business jets colonize the reliever fields left
behind. Rail yards dieselized, containerized, and
precision-scheduled — fewer throats, longer trains. Every
gateway migrates outward as its city grows around the old
one: the old port becomes waterfront condos, the old
airport a museum field (`lifetime.md` reuse stories).

## How it interacts with the others

Cities face their gates (`cities.md`, `towns.md`): harbor
fronts, airport corridors, warehouse belts along the
truck routes. Roads and rails exist for them
(`networks.md`): interstates ending at quays, belt lines
circling yards, pipelines from tank farms. Farms feed
through them (`farmland.md`): grain elevators at the
quay, reefer boxes of produce. Factories cluster
(`extraction-energy.md`, L13 `industrial.md`): refineries
at tanker berths, gas flares at the field's edge. Coasts
host (`coasts.md`): dredged channels, filled marshes,
beach sand starved by jetties. The sky rules (`sky.md`):
fog closures, crosswind limits, ash grounding fleets
(`volcanoes.md`). Ships and aircraft themselves belong at
L13; the complex is here.

## Key numbers

- Quays: draft ~15 m for neo-Panamax; cranes minutes per
box; yards rainbow-gridded.
- Runways 3–4 km; terminals horseshoe/pier/satellite;
65-dB line zoning neighbors.
- Yards fan dozens of throats; dry ports a thousand km
inland; locks lift thousand-footers.
- Long Beach/LA busiest in the Americas; Logan in the
harbor; Chicago the rail throat.
- Gateways migrate outward; old ones become condos and
museums.
- L12 frame: 1–100 km — one complex with aprons per view.
