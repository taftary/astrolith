# Extraction and energy — the working lands

This page is the L12 part doc for mines, oil fields, solar
and wind farms, and dams with their reservoirs at 1–100 km:
the scale where one pit, field, farm, or dam with its lake
fills the view. Power lines and pipelines feeding out live
in `networks.md`; farm pivots in `farmland.md`; dam walls
as structures in the planned L13 structures page; the
reservoir water in L11 (`rivers.md`, `lakes.md`); the dated
story in `lifetime.md`.

From 10 km up, working lands read as geometry of taking:
terraced pits spiraling down, pumpjacks nodding in grids,
flare stacks burning at night, solar panels gridded blue-
black, turbine rows marching ridgelines, dam walls curving
across canyons with blue lakes behind. Every pattern is an
energy accounts book — sunlight, ancient sunlight, falling
water, split atoms — written on the ground.

## Open pits and quarries

Bingham Canyon outside Salt Lake City is the largest
excavation on Earth: 4 km wide, 1.2 km deep, working since
1906 — terraces spiraling down to ore, trucks like ants on
a spiral road, a 2013 landslide (the largest non-volcanic
slide in North American history) reminding that walls move.
Copper, coal, iron, and gold pits repeat the plan worldwide;
quarries cut dimension stone and aggregate nearer cities —
limestone for cement, gravel for concrete — each a pale
scar with settling ponds. Reclamation follows: terraced
lakes, golf courses, and suburbs on filled highwalls —
the pit's second life in `lifetime.md`.

## Oil and gas fields

The Permian and Bakken read at night as flare constellations
(`networks.md:76-80`): pumpjacks and pads gridded on the
section lines, tank batteries, gathering pipelines, and
flare stacks burning stranded gas — the Black Marble's
rural lights. Fracking multiplies pads into lattices;
refineries cluster at tanker berths (`ports-airports.md`)
with white tank farms and flare trees of their own. Spills
and brine ponds are the shadow pattern; reclamation bonds
the paperwork. The field's life runs decades per well,
years per flare — fast compared to pits, instant compared
to geology.

## Solar and wind farms

Solar reads as blue-black geometry: photovoltaic panels
gridded where sun and flat land meet (`entry.md:33-34`,
`farmland.md:30-31`), tracking rows following the sun,
inverters humming at the block corners — a square kilometer
yielding roughly a large power station's neighborhood share
at noon and nothing at night. Concentrated solar adds
towers with glowing receivers ringed by mirror fields.
Wind reads as dotted lines: turbine rows on ridgelines and
passes — Tehachapi's thousands, Alta's 1,020–1,320 MW
among America's largest — spaced by rotor diameters,
access roads threading between, flicker and setback lines
drawing the neighbor relations. Both convert diffuse flow
to current without fuel — and both sprawl: energy per
square kilometer far below a dam or reactor, which is why
they fill frames at L12.

## Dams and their lakes

The wall belongs at L13; the lake and its works are here.
Three Gorges on the Yangtze — 22,500 MW, 181 m high,
2,335 m long, reservoir 1,084 square km reaching 600 km
upstream — is the world's largest power station; Grand
Coulee (6,800 MW) is America's. Behind every great dam: a
drowned valley (`rivers.md`: Powell's 300 winding km),
silt settling where current dies, turbines humming at the
base, spillways for the flood that must never overtop, and
fish ladders where salmon still run. Downstream the river
starves of mud while the delta sinks (`coasts.md`); the
reservoir breeds recreation, irrigation, and earthquakes of
its own loading. Glen Canyon's 1,320 MW holds back Powell;
the Colorado's whole staircase is one machine across
frames.

Sources: USGS Three Gorges pages; USGS water and dam
pages; in-repo `landscapes.md`, `networks.md`,
`farmland.md`.

## How working lands change over time

Pits deepen until stripping ratios kill them, then flood or
fill; fields boom with prices and bust with geology, pads
drilling out in decades; solar and wind repower on
20–30-year cycles, panels and blades piling as new waste
streams; dams silt over a century and face removal or
raising — the Northwest's dam-breaching debates are the
reservoir's old age arriving. The High Plains aquifer drops
25–100 ft since 1950 under the pumps (see L11 `plains.md`
via `farmland.md`); Powell touched record low 3,517 ft in
2026 (see L11 `rivers.md`). Every working land is a
withdrawal; the statement arrives later.

## How it interacts with the others

Cities drink and burn (`cities.md`, `towns.md`): power,
water, and aggregate flowing in; waste and demand flowing
out. Roads serve (`networks.md`): haul roads, transmission
corridors, gas pipelines with their flare lights. Farms
compete (`farmland.md`): water for panels or crops,
pivots beside turbines, dust on both. Forests fall or
stand (`forests-deserts.md`): clearcuts for pits, panels
on flat sun, ridges shared with turbines. Coasts take the
mud loss (`coasts.md`): starved deltas, drowned marshes.
Lakes are the product (`landscapes.md`, L11 `lakes.md`,
`rivers.md`): Powell, Mead, Three Gorges' 600-km stair.
The sky constrains (`sky.md`): wind for turbines, sun for
panels, drought for reservoirs. Gateways export
(`ports-airports.md`): coal terminals, LNG berths. Power
stations anchor L13 `industrial.md` from below.

## Example objects

Bingham is the pit standard: 4 km of terraces, a century
deep — the largest hole.

The Permian is the field standard: flare constellations,
pads on sections — night lights without cities.

Alta is the wind standard: ridgelines dotted, gigawatts
from air — sprawl for current.

Three Gorges is the dam standard: 22,500 MW, 600 km of
lake — the largest machine.

Powell is the reservoir standard: drowned canyon, bathtub
rings — storage with its low level written in stone.
