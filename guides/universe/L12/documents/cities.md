## How it looks

A city reads as a pale grey and beige built mass against green and brown land. By day the downtown
core is dense and continuous, districts around it looser, arterial roads as pale lines, parks as
dark green voids; by day a large city can even blend into the countryside as a grey smudge,
depending on development style. By night the same mass is a bright blob with street grids visible:
older US neighborhoods irregular with green mercury light, newer western grids rectilinear with
orange sodium light; Japanese cities glow cooler blue-green from mercury vapor, with newer orange
sodium developments along bays like Tokyo Bay. City lights give sharp boundaries for
the densest concentrations of people, and astronaut photography at better than 10 m with long lenses
refines the urban boundaries drawn from satellite data. The systematic night record is NASA Black
Marble: VIIRS day-night band observations from Suomi NPP, NOAA-20, and NOAA-21, composited to
daily, monthly, and yearly maps at up to 500 m, filtering moonlight, clouds, and atmosphere.
Landsat at 30 m separates impervious concrete and rooftops from vegetation; the core, districts,
and corridors are all legible at 10–100 km.

## How it changes over time

Footprints coalesce into ever-larger bright blobs; roads connect into a lace-like web. Repeat night
photos from orbit document the growth. The USGS Annual National Land Cover Database (1985–2024 in
Collection 1.1, with Collection 1.2 adding 2025; Landsat 30 m, six products, 16 Anderson Level II
classes) maps the growth across the US, including year-by-year new development from 1985 to 2024;
Dallas–Fort Worth 1985–2023 is textbook sprawl. The Atlas of Urban Expansion records New York
built-up 688,875 ha (1991) to 951,103 ha (2011) with 51 percent infill, and Tokyo 417,904 ha (1990)
to 643,240 ha (2014). Tokyo grew in area more than 2.6 times faster than in population; farmland
around it shrank while the inner core depopulated. Night growth is no longer a one-way
brightening: a 2014–2022 Black Marble analysis of over a million nightly VIIRS images found global
radiance up 34 percent with strong bidirectional change side by side. US West Coast cities brightened
with population growth while much of the East Coast dimmed on energy-efficient LEDs and economic
restructuring; light surged with urban development in China and northern India, while LEDs and
conservation dimmed France 33 percent, the UK 22 percent, and the Netherlands 21 percent, and
European nights dimmed sharply in the 2022 energy crisis. Industrial booms and busts, construction,
blackouts, and gas flaring over fields like the Permian Basin flicker on top of the gradual trend.

Sources: NASA Earth Observatory city-night pages; USGS Annual NLCD pages; Atlas of Urban Expansion
New York and Tokyo pages; NASA Black Marble 2014–2022 change analysis pages.

## How it is created

Cities grow by rural-to-urban migration plus economy plus infrastructure, built along transport and
arterial corridors. Pre-1990 New York roads averaged 10.83 m wide with arterial density 1.75 km per
km2; 1990–2014 roads narrowed to 8.88 m with density 0.74. Tokyo roads fell from 37 to 25 percent of
built-up. Landsat-based maps separate urban from vegetation to model land-use efficiency, energy,
water, carbon, and forecast growth; the Human Built-up and Settlement Extent map gives the global
look while the World Settlement Footprint tracks urbanization year by year from 1985 to 2015.
Nearly 60 percent of people now live in areas of contiguous urban development, and Landsat's 30 m
visible-plus-infrared record watches that share move. Building height comes from the Copernicus
Urban Atlas building-height raster at 10 m, with the 2021 release covering functional urban areas
over 50,000 inhabitants across EEA38 countries and the UK.

Sources: NASA Landsat urban-development pages; Atlas of Urban Expansion pages; US Census urban-area pages.

## How it interacts with the others

A city interacts with every other L12 part. It pulls daily commuters from towns and suburbs —
Tokyo de jure 14.05 million rises to 16.75 million by day. It anchors the networks: highways, rails,
and corridors end at its ports and airports. It converts farmland at its fringe into housing. It
sits inside landscapes: rivers through its core, lakes beside it, valleys as walls, forests as parks.
It creates a daytime heat island up to 6 C from urban materials and raises downwind rainfall up to
20 percent; its lights fragment habitat. Rapid unplanned growth strains housing, infrastructure,
and services — over 30 percent of urban people in developing regions live in informal settlements —
and concentrates climate risk from heatwaves, floods, and sea level in low-lying districts.
In short the city interacts as the sink that towns feed,
networks serve, farmland yields to, and landscapes frame.

Sources: Tokyo metropolitan population pages; US Census urban-rural pages; NASA Landsat pages;
ESA Sentinel-2 pages.

## Typical size and mass

L12 cities span 1–100 km. Over half of humanity now lives in urban areas — about 6 in 10 by 2030
and 70 percent by 2050 on UN-Habitat projections — and the UN World Urbanization Prospects 2025
revision tracks over 12,000 cities of 50,000-plus with a harmonized Degree of Urbanization method,
estimates for 1950–2025, and projections to 2050. The US Census urban area (contiguous built-up,
not metro) is the measure under the 2020 criteria (densely settled core of 2,000 housing units
or 5,000 people): New York–Jersey City–Newark 19,426,449 people on 3,248.12 sq mi
(8,413 km2, about 100 km across); Los Angeles–Long Beach–Anaheim 12,237,376 on 1,636.83 sq mi
(4,239 km2, about 73 km across, the densest large US urban area at 7,476 per sq mi). Tokyo metropolis
14.27 million on 2,200 km2 with 23 special wards near 15,853 per km2. Between 2010 and 2020 US urban
land area fell 2.4 percent while urban population rose 6.4 percent, so density rose 9.0 percent.
Mass is not catalogued; built area times impervious fraction (USGS NLCD) with building height
(Copernicus Urban Atlas) is the proxy.

Sources: UN-Habitat human-settlements pages; UN World Urbanization Prospects 2025; US Census 2020
urban-area facts; Tokyo metropolitan pages; USGS NLCD pages.

## Example objects

- Example: New York–Jersey City–Newark urban area, 8,413 km2 and 19,426,449 people — archetype
  pre-war core (Manhattan) with boroughs, districts, and sprawling suburbs; the largest and
  fourth-densest US urban landmass at 5,981 per sq mi.
- Example: Los Angeles–Long Beach–Anaheim urban area, 4,239 km2 and 12,237,376 people — archetype
  dense sprawl with compass grid, freeway districts, and a downtown core lower than Manhattan;
  the densest large US urban area at 7,476 per sq mi.
- Example: Tokyo metro, 36–38 million depending on definition — the world's largest; 23-ward core
  (Shinjuku, Shibuya, Marunouchi) with Tama, Yokohama, Kawasaki, Saitama, and Chiba satellites.
  (Metropolis definition 14.27 million on 2,200 km2; wider metro definitions reach 36–38 million.)

Sources: US Census pages; Tokyo metropolitan pages; Demographia urban-areas pages.
