# Networks — roads, rails, infrastructure corridors

One of five L12 parts: transport and infrastructure networks at 1–100 km.
The bridge from L11 lives in `previous-l11.md`; the dive lives in `entry.md`; the timeline in `lifetime.md`.
For the built mass the links serve see [cities](./cities.md); for the spread they feed see
[towns](./towns.md); for the patchwork they cross see [farmland](./farmland.md);
for the frame they bridge see [landscapes](./landscapes.md).

## How it looks

Roads read as thin grey and tan lines, high contrast by day: grids in cities, long curves across
countryside, interchanges as knots and cloverleafs [S001][S002]. Arterials run pale against the
built mass; collectors feed subdivisions with curved roads and culs-de-sac; rural highways run
straight on survey lines with wide shoulders. At night the same roads are lit ribbons: older US
neighborhoods irregular with green mercury light, newer western grids rectilinear with orange
sodium light, Japanese cities cooler blue-green from mercury vapor with newer orange sodium
developments along bays like Tokyo Bay [S003]. City lights give sharp boundaries for the densest
concentrations of people, and astronaut photography at better than 10 m with long lenses refines
the urban boundaries drawn from satellite data [S003]. Border pairs read the culture of the grid
side by side: Ciudad Juarez dense against El Paso, where Interstate Highway I-10 cuts across
the city as a bright line [S003].

Rails read thinner and darker than roads, with straighter curves and gentler grades; railyards
read as fans and combs with sidings and spurs [S002]. Corridors for power and pipelines read as
straight clearings often parallel to roads and rails, best in vegetation bands where the cut
contrasts with forest: high-voltage transmission lines on tall metal towers carry bulk power over
long distances, step-down transformers at substations feed lower-voltage distribution lines to
homes and businesses [S004]. The natural-gas pipeline network underneath runs about 3 million
miles of mainline and other pipelines, with roughly 217,000 miles of interstate mainline
transmission plus about 89,000 miles intrastate, gathering raw gas from wellheads to processing
plants and then to storage and local distribution companies [S005].

The systematic night record is NASA Black Marble: VIIRS day-night band observations from
Suomi NPP, NOAA-20, and NOAA-21, composited to daily, monthly, and yearly maps at up to 500 m,
filtering moonlight, clouds, and atmosphere [S006][S007]. The VIIRS day-night band senses green
to near-infrared with a low-light sensitivity that resolves sources down to about a toll booth
on a dark highway, a step past the older Defense Meteorological Satellite Program record [S007].
Sentinel-2 at 10 m resolves highways and wide arterials, not lane markings; the 290 km swath
with 5-day revisit tracks change, and the 13 spectral bands separate pavement, vegetation, and
water [S008]. Landsat at 30 m separates impervious concrete and rooftops from vegetation; the
core, districts, and corridors are all legible at 10–100 km.

The Mexicali target visual pins the daytime readout to a real place. Astronaut photograph
ISS071-E-131092, taken 25 May 2024 from the International Space Station with a Nikon Z9 at
400 mm on Expedition 71, looks over Mexicali in Baja California where the city grid meets
United States farm grids along the border in the Sonoran Desert [S009]. City grids meet farm
grids with canals as thin lines beside roads: sunlight glints off Laguna Mexico,
water-treatment ponds, and canals including the All-American Canal, which carry irrigation
water to both sides of the border [S009]. North of the border the grid is repetitive with
alfalfa, wheat, and sugarcane, plus a solar farm recognizable as small grey rectangles in
parallel rows [S009].

## How it changes over time

Networks change slowly by growth — new bypasses, widenings, interchanges — and resurfacing
changes brightness [S001]. The Highway Statistics series tracks the slow half year by year back
to 1945: public-road mileage, lane-miles, and vehicle-miles of travel by functional system, with
the 2023 edition (tables HM-10 through HM-81, VM-1 through VM-4) as the current baseline [S010].
They change fast by use — jams, construction closures, floods and snow cover. Rails change by
abandonment versus siding addition; corridors change by vegetation regrowth after clearing.
Night lights dim and brighten with use, and the 2014–2022 Black Marble analysis of over a
million nightly VIIRS images found global radiance up 34 percent with strong bidirectional
change side by side [S006][S007].

The volatility has a geography. US West Coast cities brightened with population growth while
much of the East Coast dimmed on energy-efficient LEDs and economic restructuring; light surged
with urban development in China and northern India, while LEDs and conservation dimmed France
33 percent, the UK 22 percent, and the Netherlands 21 percent, and European nights dimmed
sharply in the 2022 energy crisis [S006][S007]. Industrial booms and busts, construction,
blackouts, and gas flaring over fields like the Permian Basin and the Bakken Formation flicker
on top of the gradual trend: flaring at oil wellheads burns excess methane to carbon dioxide
and soot, and the night record catches its cycles as domestic oil and gas production ran at
record levels 2014–2022 [S007]. Tracking such dips and flares night after night is like
watching the heartbeat of the planet [S007].

Preservation is the third clock. Interstate maintenance responsibility rests with the states;
as the system aged, a federally funded resurfacing, restoration, and rehabilitation program
followed (the 3R program from 1976, expanded to 4R with reconstruction in 1981, continued as
the Interstate Maintenance Program), so much of the visible change on old mileage is
resurfacing and rehabilitation rather than new alignment [S001]. The space segment keeps
refreshing too: Sentinel-2C launched 5 September 2024 into the same constellation, holding
the 10 m, 290 km, 5-day watch [S008]. For the L12 reader the rule is simple: new ribbons
mean growth, brighter pavement means resurfacing, flickering lights mean use and energy
change, and regrown clearings mean the corridor is resting.

Sources: FHWA Highway Statistics 2023 [S010]; NASA Black Marble 2014–2022 change analysis
(Li et al. 2026) [S006][S007]; FHWA Route Log [S001]; USGS transportation content [S002].

## How it is created

Roads are planned, graded, and paved to Interstate standards: minimum four 12-foot lanes,
10-foot shoulders, full access control, 50–70 mph design speed, built for a 20-year design
traffic horizon [S001]. The standards were developed by the state transportation departments
through AASHTO and adopted by FHWA under Section 109(b), Title 23 [S001]. Funding made the
standard real: token authorizations in 1952–1954, then the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956
with the Highway Trust Fund paying the 90 percent federal share on a pay-as-you-go basis from
gas and motor-vehicle user taxes [S001]. Designated mileage grew from 41,000 miles in 1956 to
42,500 in 1968 plus Howard-Cramer additions, with 42,795 miles designated for Interstate
Construction funds before the 1978 cutoff; later logical additions under Section 103(c)(4)(A)
and future corridors under ISTEA Section 1105 brought the January 2025 system to 49,699.35
miles serving the 48 contiguous states, Alaska, Hawaii, the District of Columbia, and
Puerto Rico [S001]. (Alaska and Puerto Rico are exempt from the Section 109(b) geometric
standards and build to local traffic needs [S001].)

Numbering is a second design layer. AASHTO's 1957 procedure with Bureau of Public Roads
concurrence gives one- and two-digit shields to major routes — odd north-south lowest in the
west (I-5 on the West Coast), even east-west lowest in the south (I-10 along the southern
border) — with suffixed branches like I-35E/I-35W and three-digit beltways (even prefix) and
radial spurs (odd prefix) that stay unique only within a state [S001]. Interchanges number by
consecutive or milepost method from the most westerly or southerly point, so the exit number
reads as distance on milepost routes [S001]. Corridors are co-located for permitting —
highway, rail, power, and pipeline sharing valleys and passes; I-90 follows the Erie Canal
and lake shores across New York, with passes like Snoqualmie and Homestake (6,329 ft, highest
on I-90). Today 62 main one- and two-digit routes and 261 auxiliary designations (151 unique
three-digit numbers, reused across states) connect 46 of the 50 state capitals [S001].

Rails are graded bed with ballast, ties, and steel rails at 1.435 m standard gauge in the US,
laid to gentler grades and broader curves than highways, which is why they read straighter
from orbit [S002]. USGS models rail as two interacting layers — railroad main lines plus
siding and spur tracks — alongside the road layers [S002].

Power corridors are built as a hierarchy, not a single line. Power plants feed high-voltage
transmission lines on tall towers for efficient long-distance delivery; substations step
voltage up for the journey and down for distribution lines that deliver safer low-voltage
power to homes and businesses [S004]. What began as 4,000-plus isolated early-1900s utilities
interconnected after World War II into three Lower-48 interconnections — Eastern, Western,
and ERCOT over most of Texas, with the Eastern and Western systems also tied to Canada — run
day to day by balancing authorities that match supply to demand or risk blackout, under
mandatory reliability standards developed by NERC and approved by FERC [S004]. Gas corridors
follow the same gathering-to-delivery logic: small low-pressure gathering lines from
wellheads, processing plants stripping liquids and water, wide high-pressure interstate and
intrastate transmission with compressor stations, then local distribution companies on
small-diameter low-pressure service lines [S005]. About half the mainline gas transmission
network went in during the 1950s–1960s postwar demand doubling; shale and tight-formation
production since has added links toward new supply, notably in the Northeast [S005].

Sources: FHWA Route Log and Finder List (status January 2025) [S001]; USGS National Map
transportation content [S002].

## How it interacts with the others

Networks interact as the links that [cities](./cities.md), [towns](./towns.md),
[farmland](./farmland.md), and [landscapes](./landscapes.md) share. They fragment habitats,
fields, and cities; they bridge rivers — I-90 over the Columbia at Vantage and over the
Missouri — tunnel under hills and cross lakes on causeways. They create runoff, heat, and
impervious surface with wildlife barrier effects: city materials raise daytime heat-island
temperatures and downwind rainfall (see [cities](./cities.md#how-it-interacts-with-the-others)),
and paved surfaces shed soil and water to ditches and streams while center pivots draw
groundwater beneath (see [farmland](./farmland.md#how-it-interacts-with-the-others)).
They feed suburbs, ports, and airports: I-90 ends at the Seattle port and Boston Logan, and
orange sodium light marks round-the-clock port operations like Long Beach [S003]. Long Beach
is one of the world's busiest commercial cargo ports, and its light reads the freight half
of the corridor story [S003]. Commuters close the loop daily (see
[cities](./cities.md#how-it-interacts-with-the-others)), and job sprawl keeps pushing work
beyond 5 miles from the center onto the suburban periphery
(see [towns](./towns.md#how-it-interacts-with-the-others)). USGS models the layers as separate interacting
features: controlled-access highway, secondary highway and major connecting road, local and
local connecting road, ramp, tunnel, railroad, siding and spur, ferry and water-trail routes,
and terrestrial trail [S002]. The smart-grid layer adds a digital loop on top: meters, line
sensors, and substation controls that report outages, manage voltage, and price peak demand,
letting utilities detect and fix problems faster [S004].

Sources: FHWA Route Log [S001]; USGS transportation content [S002]; NASA cities-at-night
feature [S003]; EIA electricity delivery [S004].

## Typical size and mass

Interstate paved width runs 24–50 m with right-of-way 50–100 m; urban streets about 18 m;
rail beds 5–10 m with corridors 15–30 m; power corridors 20–50 m. Length dominates: US public
roads exceed 4 million miles, tracked by functional system in Highway Statistics tables HM-10
through HM-81 with lane-miles and vehicle-miles of travel alongside [S010]; the Interstate
runs 49,699.35 miles as of the January 2025 status, including Alaska, Hawaii, and
Puerto Rico, of which 42,793 miles were developed with Interstate Construction funds and
42,787 of those (over 99.9 percent) stood open to traffic [S001]. Mass is negligible beside
geology; L12 states length and width, not mass. I-90 runs 3,020.44 miles FHWA official
(Seattle to Boston, 13 states, longest Interstate), ahead of I-80 at 2,899.59 and I-40 at
2,556.61; the shortest two-digit routes run under 25 miles (I-97 at 17.62, I-11 at 22.6,
I-14 at 25.1), and the shortest segment anywhere is 0.11 mile of I-95 in the District of
Columbia [S001]. Street blocks run 0.1–1 km — Manhattan 80 by 274 m, Chicago 100 by 200 m,
Portland 61 m, Salt Lake 200 m. For scale beside the energy corridors: the gas transmission
network alone spans about 3 million miles all-in and delivered about 29.2 trillion cubic feet
to about 78.3 million consumers in 2022 [S005].

Sources: FHWA Route Log [S001]; FHWA Highway Statistics 2023 [S010]; EIA natural-gas
pipelines [S005]; encyclopedia city-block pages.

## Example objects

- Example: I-90, 3,020.44 miles Seattle to Boston — the longest Interstate, crossing 13 states;
  busiest at Chicago (306,000 vehicles per day), quietest in rural Wyoming [S001].
- Example: Manhattan blocks, 80 by 274 m — archetype dense grid; Chicago 100 by 200 m, 16 per
  mile (see [cities](./cities.md#how-it-is-created) for pre- versus post-1990 New York road
  widths and arterial density).
- Example: Erie Canal with Thruway and rail in parallel — archetype shared corridor across
  New York, the permitting logic of valleys and passes in one view [S001].
- Example: Permian Basin and Bakken flares — archetype energy-use flicker on the night record,
  gas flaring cycling with record 2014–2022 production [S007].
